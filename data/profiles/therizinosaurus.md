## Overview

Therizinosaurus cheloniformis was a giant therizinosaurid from the Nemegt Formation, known mainly from enormous forelimbs and claws plus limited hindlimb material.

Therizinosaurus cheloniformis is known primarily from enormous claws — the longest manual claws of any known animal, reaching nearly 1 metre along their outer curve. Found in the Late Cretaceous Nemegt Formation of Mongolia (~70 Ma), the animal was initially described as a giant turtle-like creature before additional material from relatives (therizinosaurs from China) revealed its true nature as a large, bizarre herbivorous theropod.

Therizinosaurs represent one of the most dramatic evolutionary reversals in dinosaur history: derived from carnivorous coelurosaur ancestors, they evolved broad hips (convergent with ornithischians), leaf-shaped teeth and long necks suited for browsing vegetation. Therizinosaurus itself may have reached 9–10 metres, making it the largest known therizinosaur.

## Anatomy & life

Therizinosaurus was one of the largest members of Therizinosauria, an unusual group of theropods that turned away from meat-eating. Its forelimbs were enormous: the complete arm of one referred specimen is about 2.4 metres long, and the hand claws reach around half a metre along their curve, the longest known from any land animal.

Almost everything else about its body is inferred. No skull, neck or trunk has been found, so the small head, long neck, wide belly and short tail seen in reconstructions come from better-known relatives such as Erlikosaurus, Segnosaurus and Nothronychus rather than from Therizinosaurus itself. Published lengths of nine to ten metres and masses of several tonnes are extrapolations from the limb bones.

The function of the claws is still debated. Biomechanical modelling by Lautenschlager in 2014 found that the long, slender unguals of Therizinosaurus were poorly suited to digging or forceful strikes and were better matched to hooking and pulling vegetation, although display, defence and intraspecific combat cannot be excluded.

- Hand claws up to roughly 50 centimetres long on arms about 2.4 metres in length are directly measured from fossils.
- Its herbivorous, wide-bodied build is inferred from the anatomy of smaller, better-known therizinosaurids.
- Modelling suggests the slender claws were suited to hooking and pulling branches rather than to fighting or digging.

**Feeding: Probable plant-eater.** No skull or teeth are known. Herbivory rests on the beaked, small-toothed skulls and wide guts of other therizinosaurids.

**Movement: Bipedal.** The stout hindlimb bones and four weight-bearing toes are consistent with a slow, upright biped, as in other therizinosaurids.

**Appearance: Largely reconstructed.** The arms are well known; the skull shape, neck length, plumage and colour are not preserved for this genus.

## Fossil record

Therizinosaurus is known from a handful of Nemegt Formation specimens dominated by forelimb bones and enormous hand claws. Limited hindlimb material is referred to it; the skull, neck, trunk and tail have never been found.

The holotype consists of giant hand claws with a metacarpal fragment and ribs. A referred specimen preserves a nearly complete forelimb, including the shoulder girdle, and a further specimen preserves part of a hindlimb and foot.

Because the material comes from separate finds with no overlapping skeleton, the referral of the hindlimb rests on size and Nemegt provenance rather than on direct association with the claws.

Giant claws and related therizinosaur skeletons support its unusual herbivorous/omnivorous theropod body plan.

- No complete skull or skeleton is known.
- Herbivory is inferred from close relatives; the five-tonne catalogue mass is too precise for the fragmentary material and is removed.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous Mongolian floodplain ecosystems.

All secure material is Maastrichtian Nemegt Formation.

## Classification

Therizinosaurus is the name-giving member of Therizinosauridae, a group of coelurosaurian theropods that evolved a plant-eating body plan. Its position within the family is stable, but its fragmentary remains limit how many characters it contributes to phylogenetic analyses.

## Open questions

### What did its head look like?

No cranial material is known. The small, beaked head shown in reconstructions is borrowed from Erlikosaurus and other relatives.

### What were the claws for?

Biomechanical modelling favours hooking and pulling vegetation, but display, defence and other uses remain possible and are not directly testable from the fossils.

### How big was it really?

Length and mass estimates scale the arms and hindlimb against smaller therizinosaurids; the catalogue carries no mass figure because none can be measured directly.
