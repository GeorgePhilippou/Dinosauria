## Overview

Torvosaurus ('savage lizard') was one of the largest carnivorous dinosaurs of the Jurassic Period, reaching approximately 10 metres in length and belonging to the family Megalosauridae. It lived during the Late Jurassic, approximately 165–148 million years ago, with remains found in the Morrison Formation of North America and the Lourinhã Formation of Portugal, with possible records from Africa and South America. It was a powerful bipedal predator with large, robust arms — markedly different from the reduced forelimbs of later large theropods.

Two species are formally recognised: the type species T. tanneri from the USA, named by Galton and Jensen in 1979, and T. gurneyi from Portugal, named in 2014. Torvosaurus is a crucial taxon in understanding the global distribution and diversity of megalosaurid theropods during the Jurassic, and its close relationship to Megalosaurus — the first formally named dinosaur — makes it important for tracing the evolution of large carnivorous dinosaurs.

## Anatomy & life

Torvosaurus was among the apex predators of Late Jurassic ecosystems, sharing its environment with Allosaurus and Ceratosaurus in the Morrison Formation. Unlike many large theropods, Torvosaurus retained well-developed forelimbs with large, curved claws, likely used in prey capture. Estimates suggest total body length around 9–11 metres, placing it as one of the largest Jurassic carnivores alongside Saurophaganax.

The presence of closely related or identical Torvosaurus species on both sides of the Atlantic — in North America and Portugal — reflects the palaeobiogeographic connections that existed between Laurasia landmasses during the Late Jurassic when sea levels were lower. This intercontinental distribution is also shared by several Morrison-Lourinhã sauropod genera, suggesting periodic faunal exchanges across the proto-Atlantic.

Exceptional embryonic material from the Lourinhã Formation attributed to Torvosaurus gurneyi includes some of the oldest dinosaur embryos known, dating to approximately 152 million years ago. These specimens provide rare insight into the early development of large theropod dinosaurs and suggest relatively long incubation periods.

## Fossil record

Torvosaurus tanneri was described in 1979 by Peter Galton and James Jensen from material recovered at the Dry Mesa Quarry in Colorado. The species name honours Brigham Young University paleontologist Nathan E. Tanner. Additional specimens were later recognised in Portugal and named T. gurneyi in 2014 by Hendrickx and Mateus, honouring illustrator James Gurney of Dinotopia fame.
