## Overview

Dicraeosaurus (meaning 'forked lizard') was a medium-to-large sauropod dinosaur that lived during the Late Jurassic period, approximately 161–143 million years ago, in what is now Tanzania and possibly Zimbabwe. Estimated at up to 20 metres in length, it was a member of Dicraeosauridae, a family of diplodocoid sauropods characterised by bifurcated (forked) neural spines that formed a prominent ridge along the neck and back. Its fossils come primarily from the world-famous Tendaguru Formation.

Dicraeosaurus was described by Werner Janensch in 1914 following large-scale German excavations at Tendaguru in the early twentieth century. As one of the best-known dicraeosaurids, it provides important evidence for understanding the diversity of diplodocoid sauropods on Gondwana. The forked neural spines may have supported a fleshy dorsal crest or served as muscle attachment sites, though the exact function remains debated.

## Anatomy & life

The genus name references the distinctive bifurcated neural spines crowning the vertebrae of the neck and trunk, which gave Dicraeosaurus a sail-like or ridged profile. Similar structures appear in the closely related Amargasaurus from South America. The spines may have functioned in display, thermoregulation, or as anchor points for enlarged neck muscles.

## Fossil record

Dicraeosaurus was named by Werner Janensch in 1914 from specimens collected during the Tendaguru expeditions (1909–1913), one of the most ambitious fossil-collecting efforts of the early twentieth century. The type species, D. hansemanni, honours Consul-General Hans Meyer and reflects the diplomatic context of colonial-era German palaeontology in East Africa.

Multiple partial skeletons with skull, vertebral and appendicular material.

## Where it lived

Dicraeosaurus shared the Tendaguru landscape with Brachiosaurus, Giraffatitan, and several stegosaurs, constituting a diverse Late Jurassic fauna comparable to the Morrison Formation of North America. This parallel diversity has informed biogeographical hypotheses about faunal exchange between northern and southern landmasses during the Jurassic.

Principal fossil site; extensive German expeditions between 1909 and 1913 recovered multiple partial skeletons.

## Classification

Dicraeosauridae is a well-supported family within Diplodocoidea, sharing with diplodocids a peglike dentition, elongated neck, and whip-like tail. Dicraeosaurids are generally shorter-necked and smaller-bodied than classic diplodocids, suggesting a different niche, possibly browsing at lower vegetation heights.

## Open questions

- Association of disarticulated bones to individuals is incomplete.
- Neck posture and precise adult mass are model-dependent.
