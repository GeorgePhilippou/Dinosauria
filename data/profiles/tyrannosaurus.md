## Overview

Tyrannosaurus rex was a giant tyrannosaurid theropod from the final two million years of the Cretaceous. It lived in western North America and combined a massive skull, bone-crushing bite, forward-facing vision and a strong sense of smell.

The fossils tell a more nuanced story than a simple movie monster: T. rex was both predator and scavenger, fought with other tyrannosaurs, grew through dramatic body changes and lived in warm, humid floodplain ecosystems.

## Anatomy & life

Tyrannosaurus rex was a massive two-legged predator that lived across western North America in the last two million years of the Cretaceous. Adults reached about twelve metres in length and weighed around eight to nine tonnes, with a skull up to one and a half metres long carrying thick, banana-shaped teeth built for crushing bone.

Its tiny two-fingered arms contrast with an enormously deep skull, a heavily muscled neck and long, powerful hindlimbs. Bite marks on prey bones, healed injuries, tooth-marked Triceratops pelves and coprolites full of crushed bone show that it fed on the largest animals in its environment.

Because so many individuals are known, Tyrannosaurus is the reference point for how a giant dinosaur grew, moved and fed. That sample also creates its biggest controversy: whether the small tyrannosaurs found alongside it are juveniles or a separate genus, Nanotyrannus.

- A skull up to 1.5 m long with thickened teeth and one of the strongest bites measured for any land animal.
- Reduced two-fingered forelimbs paired with long, robust hindlimbs and a deep, muscular tail.
- Patches of small scales are preserved from the neck, hip and tail of adult specimens; feathering is not directly documented in adults.

**Feeding: Apex carnivore.** Bite-marked bones of Triceratops and Edmontosaurus, bone-rich coprolites and tooth wear document feeding on large prey.

**Movement: Bipedal walker.** Limb proportions and biomechanical models suggest a brisk walk in adults; a true gallop is unlikely at adult mass.

**Appearance: Scales confirmed in patches.** Skin impressions from several adults show small scales; whether juveniles or other body regions carried feathers is not directly known.

## Fossil record

Tyrannosaurus rex is known from more than fifty specimens across Montana, the Dakotas, Wyoming and the Canadian prairies, including some of the most complete large theropod skeletons ever found. Its evidence base is exceptional; the debate is about the edges of that sample.

Complete and near-complete skulls, full vertebral series, forelimbs with both fingers, pelves, hindlimbs and feet, furculae and gastralia, plus small patches of scaly skin from the neck, pelvis and tail of several adults.

The main gap is at the young end of the growth series: if the Nanotyrannus specimens are a separate genus, very few true juvenile T. rex are known.

Skulls, teeth, limb bones, bite marks and bone-filled coprolites support feeding, growth and sensory interpretations.

## Where it lived

Latest Cretaceous western North American floodplains.

## Classification

Tyrannosaurus is the name-bearing genus of Tyrannosauridae and of the subfamily Tyrannosaurinae. Its closest relative is usually recovered as the Asian Tarbosaurus. Proposals to split the sample into several species, and the status of Nanotyrannus, remain unsettled.

## Open questions

### Is Nanotyrannus a separate genus?

Two 2025 studies concluded that the small tyrannosaurs of Hell Creek were mature animals of a distinct genus. If accepted, several specimens leave the T. rex growth series and the juvenile stage becomes less well known.

### How fast could it move?

Estimates range from a fast walk to a slow run. Bone strength and muscle-mass models limit adults to roughly 20–30 km/h, while juveniles were probably much quicker.

### One species or several?

A 2022 proposal to divide Tyrannosaurus into three species was rejected by most subsequent analyses, but variation across the sample is real and still under study.
