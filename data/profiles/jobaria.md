## Overview

Jobaria (named after Jobar, a mythical animal from Tuareg folklore) was a large herbivorous sauropod from the Late Jurassic (or possibly earliest Cretaceous) of Niger, living approximately 161–143 million years ago. Known from a skull and multiple skeletons collected from the Tiourarén Formation of the Agadez region, it reached an estimated 21 metres in length. It was named by Paul Sereno and colleagues in 1999 following major expeditions to the Sahara.

Jobaria is placed within Macronaria, but it sits in an uncertain position — some analyses have placed it as a basal macronarian or even a non-neosauropod eusauropod. Its skeleton is relatively complete, making it one of the best-known African sauropods. Notably, evidence of healed wounds on the tail of one Jobaria individual suggests predator-prey interaction, possibly with the theropod Afrovenator, which is known from the same or similar formations. Jobaria's discovery greatly expanded knowledge of Jurassic dinosaurs in sub-Saharan Africa.

## Anatomy & life

Jobaria is known from a skull and several skeletal elements representing multiple individuals, making it one of the more completely known Jurassic sauropods from Africa. It had a relatively short neck for a large sauropod, suggesting a different feeding strategy from the long-necked brachiosaurids. Estimated at about 21 metres, it was a dominant herbivore of its ecosystem.

Healed tail vertebrae in at least one Jobaria individual show bite marks consistent with a large theropod attack. The theropod Afrovenator is known from the same region and is considered the probable attacker. The healed injuries indicate the animal survived the encounter, providing direct evidence of predator-prey interactions in Jurassic Africa.

Jobaria substantially increased knowledge of African Jurassic sauropods and Saharan dinosaur faunas. Its relatively primitive features for a large Jurassic sauropod raised questions about whether African populations were isolated from Laurasian forms. The associated fauna, including Afrovenator and other taxa, helps reconstruct the ecosystems of Jurassic West Africa.

## Fossil record

Jobaria tiguidensis was described in 1999 by Paul Sereno, David Beck, Didier Dutheil, Hans Larsson, Gabrielle Lyon, Boubacar Moussa, Rudyard Sadleir, Christian Sidor, David Varricchio, Gregory Wilson and Jeffrey Wilson from a skull and multiple skeletons collected during expeditions to Niger. The genus is named for Jobar, a mythical creature of local Tuareg legend.

Multi-individual cranial and postcranial assemblage with much of the skeleton represented.

- The material represents multiple individuals and should not be described as one complete skeleton.
- A 2009 stratigraphic reassessment favours a late Middle Jurassic age.
- Its exact position among non-neosauropod eusauropods varies across analyses.

## Where it lived

Desert outcrops in the Ténéré region of Niger where Sereno's expeditions recovered skull and skeletal material of Jobaria in the 1990s.

Corrected from Early Cretaceous to the late Middle Jurassic interpretation of the Tiouraren Formation; no direct radiometric date is available.

## Open questions

- The formation lacks direct radiometric dates.
- The association of disarticulated material and exact eusauropod position remain uncertain.
