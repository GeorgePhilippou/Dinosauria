## Overview

Avaceratops lammersi was a small centrosaurine ceratopsid dinosaur from the Late Cretaceous of Montana and Alberta, living approximately 80–75 million years ago during the Campanian stage. Estimated at about 2.3 metres in length based on known material, it is known primarily from fossils recovered from the Judith River Formation of Montana. It was one of the smallest members of the Ceratopsidae, and it is possible that the known specimen represents a juvenile rather than an adult individual.

Named by Dodson in 1986, Avaceratops is placed within the family Ceratopsidae (Centrosaurinae) and provides information about body size variation among ceratopsids. The question of whether the known material represents an adult individual or a juvenile of a larger species has been debated. PBDB data places it within the tribe Nasutoceratopsini, suggesting taxonomic affinities with other medium-sized centrosaurines of the Campanian.

## Anatomy & life

Avaceratops possessed the typical centrosaurine skull plan — a prominent nasal horn, short brow horns, and a moderately developed frill with epoccipitals along its margin. Its relatively small size compared to other ceratopsids has been interpreted by some researchers as indicating the known specimen is a subadult, while others maintain it represents a valid small-bodied adult species. The frill anatomy places it within Centrosaurinae.

A significant debate about Avaceratops concerns whether its small size indicates it was a juvenile or a genuinely small-bodied adult. Bone histological analysis (examination of growth lines in thin sections of bone) has the potential to resolve this question, but interpretations of the available data have not produced consensus. If the specimen is indeed a juvenile, Avaceratops may represent a growth stage of a larger and possibly already-named ceratopsid.

Avaceratops inhabited the well-watered coastal floodplains bordering the Western Interior Seaway during the Campanian. It was a ground-dwelling herbivore, processing vegetation with its beak and dental battery. The Judith River ecosystem was exceptionally diverse, including multiple ceratopsid species, hadrosaurs, ankylosaurs, small theropods, and tyrannosaurs.

## Fossil record

Avaceratops lammersi was described by Peter Dodson in 1986 from a partial skull and skeleton found in the Judith River Formation of Montana. The generic name honours Ava Cole, wife of Eddie Cole who discovered the specimen. The species name recognises the Lammers family, on whose land the fossils were found. The holotype consists of a nearly complete skull with much of the postcranial skeleton.

One partial juvenile skull and skeleton plus debated referred skull material.

## Where it lived

Most known Avaceratops fossils come from this formation, which has yielded a diverse Late Cretaceous dinosaur fauna including numerous ceratopsids and hadrosaurs.

## Open questions

- The holotype is immature.
- The referral of larger skulls and the genus's phylogenetic position remain debated.
