## Overview

Camptosaurus dispar was a stocky ornithopod from the Late Jurassic Morrison Formation (~155–145 Ma), reaching up to five metres. It could walk on two or four legs — its wrist structure allowed quadrupedal locomotion — and represents an intermediate form between the small bipedal hypsilophodontids and the large Iguanodon lineage. Camptosaurus is among the most common herbivores of the Morrison Formation.

Bone pathologies (healed injuries on ribs and limb bones) indicate Camptosaurus was a common prey item for Allosaurus. The injuries — bite marks and healed bone infections consistent with predator attack — provide direct evidence of predator-prey interactions in the Morrison ecosystem, complementing the famous Allosaurus-Stegosaurus interaction evidence.

## Anatomy & life

Unlike hypsilophodontids, Camptosaurus had a robust wrist with adaptations allowing the hand to bear weight. It could rear up to feed from higher vegetation and drop to all fours for sustained locomotion. This flexibility anticipated the obligate quadrupedalism of Iguanodon and hadrosaurs.

Several Camptosaurus specimens show healed bite marks on ribs and limbs consistent with Allosaurus attack. One individual shows a healed bite wound on the ilium — the animal survived the attack. These pathologies, combined with the size difference between Camptosaurus and Allosaurus, suggest Camptosaurus was a regular prey item in Morrison food webs.

## Fossil record

Named by Marsh in 1885 from Como Bluff material; the name "flexible lizard" refers to its curved femur. Multiple specimens are known from the Morrison Formation, making it one of the best-studied ornithopods from the Jurassic. A specimen from the Jurassic of England (C. prestwichii) has been reassigned to the genus Cumnoria.

Multiple partial skeletons and skulls, including complementary adult and juvenile material.

Limb and jaw material support a bipedal to facultatively quadrupedal browsing ornithopod.

## Where it lived

Late Jurassic Morrison herbivore fauna with sauropods, stegosaurs and small ornithischians.

## Open questions

- Historical referrals from Europe and elsewhere largely represent other taxa.
- The genus-level sample still needs specimen-by-specimen revision.
