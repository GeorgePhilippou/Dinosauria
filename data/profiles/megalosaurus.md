## Overview

Megalosaurus bucklandii was the first dinosaur formally described to science — named by William Buckland in 1824 from a partial lower jaw and postcranial elements found in the Stonesfield Slate of Oxfordshire. It was a large Mid-Jurassic theropod, about six metres long, living at the margin of the warm Tethys Sea roughly 166 million years ago.

For over a century "Megalosaurus" was a wastebasket taxon into which virtually every European theropod fragment was placed. Systematic revision in the late 20th century restricted the genus to Stonesfield Slate material, leaving it as a tetanuran of uncertain family-level placement.

## Anatomy & life

Material includes a partial dentary with large recurved serrated teeth, vertebrae, an ilium and limb elements. The teeth are laterally compressed, blade-like and strongly recurved — classic large theropod morphology. No complete skull is known, so most life reconstructions are inferred from relatives.

## Fossil record

Buckland presented his description at the Geological Society of London in February 1824. The word "dinosaur" did not yet exist — Owen coined it in 1842, using Megalosaurus as one of the three founding members. Charles Dickens referenced it walking down Holborn Hill in the opening of Bleak House (1852), reflecting its hold on Victorian imagination.

Lectotype dentary plus limited referred cranial and postcranial bones from the same regional unit.

Large theropod jaw and limb bones made it one of the first dinosaurs scientifically described.

- The lectotype is a partial right dentary with teeth.
- Some cranial, vertebral, pelvic and limb bones from the same regional unit are referred, but no complete skeleton exists.
- Most historical species named Megalosaurus have been removed or are dubious.

## Where it lived

The Stonesfield Slate was deposited in warm, shallow-marine to coastal settings on the margin of the Tethys Sea. Megalosaurus was likely the apex terrestrial predator of this coastal ecosystem, sharing its world with the sauropod Cetiosaurus, fish, marine reptiles and abundant invertebrates.

Middle Jurassic European terrestrial to nearshore environments.

The reviewed age follows the middle Bathonian Taynton Limestone and does not import unrelated Megalosaurus referrals.

## Open questions

- Association of referred bones with the dentary-bearing taxon is not direct.
- Maximum adult size is not constrained by a complete individual.
