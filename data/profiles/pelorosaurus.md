## Overview

Pelorosaurus (meaning 'monstrous lizard') was a large titanosauriform sauropod from the Early Cretaceous (approximately 145–110 million years ago) of England, UK, and possibly France. Fossils assigned to Pelorosaurus include material from the Wealden Group and Lower Greensand of southern England. Estimated at up to approximately 24–25 metres in length, it was one of the largest dinosaurs known from the Early Cretaceous of Europe. It was a quadrupedal herbivore.

Named by Gideon Mantell in 1850, Pelorosaurus is one of the earliest-named sauropods and has a notoriously complex taxonomic history: numerous species from England and other countries were assigned to the genus over more than a century, most of which have since been reassigned or declared nomina dubia. The first-named species, P. conybeari, is now considered a junior synonym of Cetiosaurus brevis. The genus is placed within Titanosauriformes (PBDB and NHM both confirm this). Despite the nomenclatural confusion, 'Pelorosaurus' material remains scientifically important as among the earliest evidence for large-bodied titanosauriforms in western Europe.

## Anatomy & life

The Early Cretaceous of western Europe was a time of reduced terrestrial faunal diversity compared to the Late Jurassic. Sauropods were less abundant but still present, and material assigned to Pelorosaurus documents the persistence of large titanosauriforms into the Hauterivian and Aptian of Britain. These animals would have inhabited the seasonal subtropical lowlands of the European archipelago.

Despite its taxonomic difficulties, Pelorosaurus material from England has contributed to understanding the anatomy of early titanosauriforms. The humerus from the type specimen, described by Mantell, was one of the first sauropod bones to be formally illustrated and discussed, making it historically significant in the development of vertebrate palaeontology as a discipline.

## Fossil record

Pelorosaurus conybeari was named by Gideon Mantell in 1850, making it one of the oldest names applied to any sauropod. Mantell based it on a humerus and vertebral material from Sussex, England. The name 'monstrous lizard' reflects nineteenth-century appreciation of the animal's enormous size. The genus name Pelorosaurus was among the first to be given to any sauropod dinosaur, predating widespread understanding of sauropod biology.

Historically associated humerus and caudal material; numerous old referrals now excluded.

## Where it lived

Early Cretaceous terrestrial to brackish deposits of East and West Sussex, Isle of Wight, and surrounding areas, principal source of material assigned to Pelorosaurus.

## Classification

More than a dozen species have been assigned to Pelorosaurus over the past 170 years, from England, Portugal, the United States and elsewhere. The vast majority of these assignments have since been rejected or reassigned to other genera. P. conybeari itself is now a junior synonym of Cetiosaurus brevis. This species-level instability makes Pelorosaurus a cautionary example of how historical 'wastebasket' taxa can accumulate unrelated material.

## Open questions

- The composition and diagnostic validity of P. conybeari remain disputed.
- Historical referrals cannot be treated as one genus.
