## Overview

Hylaeosaurus (meaning 'woodland lizard') was a herbivorous ankylosaur from the Early Cretaceous of England, living approximately 139–110 million years ago. Known from specimens from the Wealden Group of Sussex, it reached an estimated 5 metres in length and was armoured with osteoderms and spines. It was described by Gideon Mantell in 1833 and holds a remarkable distinction in the history of science: a Hylaeosaurus specimen was one of the three fossils used by Richard Owen in 1841 to define the new grouping Dinosauria.

Hylaeosaurus is placed within Nodosauridae by the PBDB, making it an ankylosaur without a tail club — in contrast to ankylosaurids. Additional possible occurrences have been noted from Germany and France based on Wealden-equivalent strata. The material from the holotype is largely still embedded in rock at the Natural History Museum in London, meaning much of its anatomy remains unknown. Its historical importance is outsized relative to the completeness of the material.

## Anatomy & life

In 1841, Richard Owen used Hylaeosaurus, together with Megalosaurus and Iguanodon, as the three founding genera for his newly coined group Dinosauria. This makes Hylaeosaurus one of the three original dinosaurs — a cornerstone of palaeontological history. Owen recognised shared features in these taxa that distinguished them from living and other extinct reptiles.

The visible armour of Hylaeosaurus includes prominent spines and osteoderms along the neck and back. Its nodosaurid placement suggests the absence of an ankylosaurid tail club. The embedded nature of the holotype means that much of its skeleton remains hidden within rock, limiting anatomical knowledge. Estimated at up to 5 metres long, it was a moderate-sized ankylosaur.

Beyond its role founding Dinosauria, Hylaeosaurus represents an important early ankylosaur from Europe. PBDB data indicates occurrences across the Wealden of England and possible equivalents in Germany and France, suggesting ankylosaurs were widespread across the Early Cretaceous European archipelago. Its full anatomy may eventually be revealed through modern CT scanning of the host block.

## Fossil record

Hylaeosaurus armatus was named by Gideon Mantell in 1833 from a partial skeleton embedded in a block of limestone from the Tilgate Stone of Sussex. The name means 'woodland lizard', reflecting the forested character of the local Wealden environment. The holotype block, still largely unprepared, resides at the Natural History Museum, London.

One incomplete associated skeleton with axial, girdle, limb and armour elements.

## Where it lived

The holotype was discovered in the Tilgate Forest area of Sussex and is one of the historically significant Early Cretaceous dinosaur localities in Britain.

## Open questions

- Most of the skull and many limb elements are unknown.
- The original position of individual osteoderms is incompletely documented.
