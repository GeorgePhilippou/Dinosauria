## Overview

Kentrosaurus aethiopicus was a stegosaurid from the Tendaguru Formation of Tanzania. Abundant disarticulated remains from many individuals represent most of the skeleton and armour, but no single complete skeleton preserves the full sequence of plates and spikes.

Kentrosaurus aethiopicus was Africa's answer to Stegosaurus — a smaller stegosaur (~5 m) from the Tendaguru Formation of Tanzania (~155–150 Ma) that replaced the broad plates of its North American cousin with a dramatic array of paired spikes running the length of its back and tail, plus a distinctive shoulder spike projecting laterally from each flank. Thousands of Kentrosaurus bones were excavated by German expeditions between 1909 and 1913.

Biomechanical studies of the tail show that Kentrosaurus could swing its spiked tail with enough force to puncture bone — a defensive weapon significantly more dangerous than Stegosaurus's thagomizer. The shoulder spikes would have made approach from the flanks hazardous for any predator. As part of the Tendaguru fauna alongside Giraffatitan, it shows that the Late Jurassic stegosaur radiation extended across both Laurasia and Gondwana.

## Anatomy & life

Kentrosaurus was a small stegosaur, roughly four to four and a half metres long, with a low head, a deep body carried on four columnar limbs and a long tail. Its armour changed along the body: narrow plates over the neck and shoulders gave way to paired, sharp spikes along the back half of the trunk and down the tail. An additional long spike is usually reconstructed on the shoulder or hip, but its position was never found in place.

Its small skull and simple leaf-shaped teeth indicate cropping of low vegetation. Bone histology from many individuals shows a growth pattern comparable to other stegosaurs, although the exact age at death of any one animal is not known.

The name means pointed lizard. Kentrosaurus was collected by the German Tendaguru expeditions of 1909 to 1913, which recovered well over a thousand bones from a single hillside quarry. Much of that collection was destroyed or lost during the Second World War, but the Berlin mount and many catalogued elements survive.

- Paired tail spikes up to about half a metre long, arranged along a highly mobile tail that could swing through a wide arc.
- A gradual change from small plates at the front of the body to spikes behind, unlike the broad plates of Stegosaurus.
- A small, narrow skull with simple teeth, suited to low browsing rather than tough vegetation.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Small leaf-shaped teeth and a low head indicate browsing near ground level. No gut contents record specific plants.

**Movement: Quadrupedal.** Robust forelimbs and hindlimbs show four-legged walking. Computer modelling suggests a rearing posture was possible but does not show it was habitual.

**Appearance: Armour outline supported.** Plate and spike shapes are known from many isolated osteoderms, but no articulated series fixes their sequence or the position of the long lateral spike.

## Fossil record

Kentrosaurus is known from a very large collection of bones representing many individuals, recovered mainly from one quarry at Tendaguru. Almost every part of the skeleton is represented, but the material is disarticulated and no complete individual was found.

Vertebrae from every part of the column, ribs, both limb girdles, forelimb and hindlimb bones, and numerous plates and spikes, together with a braincase, partial jaws and teeth. The Berlin mount and a second composite skeleton were assembled from this material.

Part of the original collection was lost or destroyed during the Second World War, and the surviving material is disarticulated, so individual skeletons cannot be reassembled and the armour arrangement remains a reconstruction.

Spikes, plates and postcranial material document a smaller, heavily armed stegosaur.

- The familiar armour arrangement is a composite reconstruction supported by repeated isolated osteoderm shapes.
- Tail spikes provided a plausible defensive weapon, as supported by anatomy and biomechanical modelling.
- Bone histology records growth but does not provide exact population ages for every specimen.

## Where it lived

Late Jurassic East African coastal plain and deltaic deposits.

Most material comes from Kimmeridgian-Tithonian Middle and Upper Dinosaur members of Tendaguru.

## Classification

Kentrosaurus is a stegosaurid, a member of the plated and spiked thyreophoran group that includes Stegosaurus. Analyses generally place it outside the clade containing Stegosaurus and its closest relatives, but its position among other Jurassic stegosaurs varies.

## Open questions

### How were the plates and spikes arranged?

No articulated armour series exists. The familiar arrangement is a composite built from repeated osteoderm shapes, and the long lateral spike may have sat on the shoulder or the hip.

### How many individuals are represented?

The quarry St bonebed yielded bones from dozens of animals, but because they were disarticulated, individual skeletons cannot be reassembled with confidence.

### What was the tail for?

Anatomy and biomechanical modelling make defensive tail strikes plausible, but no injured predator or damaged spike has been linked to the genus.
