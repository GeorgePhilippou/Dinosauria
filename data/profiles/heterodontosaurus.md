## Overview

Heterodontosaurus tucki was a small bipedal ornithischian from the upper Elliot Formation of South Africa. Several skulls and one nearly complete skeleton document a compact herbivore with a beak, cheek teeth and enlarged canine-like teeth.

Heterodontosaurus tucki was a small, early ornithischian from the Early Jurassic of South Africa (~200–190 Ma), about 1.2 metres long, named for its three different tooth types — a unique feature among dinosaurs. It had large canine-like tusks, small incisiform teeth at the jaw front, and cheek teeth with leaf-shaped crowns for processing vegetation, resembling the dental diversity of heterodont mammals.

As one of the earliest well-documented ornithischians, Heterodontosaurus provides crucial information about the ancestral ornithischian body plan. Its small size, complex dentition and possible semi-bipedal locomotion represent a generalised early ornithischian that preceded the major radiations of the group into stegosaurs, ankylosaurs, ornithopods and ceratopsians.

## Anatomy & life

Heterodontosaurus was a turkey-sized ornithischian that walked on two legs, with a short skull, a horny beak at the tip of the jaws and a long tail. The most complete individual is a little over a metre long; published mass estimates fall in the range of a few kilograms, and the animal was among the smallest dinosaurs of the Elliot fauna.

Its name refers to its unusual dentition. Small teeth at the front of the upper jaw were followed by a pair of enlarged, canine-like tusks in both jaws, then a row of tightly packed cheek teeth with chisel-shaped crowns. Wear surfaces on the cheek teeth and the jaw joint indicate that the upper and lower tooth rows slid against one another to grind plant food, one of the earliest examples of true chewing in dinosaurs.

The hands were relatively large, with five fingers and strong claws on the first three, and the forelimb bones show muscle attachments that suggest a grasping or digging capacity. The hindlimb was long and lightly built, with the shin bones and upper foot bones fused together in the near-complete skeleton, a feature more often associated with fast runners.

- Three distinct tooth types: small front teeth, enlarged canine-like tusks and closely packed cheek teeth.
- Tooth wear and jaw anatomy show that the cheek teeth ground against one another, an early form of chewing.
- Fused shin and ankle bones and a long, lightly built hindlimb are consistent with an agile two-legged runner.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Grinding wear on the cheek teeth and the beak support a plant diet. The tusks do not establish meat-eating; opportunistic animal food remains possible but unproven.

**Movement: Bipedal runner.** Long hindlimbs, fused lower-leg and ankle bones and short forelimbs indicate habitual two-legged movement; running speed is not directly measured.

**Appearance: Skeletal outline well supported.** No skin or filament impressions are preserved for Heterodontosaurus itself; bristle-like structures in restorations come from the related Tianyulong.

## Fossil record

Heterodontosaurus is known from a well-preserved holotype skull, a near-complete articulated skeleton with skull, a second near-complete skeleton studied by synchrotron scanning, and several further skulls and partial specimens, including a very small juvenile.

The skull is known in detail from the holotype and several other individuals. The near-complete skeleton SAM-PK-K1332 preserves the neck, back, sacrum and most of the tail, both girdles and all four limbs in articulation, and AM 4766 adds a second articulated skeleton that includes rarely preserved sternal ribs and gastralia.

The end of the tail is not preserved in any specimen, and no skin, filament or gut-content evidence is known for the genus itself.

Three distinct tooth types in one jaw and a small but complete skeleton make this one of the most informative early ornithischians known.

- The profile Late Triassic age is incorrect; secure material is Early Jurassic.
- Canine-like teeth do not prove a carnivorous diet and may have had display or defence roles.
- Jaw mechanics and tooth wear support effective plant processing, while opportunistic animal matter remains possible but unproven.

## Classification

Heterodontosaurus is the name-giving member of Heterodontosauridae, a family of small, early-diverging ornithischians. Most analyses place heterodontosaurids near the base of the ornithischian tree, but whether they sit closer to the marginocephalians or outside all other ornithischians remains debated.

## Open questions

### What were the tusks for?

The enlarged canine-like teeth have been read as display, defence or feeding structures. They occur in the smallest known juvenile, which weakens a purely sex-linked explanation, but no single function is established.

### Did it eat anything other than plants?

The cheek teeth are clearly built for grinding vegetation. Whether Heterodontosaurus also took insects or small animals, as some authors have proposed, cannot be tested from the fossils.

### Where do heterodontosaurids belong?

Heterodontosaurids have been placed as the earliest branch of Ornithischia and, alternatively, close to pachycephalosaurs and ceratopsians. The answer changes with the dataset used.
