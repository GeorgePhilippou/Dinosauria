## Overview

Diplodocus was a long-necked diplodocid sauropod from the Morrison Formation of western North America. D. carnegii and related material provide several substantial skeletons and skulls, but famous museum mounts are composites and species assignments have been extensively revised.

Diplodocus carnegii ("Carnegie's double beam") was one of the longest animals ever to walk the Earth, reaching 26 metres, though far lighter than the stocky Brachiosaurus at around 15 tonnes. Its neck was held roughly horizontally rather than upright, and the peg-like teeth at the very front of the snout were used exclusively for raking leaves from branches — Diplodocus could not chew and processed no food in the mouth at all.

Andrew Carnegie funded plaster casts of the famous specimen "Dippy" that were shipped to eight museums across Europe, making Diplodocus the most publicly recognised sauropod of the 20th century. The NHM London's Dippy stood in the main entrance hall for 112 years before being replaced by the blue whale skeleton Hope in 2017.

## Anatomy & life

Diplodocus was a very long but comparatively lightly built sauropod of the Late Jurassic Morrison Formation. Diplodocus carnegii, the best-known species, reached about twenty-four to twenty-six metres from snout to tail tip, most of that length made up of a long neck and an even longer tail. Mass estimates of roughly twelve to fifteen tonnes are model-dependent.

The skull was long and low with nostrils set high on the head and slender, pencil-like teeth confined to the front of the jaws. Tooth wear shows that the animal cropped or stripped vegetation rather than chewing it. The forelimbs were shorter than the hindlimbs, giving the back a gentle slope, and the tail ended in a run of thin, rod-like vertebrae often described as a whip.

Diplodocus is the name-bearing genus of Diplodocidae, but its own taxonomy is untidy. The original type species, D. longus, rests on tail bones that most recent workers consider undiagnostic, and a 2015 specimen-level revision proposed D. carnegii as the new type species. The giant D. hallorum, once named Seismosaurus, is now generally included in the genus.

- A long, low skull with nostrils high on the head and slender peg-like teeth at the front of the jaws.
- An extremely long tail of more than eighty vertebrae, tapering to a thin, whip-like tip.
- Forelimbs shorter than hindlimbs, with a single large claw on each hand.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Peg-like teeth with wear facets and rapid tooth replacement indicate cropping or branch-stripping. Feeding height depends on neck posture models rather than direct evidence.

**Movement: Quadrupedal.** Complete limbs and girdles from several skeletons and Morrison sauropod trackways establish four-legged walking. Rearing on the hindlimbs is a hypothesis.

**Appearance: Skeletal outline well supported.** Proportions are secure. Skin impressions from Morrison diplodocids show small scales and a row of keratinous spines, but none is certainly Diplodocus, and colour is unknown.

## Fossil record

Diplodocus is well sampled for a sauropod, with two associated Carnegie skeletons providing most of the postcranial skeleton and further partial skeletons from Colorado, Wyoming, Utah and New Mexico. Skulls are known but their species-level referral is debated, and the original type material is a set of tail bones.

Nearly complete vertebral columns including the long whip tail, pectoral and pelvic girdles, complete fore- and hindlimbs, ribs and chevrons, plus several skulls with braincases and teeth from Morrison quarries. The Carnegie holotype and paratype together supply most of the skeleton.

The celebrated Carnegie mount and its casts combine CM 84 with CM 94 and other specimens, with a skull and feet modelled from other animals. Diplodocus skulls are known, but which species they belong to is contested.

Long neck, whip-like tail and peg-like teeth support low browsing and diplodocid feeding interpretations.

- The Carnegie mount combines principal specimens and reconstructed parts; it is not one complete individual.
- Peg-like front teeth and low skull support selective cropping, while exact vegetation and feeding height varied with posture.
- Extreme tail-whip speeds and supersonic cracking remain biomechanical hypotheses rather than consensus facts.

## Where it lived

Late Jurassic Morrison floodplains with other diplodocids, macronarians and large theropods.

Most diagnostic Diplodocus material comes from Kimmeridgian-Tithonian Morrison intervals near 154-152 Ma.

## Classification

Diplodocus gives its name to Diplodocidae and Diplodocinae within Diplodocoidea. Its position is stable; the open issues are which species are valid, whether D. longus can remain the type species, and how many historic specimens truly belong to the genus.

## Open questions

### Which species is the type?

D. longus was named first, on tail vertebrae that are hard to diagnose. A petition proposed D. carnegii as the type species instead; until that is settled, the genus name rests on shaky foundations.

### How did it hold its neck?

Reconstructions range from a near-horizontal neck to a raised one. Joint anatomy, cartilage and soft tissue all affect the answer, and no posture is directly recorded.

### Could the tail crack like a whip?

Computer models have suggested the tail tip could move very fast, but claims of supersonic cracking remain untested hypotheses rather than accepted fact.
