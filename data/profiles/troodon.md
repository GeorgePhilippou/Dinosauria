## Overview

Troodon formosus was named from a single tooth collected in Montana. From 2017 the tooth was widely treated as insufficient to anchor the many skeletons historically assigned to Troodon, and those fossils were reassigned to Stenonychosaurus and other taxa. A 2025 study defended Troodon as valid, synonymised Stenonychosaurus with it and proposed a Two Medicine Formation skeleton as a neotype. That neotype requires ICZN action, so the issue is not presented as settled.

Troodon formosus was a small Late Cretaceous coelurosaur from North America with unusually large brain-to-body ratio, large forward-facing eyes suggesting binocular vision, and sickle-shaped second toe claws. These features led Dale Russell's famous 1982 thought experiment imagining a "dinosauroid" — what Troodon might have evolved into had it survived — a humanoid figure with a large brain and bipedal stance.

Troodon is now a complicated taxon: much material historically referred to it may belong to different animals, and recent analyses suggest the name may apply only to isolated teeth, with the skeletal material belonging to Stenonychosaurus. Regardless of nomenclature, troodontids represent the most neurologically sophisticated non-avian dinosaurs documented from the fossil record.

## Anatomy & life

Troodon was named by Joseph Leidy in 1856 for one small, coarsely serrated tooth from the Judith River beds of Montana, one of the first dinosaurs named from North America. For over a century the tooth was compared with lizards and then with plant-eating dinosaurs before being recognised as belonging to a small theropod.

The animal most people picture as Troodon is a lightly built, long-legged troodontid about two to three metres long, with very large eyes, one of the largest brains relative to body size among dinosaurs, and a raised sickle claw on each foot. Those features are documented in skeletons from Alberta and Montana, including nesting sites with eggs and embryos, that were referred to Troodon during the late twentieth century.

Since 2017 many researchers have argued that a single tooth cannot be distinguished from those of related troodontids, and have restored the name Stenonychosaurus for the Alberta skeletons. A 2025 study reversed course, defending Troodon and proposing a Two Medicine Formation skeleton as a replacement type, but that change needs a ruling from the zoological naming commission before it takes effect.

- The name-bearing fossil is one tooth, ANSP 9259, with only approximate locality information.
- Referred skeletons describe a large-eyed, large-brained troodontid with a sickle claw, and nests with eggs and embryos have been linked to the same animal.
- Whether those skeletons can carry the name Troodon is actively disputed between the 2017 and 2025 revisions.

**Feeding: Probably omnivore.** Coarse serrations and relatively small, leaf-shaped teeth differ from typical meat-eaters and have been read as a mixed diet. No gut contents are known for the holotype or referred skeletons.

**Movement: Bipedal.** Long hindlimbs indicate a fast two-legged animal, but every limb bone belongs to referred troodontid material whose link to the name is contested.

**Appearance: Reconstructed from relatives.** No skin or feather impressions are known from any North American troodontid; the feathered restoration rests on Asian relatives.

## Fossil record

The only fossil securely attached to the name Troodon is the holotype tooth ANSP 9259. The skulls, skeletons, eggs and embryos usually shown under the name are referred troodontid material whose assignment has been reversed twice since 2017.

The holotype is a single tooth crown about a centimetre tall with large, hooked serrations. Referred material, if accepted, includes partial skulls, braincases, limb bones and vertebrae from the Dinosaur Park and Two Medicine formations, together with nests, eggs and embryonic bones.

Everything beyond the tooth depends on a taxonomic argument. The panel treats the tooth as the secure evidence and the rest as provisionally referred.

Dental and partial skeletal evidence once grouped under Troodon has been split or reinterpreted in recent research.

- The holotype ANSP 9259 is one isolated tooth with incomplete locality information.
- The familiar full-body reconstruction was assembled from referred troodontid skeletons, not from the holotype.
- The 2017 revisions treated Troodon as a doubtful name and restored Stenonychosaurus for Dinosaur Park material.
- The 2025 paper argued for priority and stability under Troodon and proposed MOR 553 as a neotype, but explicitly noted that only the ICZN can replace an existing holotype in this way.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous North American records, but the genus is now treated cautiously.

A single genus-wide 77-66 Ma range is not defensible while the name-bearing tooth and referred skeletons remain taxonomically disputed. The current 2025 species concept focuses on Campanian Montana and Alberta material.

## Classification

Troodon is the name-giving genus of Troodontidae, a family of small, bird-like theropods within Paraves. The family is well defined; the problem is that the tooth on which the genus rests may not be diagnostic below the family level.

## Open questions

### Can one tooth define a species?

Troodontid teeth are distinctive as a family, but distinguishing genera from teeth alone is difficult. The 2017 revisions concluded the holotype was undiagnostic; the 2025 study argued it can be matched to skeletons.

### Will the neotype be accepted?

Replacing an existing holotype requires an application to the International Commission on Zoological Nomenclature. Until a ruling exists, MOR 553 is a proposal rather than the type.

### Whose nests are they?

Eggs, embryos and clutches from the Two Medicine Formation have been assigned to Troodon. If the name is restricted to the tooth, the nesting evidence belongs to a troodontid whose name depends on the outcome of the dispute.
