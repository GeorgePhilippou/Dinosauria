## Overview

Avimimus portentosus ("bird mimic") was a small Late Cretaceous oviraptorosaur from Mongolia (~70 Ma), known for its highly bird-like proportions: a large rounded braincase, toothless beak, large eyes, and long slender hindlimbs. Its name reflects the striking resemblance to birds noticed by its describer, Kurzanov, in 1981.

A quill-attachment ridge on the ulna of Avimimus — one of the earliest such structures described in non-avian dinosaurs — suggests it bore arm feathers, consistent with its oviraptorosaur relationships. Its unusually large brain relative to body size places it among the most neurologically advanced non-avian dinosaurs known.

## Anatomy & life

The skull is proportionally large and rounded, with a toothless beak. The braincase is expanded relative to body size, one of the largest brain-to-body ratios in non-avian dinosaurs. The legs are long and gracile, optimised for fast running. The quill attachment ridge (calamus scar) on the ulna is interpreted as evidence of arm feathers.

Avimimus is among the more basal oviraptorosaurs phylogenetically, and understanding its position helps trace the evolution of the group. Oviraptorosaurs ranged from Avimimus and Caudipteryx to the giant Gigantoraptor (8 m, 1.4 tonnes). Most had beaks, crests and were probably omnivores to herbivores, occupying a diverse range of body sizes and ecological niches.

## Fossil record

Described by Sergei Kurzanov in 1981 from Mongolian material collected by Soviet-Mongolian expeditions. The name "Avimimus" — bird mimic — reflects Kurzanov's view that the animal was extremely bird-like; he even briefly proposed it might have been a flier, though this was quickly discounted. A bone bed with multiple Avimimus individuals at one site suggests it may have been gregarious.

Several incomplete cranial and postcranial skeletons.

Quill knobs on the arm bones and overall bird-like proportions document its close relationship to modern birds.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous Mongolian floodplain ecosystems.

## Open questions

- Some historical referrals may belong to A. nemegtensis.
- Diet and plumage details are inferred rather than directly preserved.
