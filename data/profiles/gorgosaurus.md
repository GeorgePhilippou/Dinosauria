## Overview

Gorgosaurus libratus was an albertosaurine tyrannosaurid from the Dinosaur Park Formation of Alberta and equivalent strata in Montana. Dozens of skulls and skeletons spanning juvenile to adult stages make its growth and anatomy unusually well documented.

Gorgosaurus libratus was a large tyrannosaur from the Campanian of Alberta (~76–75 Ma), the dominant apex predator of the Dinosaur Park Formation ecosystem. It preceded and partially overlapped with Daspletosaurus, the other large tyrannosaur in the same formation. Excellent growth-series specimens allow detailed study of tyrannosaur ontogeny.

Gorgosaurus was for many years synonymised with Albertosaurus, but current opinion treats them as distinct genera: Gorgosaurus is stratigraphically older and differs from Albertosaurus in several cranial features. Both are albertosaurine tyrannosaurs — a clade that preceded and co-existed with the derived tyrannosaurines (Daspletosaurus, Tyrannosaurus) in the Late Cretaceous of North America.

## Anatomy & life

Gorgosaurus was a large two-legged predator of the tyrannosaurid family that lived on the river plains of what is now Alberta, and probably Montana, in the late Campanian. Adults reached about eight to nine metres in length, lighter and longer-legged than the later Tyrannosaurus, with a long, low skull, small two-fingered hands and a deep tail.

It is one of the best-sampled tyrannosaurids, with dozens of skulls and skeletons from the Dinosaur Park Formation ranging from small juveniles to full adults. That growth series shows young animals were slender and long-limbed with blade-like teeth, while adults became heavier with thicker teeth, suggesting that juveniles and adults hunted differently.

A juvenile from Alberta preserves the hindlimbs of two small caenagnathid dinosaurs inside its ribcage, direct evidence of what one young Gorgosaurus ate. It shared its habitat with the larger tyrannosaurid Daspletosaurus, and the two are thought to have divided the available prey, though how is still debated.

- A long, low skull with more slender proportions than Tyrannosaurus, and reduced two-fingered forelimbs.
- A growth series from small juveniles to adults documents a change from slender, long-legged young to heavier adults.
- A juvenile specimen preserves the hindlimbs of two small caenagnathids as stomach contents.

**Feeding: Carnivore.** Stomach contents in a juvenile and blade-like serrated teeth document meat-eating. Adult prey choice is inferred from bite marks and tooth shape, not gut contents.

**Movement: Bipedal.** Complete hindlimbs across growth stages show a fast-walking biped; juveniles have longer, more cursorial legs. Top speeds are modelled rather than measured.

**Appearance: Skeleton well documented.** Skeletal proportions are secure, but no skin, scale or feather impressions are known for Gorgosaurus, so covering and colour are inferred.

## Fossil record

Gorgosaurus libratus is among the best-sampled large theropods. Its holotype is a near-complete skeleton, and many further skulls and skeletons from the Dinosaur Park Formation span small juveniles to adults, one of them with stomach contents in place.

Complete skulls with dentition and braincases, full vertebral columns, furculae, both forelimbs with two-fingered hands, pelves, hindlimbs and feet, in several articulated skeletons, plus healed injuries and one juvenile's stomach contents.

Integument is the main gap: no skin, scale or feather impressions are known for the genus, so its external covering is inferred from other tyrannosauroids.

A growth series from juvenile to adult documents the dramatic ontogenetic change in skull shape and tooth morphology in tyrannosaurs.

- Juveniles were more slender and long-legged than adults.
- Preserved stomach contents in a juvenile provide direct evidence that at least young Gorgosaurus ate small caenagnathid dinosaurs.
- That single meal does not establish the full diet of all ages or coordinated hunting.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous coastal plain of western Canada with hadrosaurs, ceratopsians and ankylosaurs.

Most secure G. libratus material comes from the Dinosaur Park Formation, approximately 76.6-75.1 Ma.

## Classification

Gorgosaurus is an albertosaurine tyrannosaurid, sister to Albertosaurus within the lighter-built branch of Tyrannosauridae. It was long treated as a species of Albertosaurus, but is now generally kept as a separate genus.

## Open questions

### How did it share its world with Daspletosaurus?

Two large tyrannosaurids lived side by side in the Dinosaur Park Formation. Differences in build hint at different prey, but direct evidence of partitioning is limited.

### Did juveniles and adults eat differently?

One juvenile's stomach contents and the change in tooth and limb shape with growth support a shift in diet, but a single meal cannot define the diet of every age class.

### Did it hunt in groups?

No bonebed or trackway demonstrates social hunting in Gorgosaurus. Group behaviour remains speculative.
