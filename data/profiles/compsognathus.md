## Overview

Compsognathus longipes was one of the smallest non-avian dinosaurs, about the size of a turkey (0.65–1 m), from the Late Jurassic Solnhofen Limestone of Germany (~150 Ma). For over a century it held the record as the smallest known dinosaur, and the first specimen described was the most complete theropod known at the time — providing crucial evidence that dinosaurs were slender, active animals rather than lumbering reptiles.

The Solnhofen lagoon-margin environment where Compsognathus is found also preserves Archaeopteryx, and the similarity between the two — a small feathered theropod and a small non-feathered theropod of similar size — fuelled early debates about the origin of birds. Its stomach contents in both known specimens include small lizards, confirming it was a fast active predator.

## Anatomy & life

Compsognathus longipes was a lightly built theropod with a small, pointed head, a long neck, slender hindlimbs and a tail that made up about half its length. The German skeleton is under a metre long and was for many years the smallest dinosaur known; the later French specimen is about a third larger.

Its diet is documented unusually well. The German specimen preserves the skeleton of a small lizard, Bavarisaurus, in the region of the gut, showing that it caught or scavenged fast-moving small prey. The forelimbs were short, with hands that Gishlick and Gauthier reinterpreted in 2007 as having two functional fingers and a reduced third.

Compsognathus lived on islands and shorelines of a shallow tropical sea, where fine limestone mud entombed carcasses with remarkable fidelity. The same deposits yield Archaeopteryx, and the two animals were compared closely in nineteenth-century debates about the origin of birds.

- Two articulated skeletons of different sizes are known, one from Germany and one from France.
- A lizard skeleton preserved in the gut region of the German specimen is direct evidence of what it ate.
- Its slender build and long legs mark it as a fast-moving pursuit predator of small animals.

**Feeding: Small-prey carnivore.** A lizard skeleton inside the German specimen shows small vertebrate prey. The wider diet is inferred from the teeth.

**Movement: Bipedal runner.** Long shins and feet relative to the thigh suggest a fast runner; actual speeds are not measured.

**Appearance: Skeleton well known.** No feathers or skin are preserved. A coat of simple filaments is inferred from Sinosauropteryx and other relatives; some authors have suggested scales on the tail.

## Fossil record

Compsognathus longipes is known from the German holotype, an articulated skeleton on a limestone slab with a lizard in its gut region, and a larger, slightly less complete skeleton from Canjuers in France originally named C. corallestris.

The skull, complete vertebral column, ribs, gastralia, shoulder and pelvic girdles, forelimbs and hindlimbs of the holotype, all lying articulated on a single slab, plus a comparable but larger skeleton from France.

Both skeletons are flattened, and the small hand bones are hard to interpret; the French specimen lacks the skull tip and parts of the limbs. No feathers or skin are preserved.

Stomach contents document its diet of small lizards; exquisite preservation confirms body outline and limb proportions.

## Where it lived

Late Jurassic shallow lagoon environments in what are now southern Germany and southern France.

## Classification

Compsognathus is a small coelurosaur and the name-bearer of Compsognathidae, a group that may also include Sinosauropteryx, Juravenator and others. Whether Compsognathidae is a natural group or a grade of small early coelurosaurs is contested.

## Open questions

### Was it feathered?

No feathers are preserved on either specimen. A filament coat is inferred from relatives, and the possibility of scaly patches on the tail has been raised.

### How old were the two animals?

The German specimen is often called a juvenile and the French one an adult, but bone histology has not settled the growth stage of either.

### Is Compsognathidae a real group?

Some analyses recover the compsognathids as a clade; others scatter them among early coelurosaurs, sometimes as juveniles of other lineages.
