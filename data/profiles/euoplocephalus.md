## Overview

Euoplocephalus tutus was the most completely known ankylosaurid — a heavily armoured tanklike dinosaur from the Campanian of Alberta (~76–75 Ma), about six metres long. Every part of its body, including the eyelids, was armoured with fused bony plates. Its large tail club, formed by fused vertebrae and surrounded by massive bony knobs, could generate forces sufficient to break the fibula of a large theropod.

Euoplocephalus is known from over 40 specimens across multiple formations, making it the most commonly found ankylosaurid in North America. Despite this abundance, most specimens are disarticulated — the heavy, dense body tends to sink and disarticulate in water — and a complete skeleton in natural articulation has not yet been found.

## Anatomy & life

Euoplocephalus was a heavily armoured, four-legged plant-eater of the ankylosaurid group that lived in what is now Alberta, and probably Montana, during the late Campanian. It was around five to six metres long, with a wide, flat body carried on short limbs and a tail ending in a heavy bony club.

Its skull was broad and short, roofed with fused plates of bony armour, with small horn-like bones at the rear corners and even bony eyelids. Rows of keeled osteoderms covered the neck, back and tail, the largest arranged in half-rings around the neck. The teeth were small and leaf-shaped, and the nasal passages inside the skull followed a looping course.

For most of the twentieth century almost every ankylosaurid skeleton from Alberta was called Euoplocephalus, making it seem one of the best-known armoured dinosaurs. A 2013 revision reassigned many of those specimens to Anodontosaurus, Dyoplosaurus and Scolosaurus, leaving a smaller but still substantial sample from the Dinosaur Park Formation.

- A broad skull capped with fused armour plates, with bony eyelids that could close over the eyes.
- Half-rings of large keeled osteoderms around the neck and a tail ending in a solid bony club.
- The historic catch-all genus for Alberta ankylosaurids, cut down to a smaller core sample by modern revision.

**Feeding: Low-browsing plant-eater.** Small leaf-shaped teeth, a broad beak and a wide, low skull fit a low-level browser on soft vegetation. No gut contents are known for the genus.

**Movement: Quadrupedal.** Short, stout limbs and a broad pelvis from partial skeletons indicate a slow, four-legged gait; speed is inferred from proportions.

**Appearance: Armour types known.** Individual osteoderms and neck half-rings are directly preserved, but no single specimen shows the complete armour arrangement, and colour is unknown.

## Fossil record

Euoplocephalus rests on a fragmentary holotype and a set of referred skulls and partial skeletons from the Dinosaur Park Formation. The sample is smaller than the historic literature suggests, because many former specimens are now assigned to other ankylosaurid genera.

A holotype skull roof and neck half-ring, several complete or near-complete referred skulls, and partial skeletons with vertebrae, ribs, girdles, limbs, osteoderms and tail-club material.

Because the holotype is so incomplete, referral of skeletons depends on skull features, and specimens without skulls are difficult to assign with confidence.

Armour plates, tail clubs and skull anatomy are documented in detail; CT scans of skull show extensive internal bone structure.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous coastal plain of western Canada.

## Classification

Euoplocephalus is an ankylosaurine ankylosaurid, part of the club-tailed armoured dinosaurs of Late Cretaceous North America. It is closely related to Anodontosaurus, Scolosaurus and Dyoplosaurus, the genera into which many of its former specimens were moved.

## Open questions

### Which old specimens still belong here?

Skulls and armour that once defined Euoplocephalus have been redistributed among several genera, and a few historic specimens without diagnostic skulls remain hard to place.

### How was the armour arranged?

Osteoderms are known from many individuals, but no single Euoplocephalus skeleton preserves the full pattern in place, so reconstructions borrow from relatives.

### How was the tail club used?

The club is real and biomechanical models suggest it could deliver damaging blows, but whether it was used against predators, rivals or both is not directly recorded.
