## Overview

Staurikosaurus is one of the earliest dinosaurs known from anywhere. It lived in what is now southern Brazil during the Carnian stage of the Late Triassic, when dinosaurs were still small and uncommon members of ecosystems dominated by rhynchosaurs, cynodonts and other reptiles. It was a slender, two-legged predator with long hind limbs and a long tail.

Edwin Colbert named it in 1970 after the Southern Cross, the constellation that appears on Brazil's flag and is best seen from the southern hemisphere. It belongs to Herrerasauridae, an early branch of predatory dinosaurs that also includes Herrerasaurus from Argentina and Gnathovorax from the same Brazilian rocks.

## Anatomy & life

The lower jaw is long and slender, with recurved, serrated teeth suited to seizing small vertebrates. The hind limb is built for fast running on two legs, with a thigh bone shorter than the shin. Only two vertebrae are fused into the sacrum, a primitive condition shared with other herrerasaurids and many dinosaur relatives, whereas most later dinosaurs incorporated three or more.

Its size is estimated by comparison with Herrerasaurus, because the skull roof, neck and forelimbs are missing. It was a small animal, well below the body size of the largest predators of its time, which were rauisuchians and other non-dinosaur archosaurs.

- Among the oldest reliably dated dinosaurs, from rocks about 233 million years old.
- Only two sacral vertebrae, a primitive feature for a dinosaur.
- Slender jaws with blade-like, serrated teeth.

## Fossil record

The single known skeleton was collected in 1936 during a Harvard expedition guided by the Brazilian palaeontologist Llewellyn Ivor Price, and lay unstudied at Harvard for more than three decades. It was redescribed in detail by Bittencourt and Kellner in 2009, who confirmed its placement among the herrerasaurids.

No second specimen has been found, so variation within the species and adult size cannot be assessed.

## Where it lived

Staurikosaurus comes from the lower Santa Maria Formation, the Hyperodapedon Assemblage Zone, where rhynchosaurs were the most common large plant-eaters. Uranium–lead dating of zircon crystals from these beds gives an age of about 233 million years, placing Staurikosaurus among the oldest dinosaurs with a numerical age. The same rocks have produced the early sauropodomorphs Saturnalia and Buriolestes and the larger herrerasaurid Gnathovorax.

## Classification

Staurikosaurus is a herrerasaurid. Herrerasaurids are clearly dinosaurs, but analyses disagree on whether they are early theropods, early saurischians outside the theropod–sauropodomorph split, or a lineage near the base of Dinosauria as a whole. Because Staurikosaurus lacks much of its skull and forelimbs, it contributes relatively little to settling that question on its own.

## Open questions

### Where do herrerasaurids sit in the dinosaur tree?

Different analyses place Herrerasauridae as early theropods, as early saurischians, or even outside Saurischia. The answer affects how the earliest dinosaur body plan is reconstructed.

### How large did it grow?

With only one individual, possibly not fully grown, adult size is uncertain.
