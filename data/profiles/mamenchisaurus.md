## Overview

Mamenchisaurus is a historically broad genus of long-necked Chinese eusauropods. The type species M. constructus is based on a partial skeleton from the Sichuan Basin; other named species differ greatly in completeness and may not form a natural genus.

Mamenchisaurus was a massive herbivorous sauropod dinosaur famous for possessing one of the longest necks relative to body size of any known animal, with the neck comprising nearly half the total body length. It lived during the Late Jurassic, approximately 160–145 million years ago, with some species possibly extending into the Early Cretaceous, in the Sichuan Basin and Yunnan Province of China. Various species ranged from roughly 15 to 26 metres in length, with some fragmentary evidence suggesting individuals potentially approaching 35 metres.

Named by Young (C.C. Yang) in 1954 based on material from Mamenchi Ferry on the Jinsha River in Sichuan, Mamenchisaurus belongs to the Mamenchisauridae, a family of Eurasian sauropods characterised by extremely elongated necks with numerous cervical vertebrae. Numerous species have been assigned to the genus over the decades, though the validity of many of these species assignments has been questioned and the taxonomy remains partially unsettled.

## Anatomy & life

Mamenchisaurus was a large four-legged plant-eater with a neck longer, relative to its body, than in almost any other sauropod. In M. hochuanensis, known from a nearly complete skeleton, nineteen neck vertebrae make up a neck about nine metres long on an animal of roughly twenty-two metres; the type species M. constructus was smaller, around thirteen metres, and the fragmentary M. sinocanadorum may have carried a neck of fifteen metres or more.

The neck vertebrae are lightly built, with long overlapping neck ribs that stiffened the neck into a beam rather than a flexible hose. The skull, known well only in M. youngi, is short and deep with spoon-shaped teeth suited to cropping foliage. Some individuals preserve a small tail club of fused vertebrae whose function is not known.

How the neck was held is debated. Biomechanical modelling suggests a gently inclined posture with the head raised well above the shoulders, giving access to a wide feeding envelope, but the animal could not have lifted its neck vertically. The different species come from separate rock units and ages, and it is not certain that they all belong in one genus.

- Up to nineteen cervical vertebrae, more than in almost any other dinosaur, are directly counted in the M. hochuanensis skeleton.
- Long, overlapping cervical ribs that braced the neck are preserved in several species.
- A short, deep skull with spoon-shaped teeth, known from the M. youngi skeleton, differs from the lighter skulls of diplodocids.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Spoon-shaped teeth and a deep snout indicate browsing on foliage. Which plants it ate and how high it habitually fed are inferred from neck modelling rather than observed.

**Movement: Quadrupedal.** Columnar fore- and hindlimbs are preserved in M. hochuanensis and M. youngi. Speed and gait beyond a walk are not measurable.

**Appearance: Skeletal outline supported.** Body proportions come from articulated skeletons. Skin texture, colour and the habitual angle of the neck are inferred.

## Fossil record

Mamenchisaurus is well represented as a whole, but unevenly. The type species M. constructus rests on a partial skeleton found during dam construction in 1952, while M. hochuanensis and M. youngi are known from articulated skeletons, and other species from far less.

The type species preserves vertebrae, including part of the neck, with ribs, pelvic and limb elements. M. hochuanensis adds a near-complete postcranial skeleton with nineteen cervicals and a tail club; M. youngi adds a near-complete skeleton with skull. M. sinocanadorum is represented by skull bones and a few neck vertebrae.

Because the best skull, the best neck and the type material belong to different species, no single individual shows the whole animal, and measurements should not be combined across species.

- Several species preserve substantial skeletons, but no single completeness claim applies to the whole genus.
- The extraordinary neck length is directly supported in some species; the largest whole-body estimates depend on scaling incomplete individuals.
- A 2023 analysis recovered named Mamenchisaurus species in a non-monophyletic series, requiring genus-level revision.

## Where it lived

Multiple species of Mamenchisaurus have been described from this Late Jurassic formation, including the type species M. constructus.

The range is restricted to well-supported Middle-Late Jurassic Chinese species; Early Cretaceous and non-Chinese referrals are not used to extend it.

## Classification

Mamenchisaurus is a eusauropod and the name-bearing genus of Mamenchisauridae, a mainly Chinese group of long-necked sauropods outside Neosauropoda. The many species assigned to it were recovered as a non-monophyletic series in 2023, so the genus is probably in need of splitting.

## Open questions

### Is Mamenchisaurus one genus?

At least seven species have been assigned to it from different rock units and ages. A 2023 analysis found them scattered across the mamenchisaurid tree, so several may need new genus names.

### How long was the longest neck?

M. sinocanadorum is known chiefly from skull bones and a few neck vertebrae. Its estimated fifteen-metre neck depends on scaling those bones against more complete relatives.

### How was the neck held?

Biomechanical models suggest a raised but not vertical posture. Direct evidence of habitual posture is unavailable, and different models give different answers.
