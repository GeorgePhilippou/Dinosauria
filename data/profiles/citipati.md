## Overview

Citipati was a medium-sized oviraptorid dinosaur from the Late Cretaceous of Mongolia, approximately 83–72 million years ago (Campanian), found primarily at the Ukhaa Tolgod locality of the Djadochta Formation. Reaching about 2.1 metres in length, it was a feathered omnivore with a prominent bony crest on its skull and a toothless beak. Its name means 'lord of the funeral pyre' in Sanskrit, referencing a figure from Tibetan Buddhism.

Named by Clark, Norell, and Barsbold in 2001, Citipati osmolskae is one of the best-known oviraptorids thanks to multiple well-preserved specimens, including individuals found preserved in brooding posture over nests of eggs. These remarkable brooding specimens—initially misidentified as Oviraptor—confirmed that parental nest-guarding behaviour analogous to modern birds was present in non-avian dinosaurs, a key finding in dinosaur palaeontology.

## Anatomy & life

The most scientifically celebrated Citipati specimens are individuals found preserved in a brooding posture directly over clutches of eggs, with arms and body spread in a manner strikingly similar to brooding modern birds. These specimens provided some of the strongest direct evidence that non-avian theropods exhibited bird-like nesting and parental care. The posture indicates that the forelimbs were covered by feathers used to shelter the eggs, further reinforcing the close relationship between theropods and birds.

Citipati had a prominent bony crest on its skull, a feature found in various oviraptorids. The crest may have been used in display behaviour or species recognition. Like all oviraptorids, it was toothless with a parrot-like beak, and its forelimbs were long with three-clawed hands. Its overall body plan was bird-like, with a short tail ending in a pygostyle-like structure, and it was almost certainly feathered.

## Fossil record

Citipati osmolskae was described in 2001 by James Clark, Mark Norell, and Rinchen Barsbold from specimens collected during American Museum of Natural History expeditions to the Gobi Desert in the 1990s. The genus name comes from the Citipati, skeletal figures in Tibetan Buddhist iconography, while the species honours Halszka Osmólska, a distinguished Polish palaeontologist. Earlier brooding specimens from the same region had been mistakenly identified as Oviraptor.

Multiple skulls and partial to near-complete skeletons, embryos, eggs and brooding adults.

- Adult specimens preserved over nests support brooding posture, though fine behavioural details remain inferred.
- The famous specimen called Big Mama is referred to Citipati; not every crest-bearing Djadokhta oviraptorid belongs to this species.

## Where it lived

The Djadochta Formation represents a semi-arid dune environment in the Late Cretaceous Gobi Desert. Citipati shared this habitat with protoceratopsids, the small dromaeosaurid Velociraptor, the troodontid Saurornithoides, and the ankylosaur Pinacosaurus. The nesting behaviour of Citipati is interpreted as an adaptation to the variable, possibly unpredictable rainfall conditions of this desert environment.

Campanian sandstone formation in the Gobi Desert yielding the primary Citipati specimens, including brooding individuals on nests.

The Djadokhta Formation is late Campanian, broadly about 75-71 million years old.

## Open questions

- Several Djadokhta oviraptorid morphs remain unnamed or differently assigned.
- Nest posture does not reveal the complete incubation strategy.
- Diet is unresolved.
