## Overview

Barosaurus lentus was an exceptionally long-necked diplodocid sauropod from the Late Jurassic Morrison Formation (~155–150 Ma), closely related to Diplodocus but with proportionally longer neck vertebrae — the cervical series alone may have reached 9 metres. A mounted skeleton at the American Museum of Natural History in New York shows a rearing mother defending her young from an Allosaurus — the largest freestanding fossil mount in the world, though the rearing posture is biomechanically uncertain.

Whether Barosaurus could hold its neck vertically to browse high in the canopy, or was constrained to near-horizontal postures like Diplodocus, is debated. If it could rear vertically, its heart would have needed to pump blood 12–15 metres upward — requiring a heart of extraordinary size or an unusual cardiovascular system.

## Anatomy & life

A vertical neck would place the head 12–15 metres above the heart — requiring blood pressure three to four times that of a modern giraffe to maintain brain perfusion. This has led to proposals of auxiliary hearts in the neck, extremely large primary hearts, or a resting brain with minimal blood flow requirements. The issue remains unresolved and is part of the broader debate about sauropod neck posture.

Compared to Diplodocus, Barosaurus has proportionally longer individual cervical vertebrae but fewer of them — producing a similar total neck length through different vertebral architecture. The elongate individual vertebrae are heavily pneumatised (air-filled), reducing mass. This construction is analogous to engineering solutions seen in modern crane booms.

## Fossil record

Barosaurus lentus was named by Marsh in 1890 from Morrison Formation material. An African species, B. africanus, was described from Tendaguru beds, showing that diplodocids had a wider distribution across the early Gondwana-Laurasia continents than previously appreciated. The AMNH rearing mount, installed in 1991, depicts a 14-metre-tall bipedal posture that remains controversial in terms of biomechanical feasibility.

Several complementary partial skeletons, including adult and juvenile material; skull remains are very limited.

Long neck vertebrae and diplodocid skeletal anatomy confirm its identity; its range documents the cosmopolitan distribution of diplodocids.

- The type specimen was founded on a series of tail vertebrae; better skeletons were referred later.
- The famous rearing museum mount is a biomechanical reconstruction, not a preserved pose or directly observed behaviour.

## Where it lived

Late Jurassic floodplains of North America and coastal environments of East Africa.

Secure material is Kimmeridgian-Tithonian within the Morrison Formation.

## Open questions

- Some referred specimens may not belong to the type species.
- The largest reported cervical vertebrae have uncertain identification.
