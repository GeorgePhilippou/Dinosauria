## Overview

Barosaurus was a long-necked sauropod from the Late Jurassic of western North America, a close relative of Diplodocus. Its neck was proportionally longer and its tail shorter than in Diplodocus, making it one of the longest-necked animals of its time.

O. C. Marsh named it in 1890 from a skeleton found in the Black Hills of South Dakota. It is best known to the public from the mount in the American Museum of Natural History in New York, which shows an adult rearing on its hind legs to protect a juvenile from Allosaurus.

## Anatomy & life

Barosaurus had the typical diplodocid build: a small head, peg-like teeth, a very long neck and tail and relatively short legs. Individual neck vertebrae were up to about a metre long, and the neck probably reached around 8 to 9 metres. The tail was shorter than in Diplodocus, with fewer and shorter vertebrae.

How it held its neck is debated. Models of the neck joints suggest it was carried roughly horizontally and swept from side to side to feed over a wide area, while other studies argue that sauropod necks were normally raised. Whether an animal of this size could rear up on its hind legs, as the New York mount shows, is also uncertain.

- One of the longest necks among Jurassic sauropods.
- A shorter tail than its relative Diplodocus.
- The subject of the celebrated rearing mount in New York.

## Fossil record

The holotype consists mainly of vertebrae from the back, hips and tail with some limb bones. Better material was found later, especially at the Carnegie Quarry in Utah, including a skeleton with a long series of neck vertebrae now at the American Museum of Natural History. Skull material is almost unknown, so the head is reconstructed from Diplodocus.

A species from Tendaguru in Tanzania was once named Barosaurus africanus, which suggested the genus lived in Africa as well. In 2006 that material was restudied and moved to a separate genus, Tornieria, so Barosaurus is now known only from North America.

## Where it lived

The Morrison Formation covers a vast area of the western USA and records seasonally dry river plains with conifers, ferns, cycads and ginkgoes. Barosaurus shared this environment with several other sauropods, including Apatosaurus, Diplodocus, Camarasaurus and Brachiosaurus, as well as predators such as Allosaurus and Ceratosaurus. Differences in neck length and head height are thought to have helped these giants divide the available plants.

## Classification

Barosaurus is a diplodocine diplodocid, closely related to Diplodocus, Kaatedocus and Supersaurus. A large specimen-by-specimen study in 2015 confirmed it as a distinct genus and clarified which skeletons belong to it.

## Open questions

### How did it hold its neck?

Estimates range from a mostly horizontal posture to a more raised one, with consequences for how and where it fed.

### Could it rear up?

The famous mount shows it on its hind legs, but whether such a heavy animal could do this, even briefly, is debated.
