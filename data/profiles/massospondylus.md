## Overview

Massospondylus was a large herbivorous to omnivorous sauropodomorph dinosaur that lived during the Early Jurassic, approximately 208–204 million years ago, in what is now South Africa, Lesotho, and Zimbabwe. Estimated at around 4 metres in length, it moved on two or four legs depending on activity, with rounded front teeth and flat back teeth suited to processing tough plant material. It also swallowed stones (gastroliths) to help grind food in its stomach. The type species is Massospondylus carinatus.

Named by Richard Owen in 1854 from material collected in South Africa, Massospondylus is one of the most completely known early sauropodomorphs, with abundant material including juveniles, embryos, and adults. Embryos discovered in 1978 from nests in the Elliot Formation represent the oldest known dinosaur embryos and provide direct evidence of parental nesting behaviour in early dinosaurs, making Massospondylus one of the most scientifically important Early Jurassic dinosaurs.

## Anatomy & life

Massospondylus was a lightly built sauropodomorph with a long neck, small head and a long tail, typically four to six metres long as an adult. It was among the most common large animals of Early Jurassic southern Africa, and its remains are found across the upper Elliot and Clarens formations.

Its skull has been described in detail from CT scans of several specimens. The leaf-shaped, coarsely serrated teeth suit a plant diet, though some researchers allow for occasional animal food. The hands carried an enlarged thumb claw whose use is debated.

Nests with clutches of eggs containing embryos, together with hatchlings, juveniles and adults, give a growth series that few dinosaurs can match. Bone histology shows growth rates varied markedly between individuals, and biomechanical studies favour bipedal walking in adults.

- A complete growth series from embryo to adult is known, including the oldest dinosaur embryos yet described.
- Cranial anatomy is established from multiple skulls studied by CT scanning.
- Adults are interpreted as habitually bipedal on current biomechanical evidence.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Coarsely serrated leaf-shaped teeth and jaw form point to herbivory; occasional omnivory has been suggested but not demonstrated.

**Movement: Bipedal.** Adult limb proportions and biomechanical models favour two-legged walking; hatchlings had different proportions and their gait is debated.

**Appearance: Skeleton well established.** Proportions come straight from complete skeletons, but skin, colour and any external ornament are not preserved.

## Fossil record

Massospondylus is one of the most completely known early dinosaurs. Beyond the articulated neotype skeleton, the sample includes many partial and complete skeletons, multiple skulls and the eggs, embryos and hatchlings from Rooidraai.

Complete and partial skulls, entire vertebral series, both girdles, complete fore- and hindlimbs with hands and feet, eggs with in-ovo embryos, and hatchling and juvenile skeletons at several growth stages.

The 1854 syntypes in London were destroyed in the Second World War; the neotype BP/1/4934 now anchors the name.

## Where it lived

Numerous specimens of Massospondylus carinatus, including nests with embryos and juveniles, have been recovered from these Early Jurassic redbeds.

## Classification

Massospondylus is the name-bearing genus of Massospondylidae, a family of early sauropodomorphs that includes Coloradisaurus, Lufengosaurus and Adeopapposaurus. It lies well outside Sauropoda, among the non-sauropod sauropodomorphs formerly called prosauropods.

## Open questions

### How many species are there?

Several species have been named from southern Africa. Most are now sunk into M. carinatus or reassigned, but the synonymy is not fully settled.

### Did adults care for the young?

Nests, embryos and hatchling tracks show nesting sites were used, but prolonged parental care is inferred rather than demonstrated.

### How did hatchlings move?

Embryos have proportionally long forelimbs, and whether the youngest animals walked on four legs before shifting to two remains debated.
