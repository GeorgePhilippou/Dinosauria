## Overview

Saltasaurus loricatus was a relatively small (12-metre) titanosaur from the Late Cretaceous of northwestern Argentina (~70 Ma), remarkable for preserving direct evidence of titanosaur dermal armour: small bony nodules (osteoderms) embedded in the skin, including larger oval scutes up to 10 cm across. This was the first unambiguous evidence that sauropods could bear bony armour — previously considered impossible given their size and posture.

Saltasaurus is known from a nesting site (Auca Mahuevo) with hundreds of eggs preserved, some containing embryos and even embryonic skin impressions. This extraordinary site provides direct evidence of colonial nesting in titanosaurs, with many females laying eggs in the same area across multiple seasons.

## Anatomy & life

Saltasaurus was a titanosaur of modest size, probably between eight and twelve metres long depending on how the disarticulated bones are scaled. It had the wide-bodied, short-necked build typical of the most derived titanosaurs, with stout limbs and a broad, barrel-shaped trunk.

Its skin carried bone. Large oval plates up to about twelve centimetres across, together with many small ossicles, were found with the skeletons at El Brete, and their microstructure confirms they grew within the skin as dermal armour. How they were arranged over the body is not preserved, so restorations that show neat rows are guesses.

Like other titanosaurs it had a small head with narrow, peg-like teeth suited to cropping rather than chewing plants. Nearby fossil eggs and nesting sites in Patagonia are often linked to saltasaurines, but no egg has been tied directly to Saltasaurus itself.

- The first sauropod with directly documented dermal armour, changing how all titanosaurs were reconstructed.
- A comparatively small, robust titanosaur with a short neck and wide stance.
- Known from at least five individuals whose bones were mixed together at one site.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Narrow, peg-like teeth and the sauropod body plan point to bulk cropping of vegetation. The specific plants eaten are not preserved.

**Movement: Quadrupedal.** Limb bones, the sacrum and pelvis show the wide-gauge, four-legged stance typical of derived titanosaurs.

**Appearance: Armoured skin confirmed.** Osteoderms are preserved and histologically verified as dermal bone, but their pattern on the body, and all colour, is reconstructed.

## Fossil record

Saltasaurus is known from a bonebed at El Brete that yielded disarticulated remains of at least five individuals, together with osteoderms. Collectively the sample covers most of the skeleton, but no single animal is complete.

Braincase fragments and teeth, cervical, dorsal, sacral and caudal vertebrae, ribs, the pelvis and shoulder girdle, forelimb and hindlimb bones, and numerous dermal plates and ossicles.

Because the material comes from several mixed individuals, reconstructions are composites and features such as the osteoderm arrangement cannot be read directly from the fossils.

Bony armour plates embedded in skin are preserved — the first confirmed armoured sauropod — alongside typical titanosaur postcranial bones.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous of northwestern Argentina, in floodplain environments with titanosaur nesting colonies.

## Classification

Saltasaurus is the name-bearing genus of Saltasauridae and Saltasaurinae, a group of small, stocky, armoured titanosaurs from the latest Cretaceous of South America. Its close relationship with Neuquensaurus is well supported; broader titanosaur relationships remain unstable between analyses.

## Open questions

### How was the armour arranged?

The plates and ossicles were found loose. Whether the large plates formed rows, a scattered mosaic or something else is not recorded by the fossils.

### Which bones belong together?

El Brete preserves several individuals mixed together, so the composite skeleton cannot be checked against a single articulated animal.

### Did it lay the Patagonian eggs?

Titanosaur nesting grounds in Argentina are often attributed to saltasaurines, but no embryo has been identified as Saltasaurus.
