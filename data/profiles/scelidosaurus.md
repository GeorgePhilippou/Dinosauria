## Overview

Scelidosaurus harrisonii is one of the earliest armoured dinosaurs — an Early Jurassic ornithischian from the Charmouth Mudstone Formation of Dorset, England (~193 Ma). At about four metres, it bore rows of bony scutes (osteoderms) embedded in the skin along its back — the most complete armoured dinosaur from the Early Jurassic. Its exact position as either an early thyreophoran (Stegosauria/Ankylosauria ancestor) or a basal ornithischian outside Thyreophora is debated.

Scelidosaurus is historically significant as the first complete dinosaur skeleton to be described in scientific detail, predating the complete Iguanodon material from Belgium. Richard Owen described it in 1861 from marine sediment — the animal had drifted out to sea after death and been preserved in limestone nodules on the Jurassic coast.

## Anatomy & life

Scelidosaurus was a heavily built, low-slung ornithischian about four metres long, with a small head, a long tail and a body walled with rows of bony plates. Its fossils come from the marine mudstones of Charmouth, where carcasses washed out to sea and settled in fine sediment that preserved several skeletons in articulation.

The skull carried a horny beak and rows of leaf-shaped teeth suited to cropping and simple slicing of foliage rather than grinding. The jaw joint sat level with the tooth rows and the wear on the teeth is modest, suggesting a simple up-and-down bite on low-growing plants.

Rows of keeled osteoderms ran along the neck, back, flanks and tail, with a distinctive three-pointed cluster behind each ear, all preserved in place on the best skeletons. The limbs were sturdy and the forelimb relatively long, indicating an animal that habitually walked on all fours, although young animals may have been more lightly built.

- Rows of keeled bony plates are preserved in their natural positions along the neck, body and tail.
- A three-pronged cluster of osteoderms behind each side of the skull is unique to Scelidosaurus.
- Sturdy limbs and a long forelimb indicate a habitual four-legged walker, the earliest well-documented example among ornithischians.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Leaf-shaped teeth and a beak indicate browsing on low vegetation. The particular plants are not preserved and there is no grinding dentition.

**Movement: Quadrupedal walker.** Forelimb length and robust limb bones show weight-bearing on all four legs. A bipedal stance in juveniles is inferred rather than observed.

**Appearance: Armour layout well supported.** The osteoderms and small skin ossicles are preserved in position, but the keratin sheaths, skin colour and pattern are not.

## Fossil record

Scelidosaurus is known from a near-complete lectotype skull and skeleton, a second near-complete adult in Bristol, a well-preserved private specimen found in 2000, and partial juveniles, all from the Charmouth Mudstone of Dorset.

The lectotype preserves the skull with lower jaws, the complete vertebral column, both girdles, all four limbs and the osteoderms in place. The Bristol skeleton and the Charmouth specimen repeat most of this anatomy, and juvenile material in Cambridge and London records earlier growth stages.

All material comes from one formation on one stretch of coast, so geographic range and variation through time cannot be assessed.

Rows of scutes along the back and primitive thyreophoran skeletal features document its position near the base of the armoured dinosaur radiation.

## Where it lived

Early Jurassic nearshore marine environment — the Dorset specimens were apparently washed out to sea after death.

## Classification

Scelidosaurus is an early thyreophoran, the armoured group that later gave rise to stegosaurs and ankylosaurs. The recent redescription places it near the base of Thyreophora, outside both later groups, rather than within Ankylosauria as some earlier analyses suggested.

## Open questions

### Which type specimen counts?

Owen first attached the name to a theropod knee joint, and a skull-and-skeleton lectotype had to be designated in 1994 so that the name applies to the armoured animal we recognise today.

### Ankylosaur or something earlier?

Scelidosaurus was long treated as a primitive ankylosaur. The full redescription argues for a position near the base of Thyreophora, but the placement still depends on which characters are scored.

### Were the plates covered in horn?

The bone cores of the osteoderms are preserved, and a keratin sheath is likely by comparison with living reptiles, but the covering itself has not been found.
