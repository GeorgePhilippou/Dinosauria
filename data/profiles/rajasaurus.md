## Overview

Rajasaurus narmadensis ("Narmada river king lizard") was an abelisaurid theropod from the Late Cretaceous Lameta Formation of Gujarat, India (~70 Ma). Known from a near-complete skull and much of the postcranium, it is one of the best-documented large predatory dinosaurs from India and provides important data on the Late Cretaceous predator fauna of the Indian subcontinent as it drifted northward toward Asia.

The Indian subcontinent was isolated throughout most of the Cretaceous, separated from Gondwana while sharing abelisaurid theropods with Africa and Madagascar. Rajasaurus shows that abelisaurids were established in India before the continent's collision with Asia, and its close relationships to African and Malagasy forms reflect a shared Gondwanan heritage.

## Anatomy & life

The skull bears a low horn above the orbits — a single rounded projection distinguishing Rajasaurus from horned Carnotaurus or domed Majungasaurus. The braincase anatomy shows affinities to both African and South American abelisaurids. Hindlimb proportions suggest a robust, not especially fast, predator.

The Lameta Formation dates to the very end of the Cretaceous. India was a large island continent moving rapidly northward across the Tethys. Its fauna included titanosaur sauropods (Isisaurus, Jainosaurus) and abelisaurid predators like Rajasaurus, with no evidence of tyrannosaurs, hadrosaurs or ceratopsians — groups that characterised other Late Cretaceous continents.

## Fossil record

Fragments of a large theropod from the Narmada River valley were known for decades; Wilson, Sereno and colleagues formally described Rajasaurus narmadensis in 2003 from an associated skull, braincase, forelimb and hindlimb. The braincase is exceptionally complete and allows detailed comparisons with other abelisaurids.

Associated partial cranial and postcranial material including braincase, vertebrae, pelvis and hindlimb.

Abelisaurid skull features including the short deep skull and distinctive nasal horn confirm its identity; the Indian occurrence is biogeographically significant.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous of the Indian subcontinent, then a rapidly drifting island continent approaching Asia.
