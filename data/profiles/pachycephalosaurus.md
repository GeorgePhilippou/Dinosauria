## Overview

Pachycephalosaurus wyomingensis ("thick-headed lizard") was the largest pachycephalosaur — a dome-headed ornithischian from the Hell Creek Formation of the latest Cretaceous North America (~68–66 Ma) reaching about five metres. Its skull roof is a dome of solid bone up to 25 cm thick, reinforced internally by struts and surrounded by a ring of bony knobs and spikes. Whether this dome was used for head-butting combat or lateral flank-pushing is actively debated.

Pachycephalosaurus is relatively rare in the Hell Creek Formation and known mainly from skulls — the extremely dense dome bone preserves well while the rest of the skeleton, being more fragile, rarely survives. It represents the culmination of the pachycephalosaur lineage that originated in the early Late Cretaceous and diversified across North America and Asia.

## Anatomy & life

Pachycephalosaurus was a two-legged ornithischian from the final two million years of the Cretaceous, living alongside Tyrannosaurus, Triceratops and Edmontosaurus on the floodplains of western North America. It is the largest known pachycephalosaur, with skulls approaching 60 centimetres in length; body length estimates of around four and a half metres rest on scaling the skull against smaller relatives.

Its defining feature is a solid dome of bone on the skull roof, up to about 25 centimetres thick, ringed by bony knobs and, at the back of the skull, short spikes. Small, leaf-shaped cheek teeth suggest a diet of soft plants, though the front of the jaw carried more pointed teeth and an omnivorous diet has been proposed.

Growth studies of dozens of skull roofs suggest the dome only formed late in life. Younger animals had flat skulls and long spikes, and the animals once named Dracorex and Stygimoloch are now widely, though not universally, regarded as juvenile and subadult Pachycephalosaurus.

- A thickened skull dome up to about 25 cm deep, surrounded by knobs and short spikes on the squamosal bones.
- Skull roofs from many individuals record a growth series from flat-headed juveniles to fully domed adults.
- Small, serrated cheek teeth and pointed front teeth leave the exact diet open; postcranial bones are rarely preserved.

**Feeding: Plant-eater, possibly omnivore.** Leaf-shaped cheek teeth support a plant diet; heterodont front teeth have prompted suggestions of omnivory. No gut contents are known.

**Movement: Bipedal.** Pachycephalosaur postcrania from better-known genera such as Stegoceras and Homalocephale are bipedal; Pachycephalosaurus itself has little limb material.

**Appearance: Skull well known.** Dome, knobs and spikes are directly preserved; body shape, skin and colour follow other pachycephalosaurs.

## Fossil record

Pachycephalosaurus is documented by dozens of thick domes and partial skulls from the Lance and Hell Creek formations, spanning juveniles to old adults. Associated bones from the rest of the body are rare, so the animal below the head is largely reconstructed from smaller relatives.

Numerous frontoparietal domes and skull roofs, several nearly complete skulls with jaws and teeth, and a small amount of associated postcranial material from one partial skeleton.

Because most specimens are isolated domes, growth and pathology are well studied while body proportions, limb anatomy and mass remain poorly constrained.

Thick skull domes and cranial ornamentation are the key evidence for its identity and behaviour debates.

## Where it lived

Latest Cretaceous western North American floodplains.

## Classification

Pachycephalosaurus gives its name to Pachycephalosauria and Pachycephalosauridae, the dome-headed ornithischians allied to the horned dinosaurs within Marginocephalia. Whether Dracorex and Stygimoloch are growth stages of Pachycephalosaurus or distinct genera remains the main taxonomic question.

## Open questions

### What was the dome for?

Head-to-head or flank butting is inferred from dome shape, neck anatomy and healed skull lesions found on many domes, but no behaviour was ever observed and display remains a live alternative.

### Are Dracorex and Stygimoloch the same animal?

Bone histology and skull shape support a single growth series, yet some workers still treat Stygimoloch as distinct. The evidence is skull-only and sample sizes are small.

### What did the rest of the body look like?

Nearly all fossils are skull roofs. Limb, hip and tail anatomy are reconstructed from smaller relatives, so size estimates could shift if an associated skeleton is fully described.
