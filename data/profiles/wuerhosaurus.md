## Overview

Wuerhosaurus was a plated dinosaur from the Early Cretaceous of northern China. Stegosaurs were widespread and varied in the Late Jurassic but became rare afterwards, so Wuerhosaurus is one of the youngest members of the group known anywhere.

Dong Zhiming named it in 1973 from fossils found near the town of Wuerho in the Junggar Basin of Xinjiang. A second species, W. ordosensis, was added in 1993 from the Ordos Basin of Inner Mongolia.

## Anatomy & life

Wuerhosaurus was a large, four-legged plant-eater with a small head, short forelimbs, a high hip region and plates along its back. Its plates were originally described as unusually long and low, but the known plates are incomplete, and their true shape may have been taller, like those of other stegosaurs.

Like other stegosaurs, it probably fed on low-growing plants and defended itself with the spikes on its tail, although tail spikes have not been found with Wuerhosaurus itself. The function of the back plates, whether display, temperature control or both, is debated for all stegosaurs.

- One of the last stegosaurs, surviving into the Early Cretaceous.
- Back plates originally described as low and long, though known only from broken pieces.
- Two named species, from Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia.

## Fossil record

The type species is known from one incomplete skeleton, which includes the hips, some vertebrae, limb bones and two broken plates. The second species is based on a smaller articulated section of the backbone and hips. No skull is known for either.

Because the material is limited, estimates of its length are approximate, and no reliable body-mass estimate exists.

## Where it lived

The Tugulu Group of the Junggar Basin consists of Early Cretaceous river and lake deposits that have also produced pterosaurs such as Dsungaripterus, turtles and crocodile relatives. The exact age of the beds that yielded Wuerhosaurus is not well constrained within the Early Cretaceous.

## Classification

Wuerhosaurus is a stegosaurid. In 2008 Maidment and colleagues argued that its anatomy was so close to Stegosaurus that it should be renamed Stegosaurus homheni. Most other researchers have kept Wuerhosaurus as a separate genus, partly because of its much younger age, and the question remains open.

## Open questions

### Is it really a separate genus from Stegosaurus?

A 2008 review placed it within Stegosaurus, but this has not been widely followed. More complete material would help decide.

### What shape were its plates?

The known plates are broken, so their original height and outline are uncertain.

### How old is it exactly?

The beds are Early Cretaceous, but their precise stage and numerical age have not been pinned down.
