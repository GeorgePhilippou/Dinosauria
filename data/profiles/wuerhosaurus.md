## Overview

Wuerhosaurus homheni was a large stegosaur from the Early Cretaceous Tugulu Group of Xinjiang, China (~130–120 Ma), notable as one of the last known stegosaurs — most of the family disappeared at the end of the Jurassic, making Wuerhosaurus a rare Cretaceous survivor of this lineage. Its plates are distinctive: low and wide rather than the tall triangular plates of Stegosaurus.

The wide, horizontal plates of Wuerhosaurus have been interpreted as thermoregulatory structures (like a solar panel) rather than display organs, since their low profile reduces visual impact. However, the function of stegosaur plates in all species remains debated, and thermoregulation, display and defence have all been proposed.

## Anatomy & life

Unlike the tall pointed or rounded plates of Stegosaurus, Wuerhosaurus plates are described as low and broad with a rounded dorsal margin. This unusual shape may reflect an ecological or thermoregulatory specialisation, though the plates of all stegosaurs are poorly understood functionally. The plates are embedded in skin rather than fused to the skeleton, as in all stegosaurs.

Asia was a major centre of stegosaur diversity in the Jurassic: Tuojiangosaurus, Huayangosaurus and Chungkingosaurus from China, plus the Indian Dravidosaurus (controversial), represent a rich Asian stegosaur fauna. Wuerhosaurus is thus part of a long Asian stegosaur tradition. Its persistence into the Cretaceous in Asia may reflect the relatively late fauna turnover in this region compared to North America and Europe.

## Fossil record

Dong Zhiming described Wuerhosaurus homheni in 1973 from Xinjiang material. Its Early Cretaceous age makes it one of the latest-surviving stegosaurs — most of the family disappears from the fossil record at the Jurassic-Cretaceous boundary (~145 Ma). Wuerhosaurus survived into the Barremian, some 15–20 million years after most of its relatives vanished, as an apparent Cretaceous relict.

Fragmentary postcranial skeletons and isolated plates; skull absent.

Stegosaur plates and postcranial anatomy confirm its identity; its Early Cretaceous age makes it one of the last known stegosaurs.

## Where it lived

Early Cretaceous of central Asia — a rare survival of the stegosaur lineage into the Cretaceous.

## Open questions

- Species composition, formation age and plate shape remain incompletely known.
