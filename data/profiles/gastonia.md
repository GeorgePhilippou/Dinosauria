## Overview

Gastonia was a heavily armoured nodosaurid dinosaur from the Early Cretaceous of Utah, USA, living approximately 137–100 million years ago. Known from a skull and the partial skeletons of multiple individuals — an unusually rich sample for an armoured dinosaur — Gastonia burgei was characterised by an impressive array of lateral spines projecting from its flanks and shoulders, as well as dense bony osteoderms covering the back. Estimates of its length vary, but it was a medium-to-large animal, broadly comparable in build to other nodosaurids.

Described by James Kirkland in 1998 from the Cedar Mountain Formation, Gastonia is placed within Nodosauridae. The material was recovered from the Yellow Cat Member of the Cedar Mountain Formation in Utah, a deposit famous for also yielding the large predatory dromaeosaurid Utahraptor. This co-occurrence has sparked popular speculation about predator-prey dynamics between Gastonia and Utahraptor, though direct evidence of interaction is lacking.

## Anatomy & life

Gastonia was one of the most heavily armed nodosaurids known, bearing large lateral spines projecting outward from the flanks and shoulders, supplemented by rows of smaller osteoderms along the back. These armament configurations would have made it a formidable target for predators. Unlike ankylosaurids, Gastonia and other nodosaurids lacked a tail club, making the lateral spines a primary passive defence.

The Yellow Cat Member that yielded Gastonia also produced remains of Utahraptor — the largest known dromaeosaurid, measuring up to 6–7 metres. Some popular accounts have framed Gastonia as a natural prey animal for Utahraptor, and this predator-prey pairing has featured in popular science. While both animals certainly inhabited the same region, direct evidence of predation is currently absent from the fossil record.

## Fossil record

Gastonia burgei was described by James Kirkland in 1998 from material collected in the Yellow Cat Member of the Cedar Mountain Formation, Utah. The genus honours Robert Gaston, a preparator whose extensive work on the specimens helped bring them to scientific attention. The species name honours Donald Burge, director of the College of Eastern Utah Prehistoric Museum, which houses some of the material.

The recovery of multiple individuals of Gastonia from the same locality is rare among armoured dinosaurs and has allowed comparative anatomical study. The assemblage includes juvenile and adult elements, providing some information about ontogenetic variation. Such bonebed or multi-specimen deposits are scientifically valuable for population-level analyses that single skeletons cannot provide.

Abundant multi-individual cranial, postcranial and armour material.

- The record is a multi-individual assemblage, not one complete skeleton.
- The absence of a tail club and presence of a sacral shield fit a polacanthine nodosaurid interpretation.
- Exact species assignments within the broader Gastonia material have been revised.

## Where it lived

Primary locality yielding both skull and multiple partial skeletal specimens of Gastonia, alongside the large dromaeosaurid Utahraptor.

Age follows the Yellow Cat Member and remains approximate because Cedar Mountain member chronology has been repeatedly revised.

## Open questions

- The bonebed is disarticulated and armour placement is partly inferential.
- The precise age and boundaries of the Yellow Cat Member vary among studies.
