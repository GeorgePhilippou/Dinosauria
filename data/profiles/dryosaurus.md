## Overview

Dryosaurus altus was a medium-sized ornithopod from the Late Jurassic Morrison Formation (~155–150 Ma), about three to four metres long. A nimble biped with no teeth at the front of the jaw (replaced by a horny beak) and ridged cheek teeth for precise cropping, it occupied the small-to-medium herbivore niche in the Morrison alongside the larger Camptosaurus.

A closely related species or genus, Dysalotosaurus (from the Tendaguru Formation of Tanzania), shows the cosmopolitan distribution of dryosaurids across Jurassic landmasses. The similarities between Morrison and Tendaguru faunas extend to both predators (Allosaurus / Yangchuanosaurus relatives) and herbivores (Dryosaurus / Dysalotosaurus), suggesting faunal exchange between North America and Africa during the Jurassic.

## Anatomy & life

Dryosaurus had a toothless premaxilla (beaked front of the mouth) and self-sharpening ridged cheek teeth. The hindlimbs were long and slender for fast running; the forelimbs were short with five fingers. It could not walk quadrupedally as Camptosaurus could. The stiff tail provided balance during bipedal locomotion.

The Tanzanian Dysalotosaurus — formerly classified within Dryosaurus — is very similar to the North American species, reflecting the faunal connections between Late Jurassic North America and Africa. These similarities across the proto-Atlantic suggest either island-hopping dispersal or a land bridge connection maintained into the Late Jurassic.

## Fossil record

Named by Marsh in 1894 from Morrison Formation material, Dryosaurus ("oak lizard") was one of the smaller common herbivores in the Morrison fauna. Juveniles are known from several sites, allowing study of growth patterns. Adult specimens reach 3–4 metres; younger individuals show proportionally larger eyes and shorter snouts, consistent with the general ornithopod juvenile pattern.

Multiple partial skeletons across growth stages with limited skull material.

Long limb bones and a lightweight skeleton confirm a cursorial herbivore lifestyle typical of small basal ornithopods.

## Where it lived

Late Jurassic Morrison floodplain ecosystem of western North America.

## Open questions

- Skull anatomy and species-level referral of several Morrison specimens remain incomplete.
- Maximum adult size and exact dryosaurid relationships vary.
