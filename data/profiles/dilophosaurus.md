## Overview

Dilophosaurus was a large Early Jurassic theropod from Arizona, about six metres long, bearing two thin parallel bony crests along the skull roof. These were display structures — far too fragile for combat — and Dilophosaurus was one of the largest terrestrial predators of its time at ~193 million years ago.

The Jurassic Park portrayal of Dilophosaurus with an expandable neck frill and venom is entirely fictional; no fossil evidence supports either feature. The notch in its upper jaw between the premaxilla and maxilla once led to scavenging hypotheses, since largely refuted by biomechanical analysis.

## Anatomy & life

Dilophosaurus was a long-bodied, lightly built predator about six to seven metres long, with a long neck, a low skull carrying a pair of thin bony crests, and hands with four fingers. It is the largest land animal known from the Kayenta Formation and among the largest theropods of the Early Jurassic anywhere.

Older reconstructions showed a weak-jawed animal with a notch beneath the nostrils, sometimes interpreted as a sign of scavenging. Re-preparation of the type material and study of a fourth skeleton showed that the jaws and skull were more robust than once thought, and that the crests were supported by extensions of the nasal and lacrimal bones and hollowed out by air spaces linked to the sinuses.

The animal in the Jurassic Park films bears its name but little else. There is no fossil evidence for venom, a neck frill or a small body; the real Dilophosaurus was a substantial hunter whose crests were probably visual structures, though their exact function cannot be tested from bones alone.

- Two thin, plate-like crests rose from the top of the snout, formed by the nasal and lacrimal bones and pierced by air spaces.
- A long neck, slender tail and four-fingered hands place Dilophosaurus among early neotheropods rather than later, more compact predators.
- The holotype skeleton preserves healed injuries on several bones, showing that the animal survived serious damage during life.

**Feeding: Meat-eater.** Blade-like, serrated teeth and a large gape indicate a predator, but no gut contents or bite marks tie it to particular prey.

**Movement: Bipedal.** Long hindlimbs and short forelimbs show habitual two-legged walking. Tracks from the region have been linked to similar theropods, but none can be assigned to Dilophosaurus with certainty.

**Appearance: Skeletal outline well supported.** The crests, skull shape and proportions are known from bone; colour, skin covering and any soft extension of the crests are not preserved.

## Fossil record

Dilophosaurus is known from a small number of partial skeletons collected from the Kayenta Formation of northern Arizona since 1942, including the holotype and a second skeleton found together. The 2020 revision re-described all of the material and added a further specimen.

The holotype preserves much of the skull and lower jaw together with most of the vertebral column, girdles and limbs. A second skeleton found beside it, a larger crested individual collected in 1964, and a fourth skeleton in the Texas collections add overlapping cranial and postcranial material.

Different specimens are complete in different regions, so the composite skeleton is well understood even though every individual has gaps. Earlier restoration in plaster complicated study of the type skull.

Paired skull crests and theropod skeletal anatomy distinguish it from later movie-inspired reconstructions.

## Where it lived

Early Jurassic fluvial and floodplain environments in western North America.

## Classification

Dilophosaurus is an early neotheropod. It was long grouped with Coelophysis and its relatives, but the 2020 revision by Marsh and Rowe recovered it closer to the later, larger theropods of the Middle and Late Jurassic. Its exact position remains sensitive to analysis.

## Open questions

### What were the crests for?

They were thin and probably served for display or recognition, but no fossil evidence tests these ideas directly and any soft-tissue extension is unknown.

### Where does it sit among early theropods?

Analyses disagree on whether Dilophosaurus is close to Coelophysis or nearer the ancestry of later large theropods. The 2020 revision favours the latter.

### How much of the skull is original?

The holotype skull was partly reconstructed in plaster for decades. Modern re-preparation separated bone from restoration, but some regions remain distorted.
