## Overview

Ouranosaurus nigeriensis was a large ornithopod from the Early Cretaceous of Niger (~112 Ma), bearing a remarkable sail or hump along its back formed by elongated neural spines up to 60 cm tall. Whether this stored fat like a bison's hump, acted as a thermoregulatory sail or served as a display structure is still debated. It lived in a warm, humid river-delta environment that is now the central Sahara.

As an iguanodontian closely related to Iguanodon, Ouranosaurus retains the thumb spike and similar overall body plan. The sail or hump convergently evolved in the same ecosystem as the spinosaurid Suchomimus, which also bore elevated neural spines — an intriguing case of parallel feature development in different lineages sharing the same palaeoenvironment.

## Anatomy & life

The neural spines are notably taller than those of Iguanodon but shorter than those of the spinosaurid Spinosaurus from the same general region. Hump hypotheses are supported by analogies with bison and camels; sail hypotheses draw on living monitor lizards and crested reptiles. Bone histology of the spines does not yet resolve the question. The convergence with spinosaurid spines in the same environment is notable but likely coincidental.

The Elrhaz Formation records a vast tropical river-delta ecosystem now buried under the Sahara. Ouranosaurus coexisted with the fish-specialist Suchomimus, the bizarre sauropod Nigersaurus, the giant crocodilian Sarcosuchus and abundant lungfish. This diverse fauna reveals that the region was ecologically rich during the Early Cretaceous, contrasting sharply with today's hyper-arid desert.

## Fossil record

Philippe Taquet discovered two Ouranosaurus skeletons in Niger in 1966 and described the species in 1976. The name means "brave monitor lizard" in Tuareg, honouring the people of the region. Both specimens are well preserved and provide detailed knowledge of the skull, which is broad and flat compared to Iguanodon, suggesting different feeding habits.

Two substantial partial skeletons including skulls and tall-spined axial columns.

Tall neural spines, skull and limb material document its sail-backed iguanodontian anatomy.

- Tall neural spines along the trunk and tail are directly preserved.
- Whether they supported a sail, fatty hump or another soft-tissue profile is unresolved.
- It had a dental battery and broad herbivorous feeding anatomy; exact diet and habitual gait are not directly observed.

## Where it lived

Early Cretaceous African river and floodplain deposits.

The Elrhaz Formation is broadly Aptian-Albian; the single 112 Ma value is illustrative rather than a direct date.

## Open questions

- Soft tissue over the neural spines is unknown.
- Its exact position among styracosternans varies.
