## Overview

Becklespinax was a large theropod dinosaur from the Early Cretaceous of England, approximately 139–133 million years ago (Berriasian to Valanginian), known from vertebrae with notably tall neural spines found in the Hastings Beds of East Sussex. Estimated at about 5 metres in length, it was a moderately large predator of the Wealden ecosystem. The genus is fragmentary and its exact relationships within Tetanurae remain uncertain.

Named by Gregory Paul in 1988 (with the genus formally erected in 1991 by Olshevsky) and associated with earlier material known as 'Altispinax', Becklespinax has a convoluted nomenclatural history. The tall neural spines on the dorsal vertebrae have invited comparisons with sail-backed theropods, though whether these formed a sail, hump, or muscle attachment ridge is unknown. Its taxonomic status within Tetanurae is poorly resolved due to fragmentary material.

## Anatomy & life

Becklespinax is known only from three articulated dorsal vertebrae with markedly elongated neural spines, suggesting a prominent dorsal ridge or sail-like structure in life. Without skull, limb, or pelvis material, body length estimates of around 5 metres are approximate. The tall spines are reminiscent of those in Acrocanthosaurus and some spinosaurids, but direct phylogenetic comparison is hampered by the paucity of material.

## Fossil record

The vertebrae now assigned to Becklespinax were originally part of a collection made by amateur collector Samuel Beckles from Wealden deposits of East Sussex in the 19th century. The material was long associated with the name 'Altispinax' before Gregory Paul reassigned it in 1988. The genus name Becklespinax honours Beckles and references the tall spines ('spinax' from Latin spina, spine).

## Where it lived

During the Early Cretaceous the Wealden of southern England was a broad, low-lying fluvial plain with rivers, lakes, and deltaic environments. Becklespinax shared this landscape with other theropods, ornithopods such as Iguanodon, and sauropods. It was likely an apex or near-apex predator in this system.

Lower Cretaceous Wealden fluvial and lacustrine sediments yielding theropod vertebrae attributed to Becklespinax.

## Classification

The genus has a complex history, having been variously assigned to Altispinax, to unnamed Tetanurae, and debated as potentially synonymous with other Wealden theropods. Current consensus treats Becklespinax as a valid but highly fragmentary and incertae sedis tetanuran. Future discoveries of more complete material from Wealden beds would be needed to clarify its relationships.
