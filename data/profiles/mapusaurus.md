## Overview

Mapusaurus roseae was a giant carcharodontosaurid theropod from the Cenomanian–Turonian Huincul Formation of Neuquén Province, Argentina (~99–94 Ma). Named by Coria and Currie in 2006, it reached an estimated 10–12 metres in length, making it one of the largest theropods from South America and closely comparable in size to its relative Giganotosaurus. Its name combines the Mapuche word 'mapu' (land or earth) with the Greek 'sauros' (lizard), and the species name roseae honours Rose Letwin, who helped fund the expedition that recovered the specimens.

Mapusaurus is known from a bonebed containing the disarticulated remains of at least seven to nine individuals of varying sizes and ages, excavated from the Huincul Formation between 1997 and 2001. This accumulation of multiple individuals in a single locality has been interpreted as evidence for social behaviour or group hunting, providing some of the strongest fossil evidence for gregarious behaviour in a large theropod. Whether Mapusaurus actively hunted cooperatively or was merely attracted to the same carcass or water source remains a subject of ongoing discussion.

## Anatomy & life

The Mapusaurus bonebed is one of the most compelling lines of evidence for social behaviour in large theropods. The presence of juvenile, subadult and adult individuals at a single locality suggests a multi-age group rather than a chance accumulation of unrelated animals. Coria and Currie proposed that groups of Mapusaurus may have cooperated to hunt titanosaur sauropods such as the enormous Argentinosaurus, which shared the same formation — a prey item far too large for any single individual to subdue. Critics note that bonebeds can also form through hydraulic concentration of carcasses, so the social interpretation, while plausible, is not definitive.

Mapusaurus shares the carcharodontosaurid body plan: a massive skull with blade-like serrated teeth, robust forelimbs with three-fingered hands, and powerful hindlimbs. Estimates of its total length range from 10 to 12 metres depending on the specimen and methodology, overlapping with or slightly exceeding estimates for Giganotosaurus carolinii from the same region and a slightly older time. Its skull was proportionally large relative to the body — a hallmark of carcharodontosaurids — bearing teeth adapted for slicing flesh rather than the bone-crushing function of contemporary tyrannosaurs.

## Fossil record

Fossils were first recovered from the Cañadón del Gato locality during expeditions between 1997 and 2001 led by Rodolfo Coria and Philip Currie. The bonebed contained cranial, axial and appendicular elements from multiple individuals at different growth stages, from juveniles to adults. Coria and Currie described the genus formally in 2006, noting that the co-occurrence of size-varied individuals — and evidence of feeding traces on some bones — was consistent with a social or gregarious lifestyle.

## Where it lived

The Huincul Formation represents a fluvial to floodplain depositional environment in the Neuquén Basin during the early Late Cretaceous, when South America was still largely isolated from North America. The fauna was dominated by large titanosaur sauropods and carcharodontosaurid theropods — an ecological structure very different from the tyrannosaur-dominated faunas of the northern hemisphere at the same time. Mapusaurus occupied the apex predator role in this ecosystem, alongside the spinosaurid Baryonyx relative Irritator-related forms from slightly different facies.

The Cañadón del Gato site in the Huincul Formation produced the multi-individual Mapusaurus bonebed; the same formation also yields the titanosaur sauropod Argentinosaurus, a possible prey item for large theropods.

## Open questions

- The cause and behavioural meaning of the multi-individual accumulation are unresolved.
- Maximum size relies on fragmentary large individuals.
