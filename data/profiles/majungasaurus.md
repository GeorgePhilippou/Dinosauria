## Overview

Majungasaurus crenatissimus was the apex predator of Late Cretaceous Madagascar (~70 Ma), an abelisaurid theropod about seven metres long. Bone surface marks on Majungasaurus bones that match its own tooth morphology provide the most direct fossil evidence of intraspecific cannibalism in any non-avian dinosaur — feeding on members of its own species.

Madagascar was isolated from mainland Africa by the Cretaceous, and its fauna evolved in parallel: Majungasaurus replaced tyrannosaurs (which never reached Madagascar) as top predator, and the giant titanosaur Rapetosaurus provided the largest available prey. As an abelisaurid it is related to Carnotaurus of South America and Rajasaurus of India.

## Anatomy & life

Majungasaurus bones found with tooth marks matching the distinctive ridged abelisaurid dentition — and specifically matching Majungasaurus itself — are the strongest evidence of intraspecific cannibalism in any non-avian dinosaur. Whether this was scavenging conspecific carcasses or active predation of living individuals cannot be determined from the marks alone.

Like other abelisaurids, Majungasaurus had extremely reduced forelimbs — even more vestigial than Carnotaurus — a short deep skull with a thick dome above the orbits (the feature initially leading to the "Majungatholus" genus name), and heavily textured outer skull bones. These surface textures supported a keratinous skull covering rather than simple scales.

## Fossil record

Fragmentary material was known since the 1890s, but substantial specimens — including near-complete skulls — were not recovered until the expeditions of David Krause and colleagues from the 1990s onward. The species was often referred to as Majungatholus atopus before synonymy established Majungasaurus as the valid name in 2007.

Multiple skulls and extensive postcranial material from many individuals, collectively nearly all skeletal regions.

Tooth marks matching Majungasaurus teeth on Majungasaurus bones provide rare direct evidence of cannibalism — one of only a few dinosaurs (alongside Allosaurus and some tyrannosaurids) with such direct evidence.

- Its short deep skull and reduced forelimbs are directly documented by a strong sample.
- Majungasaurus tooth marks on Majungasaurus bones provide evidence of cannibalistic feeding, though not necessarily active predation.
- Growth studies indicate slow, prolonged growth relative to many large theropods.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous semi-arid to seasonally wet environments of Madagascar, isolated from mainland Africa.

The Maevarano Formation sample is late Maastrichtian.

## Open questions

- Cannibal tooth marks do not distinguish scavenging from predation.
- Some composite skeletal proportions unite individuals.
