## Overview

Pentaceratops was a large chasmosaurine ceratopsid, a relative of Triceratops, that lived in what is now New Mexico during the late Campanian. It had one of the longest frills of any horned dinosaur, with a short nose horn and two long horns above the eyes.

Its name, "five-horned face", was given by Henry Fairfield Osborn in 1923. Only three of the "horns" are true horns; the other two are the pointed tips of the cheek bones (epijugals), which are especially long in Pentaceratops.

## Anatomy & life

The skull was very large, with a long frill whose rim carried a row of bony points. The frill is pierced by two large openings, which would have been covered by skin in life and made the structure much lighter than it looks. At the midline of the back edge there is a distinct notch.

Like other ceratopsids, Pentaceratops was a four-legged plant-eater with a sharp, parrot-like beak and rows of shearing teeth for slicing tough vegetation. Its horns and frill were probably used for display and for recognising members of the same species, and possibly in contests between rivals.

- One of the longest frills of any horned dinosaur.
- "Five horns": a nose horn, two brow horns and two long cheek points.
- Large openings that lightened the frill.

## Fossil record

The holotype is a partial skull collected in 1921 by the fossil hunter Charles H. Sternberg, after whom the species is named. Several more skulls and partial skeletons have since been found in the San Juan Basin, and Lehman's 1993 study added much new information on its skull and skeleton.

The most famous specimen, OMNH 10165, is an enormous skull that was reconstructed with an estimated length of nearly 3 metres. In 2011 Nicholas Longrich argued that it belongs to a different animal, Titanoceratops. Other researchers regard it as a large Pentaceratops, and because much of the skull is restored the question remains open.

## Where it lived

Pentaceratops lived on a coastal plain west of the Western Interior Seaway, crossed by rivers and dotted with swamps and forests. The same rocks have yielded the hadrosaurs Parasaurolophus and Kritosaurus, the ankylosaur Nodocephalosaurus, tyrannosaurids, turtles and crocodilians. Its fauna differs from that of the same age in Alberta and Montana, which is taken as evidence that dinosaurs in the north and south of western North America lived in partly separate communities.

## Classification

Pentaceratops is a chasmosaurine, the long-frilled branch of ceratopsids that includes Chasmosaurus, Anchiceratops and Triceratops. Studies of the San Juan Basin sequence suggest that it sits within a series of closely related forms that changed gradually through time, with the notch at the back of the frill becoming progressively shallower and finally closing in successive forms.

## Open questions

### Is the giant skull a Pentaceratops?

OMNH 10165 has been treated both as a very large Pentaceratops and as the separate genus Titanoceratops. How it is classified changes estimates of how big Pentaceratops grew.

### How many species were there?

Material from different levels in the San Juan Basin, and a proposed northern species from Canada, may represent one variable species or several related ones.
