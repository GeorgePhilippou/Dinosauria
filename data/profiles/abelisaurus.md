## Overview

Abelisaurus comahuensis is the namesake of Abelisauridae, the dominant predator family of Gondwana in the Late Cretaceous. Known only from a skull — described by Bonaparte and Novas in 1985 from the Anacleto Formation of Patagonia (~83–80 Ma) — it establishes the basic anatomy of the abelisaurid skull: a tall, rough-surfaced cranium with large fenestrae, a short deep profile and small teeth.

Though fragmentary compared to Carnotaurus, Abelisaurus is historically important as the taxon that anchors the family name. Its skull surface texture — rugose and pitted rather than smooth — is now known to be characteristic of abelisaurids generally and supports the inference of extensive keratinous skull covering in these animals.

## Anatomy & life

The skull is tall and short compared to allosauroids of similar size. Large antorbital and infratemporal fenestrae reduce weight. The outer surface is roughened and pitted — now recognised as a taxonomic feature of abelisaurids generally, probably supporting a keratinous rhamphotheca (beak sheath) over parts of the skull. The dentition is small relative to skull size.

Abelisauridae radiated across the southern continents during the Cretaceous. South America, Africa, Madagascar and India all yield abelisaurid remains, demonstrating that this lineage was the dominant large theropod clade of Gondwana in the Cretaceous, analogous to the tyrannosaurs of Laurasia.

## Fossil record

José Bonaparte and Fernando Novas described Abelisaurus comahuensis in 1985, the same year as Carnotaurus. The skull — about 85 cm long — is the sole known element but shows enough anatomy to establish its abelisaurid affinities and distinguish it from Carnotaurus. Its discovery confirmed that the South American Late Cretaceous hosted a diverse abelisaurid fauna.

One incomplete skull.

The skull alone was distinctive enough to diagnose the Abelisauridae family — its deep proportions and surface texture are diagnostic.

- The holotype is a partial skull lacking much of the snout and lower jaws.
- Its body proportions, size and external covering must largely be inferred from other abelisaurids.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous Patagonian terrestrial ecosystems with titanosaur sauropods and abelisaurid predators.

The Anacleto Formation is generally dated to the early Campanian.

## Open questions

- Only one specimen is securely known.
- Its detailed position within Abelisauridae is unstable.
