## Overview

Thescelosaurus ('wonderful lizard') was a small to medium-sized ornithopod dinosaur from the very end of the Late Cretaceous of western North America, living approximately 76–66 million years ago right up to the end-Cretaceous mass extinction. Reaching about 3.5 metres in length, it was a bipedal herbivore with a robust build, relatively short arms, and a beak suited for cropping vegetation. Fossils are known from the Hell Creek, Lance, and Frenchman formations of Canada and the United States.

Thescelosaurus neglectus was named by Charles W. Gilmore in 1913, and the genus is the namesake of the family Thescelosauridae. It gained considerable public and scientific attention when a specimen nicknamed 'Willo' was reported in 2000 to preserve a fossilised heart — a claim that generated much debate about dinosaur physiology, though most subsequent analyses interpreted the structure as a concretion rather than soft tissue. The genus is noteworthy for surviving until the very last days of the Mesozoic.

## Anatomy & life

A Thescelosaurus specimen known as 'Willo', discovered in South Dakota, was reported in 2000 to preserve a fossilised heart with a four-chambered structure, potentially suggesting warm-blooded physiology similar to modern birds and mammals. Subsequent re-examination, however, concluded that the structure was most likely an iron-rich concretion shaped by geological processes rather than actual cardiac tissue, illustrating the challenges of interpreting soft-tissue preservation in fossils.

Thescelosaurus was among the dinosaurs alive immediately before the Chicxulub impact 66 million years ago, with its remains found in the uppermost layers of the Hell Creek Formation. Its persistence through the final millions of years of the Cretaceous makes it valuable for understanding latest Cretaceous ecosystem dynamics in Laramidia.

## Fossil record

Thescelosaurus neglectus was described by Charles W. Gilmore in 1913, based on a nearly complete skeleton collected in 1891 that had remained unstudied for over two decades — hence the species epithet neglectus ('neglected'). Additional species have been proposed over the years, including T. garbanii (1976) and T. assiniboiensis (2011), though the taxonomy of the genus continues to be debated.

Several partial to near-complete skeletons, including skulls, across multiple species.

- The alleged fossilised four-chambered heart in one specimen is now generally regarded as an iron-rich concretion.
- Jaw and tooth anatomy support plant feeding, but occasional omnivory cannot be excluded.

## Where it lived

The richest source of Thescelosaurus material, including the 'Willo' specimen.

Secure records are Hell Creek, Lance and equivalent latest Maastrichtian formations; 76 Ma is too old.

## Classification

Thescelosaurus was a robustly built, primarily bipedal herbivore with a relatively small head, leaf-shaped teeth, and short forelimbs. Its placement within Thescelosauridae or as a basal ornithopod has varied across studies; recent phylogenetic analyses often place it within Neornithischia outside Ornithopoda proper, or within a broadly defined Thescelosauridae alongside orodrominids.

## Open questions

- Species limits and diet breadth remain under study.
