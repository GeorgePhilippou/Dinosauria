## Overview

Dromiceiomimus (meaning 'emu mimic') was a medium-sized ornithomimosaurian theropod from the Late Cretaceous of Alberta, Canada, living approximately 74–70 million years ago. Estimated at around 3.5 metres in length and about 100 kilograms, it superficially resembled a modern ostrich or emu, with long hindlimbs, a lightly built body, and a toothless beak. It is currently regarded as a nomen dubium by many researchers, meaning the material attributed to it is no longer considered to reliably diagnose a species distinct from other ornithomimids.

Dromiceiomimus brevitertius was named by Dale Russell in 1972 from the Horseshoe Canyon Formation of Alberta. Questions about its validity stem from difficulties in separating it from Ornithomimus based on available skeletal material. The taxon illustrates broader challenges in ornithomimid taxonomy, where multiple closely related species in the same formation can be difficult to distinguish without multiple well-preserved specimens.

## Anatomy & life

Many subsequent assessments have concluded that Dromiceiomimus cannot be reliably distinguished from Ornithomimus based on available diagnostic features. The NHM notes it is now widely treated as a nomen dubium. This does not mean the specimens are unimportant — they contribute to understanding ornithomimid anatomy — but the genus name itself is of uncertain validity.

Ornithomimids like Dromiceiomimus were almost certainly among the fastest non-avian dinosaurs, with long, slender hindlimbs and a lightly built frame conducive to high running speeds. Biomechanical estimates for comparable ornithomimids suggest top speeds potentially exceeding 50 km/h, though such figures carry significant uncertainty.

Ornithomimids are generally inferred to have been omnivores, using their toothless beaks to consume a variety of plant material, insects, small vertebrates, and possibly eggs. The absence of teeth and the structure of the forelimbs suggest a generalist opportunistic diet rather than specialised predation.

## Fossil record

Dromiceiomimus brevitertius was described by Dale Russell in 1972 from skeletal material in the Horseshoe Canyon Formation of Alberta. The genus name references the emu (Dromaius) of Australia, emphasising the animal's bird-like proportions, while the species epithet brevitertius refers to the proportionally short third metatarsal.

Several partial skulls and skeletons historically distributed between Dromiceiomimus and Ornithomimus.

## Where it lived

Type locality; the only confirmed occurrences of Dromiceiomimus come from this Early Maastrichtian formation.

## Open questions

- The genus-level validity and boundaries of the referred hypodigm remain disputed.
- Age range depends on which specimens are accepted.
