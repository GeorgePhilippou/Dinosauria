## Overview

Carcharodontosaurus saharicus was a massive carcharodontosaurid theropod from the Cenomanian of North Africa (~100–94 Ma), rivalling Tyrannosaurus in length and sharing its continent with Spinosaurus in a vast river-delta ecosystem. Its name — "shark-toothed lizard" — refers to its large, flattened, serrated teeth convergent with those of great white sharks.

The original specimens, described by Stromer in 1931, were destroyed in the 1944 Allied bombing of Munich. New material described by Sereno and colleagues in 1996 from Morocco provided a far more complete picture of this giant predator and confirmed it as a carcharodontosaurid distinct from South American relatives.

## Anatomy & life

Carcharodontosaurus was a very large two-legged predator of the carcharodontosaurid family, named for its blade-like, serrated teeth that reminded early describers of those of the great white shark. The Moroccan neotype skull is about 1.6 metres long, which places the animal among the largest theropods known. Whole-body estimates of twelve to thirteen metres and several tonnes are extrapolations from that skull using the proportions of relatives such as Giganotosaurus.

The skull is long and relatively narrow, with a large opening in front of the eye, thin, ornamented nasal and lacrimal bones and a lightly built braincase. CT study of the neotype braincase shows a small, elongate brain with large olfactory bulbs and a relatively small optic region, a pattern shared with other allosauroids.

It lived on the river deltas and floodplains of the Kem Kem region alongside Spinosaurus, the abelisaurids and Deltadromeus. Much of the body plan, limb proportions and any behaviour attributed to it in popular accounts come from other carcharodontosaurids, because the securely referred fossils of Carcharodontosaurus itself are almost entirely cranial.

- Blade-like teeth with wrinkled enamel bands across the crown, a feature shared by the family that bears its name.
- A large, lightly built skull with a long snout, thin skull roof bones and a small braincase, all directly seen in the neotype.
- A body reconstructed from relatives: postcranial anatomy is not securely known for the genus after the 2025 removal of the Egyptian specimen.

**Feeding: Meat-eater.** Serrated, laterally compressed teeth and a slicing skull architecture indicate a predator of large animals. Prey is inferred from the Kem Kem fauna; no gut contents or bite-marked bones are securely attributed.

**Movement: Bipedal.** No secure hindlimb of C. saharicus is known. Bipedal posture follows every other large theropod and the closely related Giganotosaurus.

**Appearance: Skull outline supported.** Skull shape is known from the neotype. Body proportions, skin texture and colour are borrowed from better-known carcharodontosaurids.

## Fossil record

Carcharodontosaurus saharicus has an unusually tangled evidence base. The original Algerian teeth are lost, the Egyptian skeleton described in 1931 was destroyed in the Second World War and has since been reassigned to Tameryraptor, and the name is stabilised by a partial Moroccan skull designated as neotype in 1996.

The neotype SGM-Din 1 preserves much of the skull, including the maxilla, nasal, lacrimal, postorbital, jugal, part of the skull roof and a braincase, with teeth. Isolated teeth and a few bones from the Kem Kem beds and elsewhere have been referred to the species with varying confidence.

Postcranial features once attributed to Carcharodontosaurus came from the destroyed Bahariya specimen. After its separation as Tameryraptor markgrafi in 2025, those details should no longer be presented as C. saharicus anatomy.

Large blade-like serrated teeth and skull material define its carcharodontosaurid identity.

## Where it lived

Cretaceous North African river and coastal systems with giant theropods, sauropods and abundant fish.

## Classification

Carcharodontosaurus is the name-bearing genus of Carcharodontosauridae, a family of large allosauroid theropods that also includes Giganotosaurus, Mapusaurus and Acrocanthosaurus. Its closest relatives are the South American giganotosaurines; the second species, C. iguidensis from Niger, may not belong in the genus.

## Open questions

### How much of the classic skeleton was really Carcharodontosaurus?

The Egyptian skull and partial skeleton described by Stromer in 1931 were destroyed in 1944. Their re-evaluation from Stromer's records led to their separation as Tameryraptor in 2025, so older reconstructions of the body may not represent the Moroccan animal.

### How big was it?

Only the skull is securely measurable. Length and mass estimates rely on scaling from Giganotosaurus and other relatives, so published figures vary considerably.

### Is C. iguidensis a Carcharodontosaurus?

The Niger species was named in 2007 on a partial skull and braincase. Later analyses have questioned whether it belongs in the same genus, and its status remains under review.
