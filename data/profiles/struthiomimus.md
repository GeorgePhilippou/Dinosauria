## Overview

Struthiomimus altus was an ornithomimid from the Dinosaur Park Formation of Alberta, known from several partial skeletons including well-preserved limbs and hands.

Struthiomimus altus ("ostrich mimic") was a large ornithomimosaur from the Campanian of Alberta (~75 Ma), reaching about 4.2 metres. As one of the better-known ornithomimosaurs, it has been at the centre of debates about the diet of these toothless, long-legged theropods — with hypotheses ranging from insectivory to herbivory to filter-feeding depending on the era of analysis.

Current understanding, informed by bone microstructure, jaw mechanics and ecological comparisons, favours an omnivorous diet similar to modern ratite birds: plant material, insects, small vertebrates and occasional carrion. Struthiomimus was fast — likely capable of 50+ km/h — and this speed was its primary defence in a fauna that included the large tyrannosaur Gorgosaurus.

## Anatomy & life

Struthiomimus was a lightly built theropod about four metres long with a small, toothless, beaked head on a long neck, slender arms ending in three long-clawed fingers, and long, powerful hindlimbs. Its name means ostrich mimic, and the body proportions really are close to those of a large flightless bird with a long tail added.

The best specimen, AMNH 5339, was found on the Red Deer River in 1914 with the skeleton still articulated in a death pose, which is why the proportions of Struthiomimus are known more securely than for most ornithomimids. Other skeletons from the same rocks are partial, and the skull is less well preserved than the rest of the body.

The beak and grasping hands have been read in several ways, from browsing on plants and filtering water to catching small animals. None of these is confirmed by gut contents, so the diet remains open, with a plant-rich or mixed diet the most commonly favoured options.

- Ostrich-like proportions with long legs and a slender neck are documented directly in an articulated skeleton.
- The toothless beak and three long, nearly equal fingers with weakly curved claws are unusual among theropods.
- The hindlimb is built for speed, although no top speed can be measured from the bones.

**Feeding: Uncertain.** A toothless beak and clawed hands are consistent with browsing, omnivory or small-prey capture. No gut contents or wear evidence settles the question.

**Movement: Fast bipedal runner.** Long, slender hindlimbs and a compact foot indicate a fast two-legged animal; an exact speed cannot be calculated from the skeleton.

**Appearance: Skeletal outline well supported.** No integument is preserved in Struthiomimus; feather evidence comes from the closely related Ornithomimus.

## Fossil record

Struthiomimus altus is anchored by Lambe's partial pelvis and hindlimb and is best known from AMNH 5339, an articulated skeleton collected by Barnum Brown in 1914, supplemented by other partial skeletons from the Dinosaur Park Formation.

AMNH 5339 preserves the vertebral column, girdles, both forelimbs with hands and both hindlimbs in articulation, with a damaged skull. The holotype and further referred specimens add pelvic, limb and hand material from other individuals in the same formation.

Because so much rests on one skeleton, individual and growth variation are hard to assess, and specimens from outside the Dinosaur Park Formation are not securely assigned.

A long-limbed, toothless-beaked skeleton supports fast locomotion and an omnivorous diet; direct feather/quill-knob evidence comes from its close relative Ornithomimus, with a similar covering inferred for Struthiomimus by phylogenetic bracketing.

- Long hindlimbs support rapid bipedal locomotion, but no exact top speed can be recovered.
- Its toothless beak does not identify a single diet; herbivory and omnivory remain alternatives.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous coastal plain of Alberta alongside Albertosaurus and Edmontosaurus.

Secure S. altus material is late Campanian Dinosaur Park Formation.

## Classification

Struthiomimus is an ornithomimid, one of the toothless ostrich-mimic theropods within Coelurosauria. It is closely related to Ornithomimus from the same rocks, and telling the two apart from partial material remains a recurring problem.

## Open questions

### What did it eat?

The beak does not identify a diet. Suggestions range from plants and small animals to filter-feeding, and the hands have been compared with those of sloths and birds. No direct evidence exists.

### Which bones are really Struthiomimus?

Ornithomimus lived in the same rocks and looks similar. Partial skeletons and material from other formations have been moved between the two genera more than once.

### How fast could it run?

The hindlimb is clearly built for speed, but published speed figures are extrapolations from limb proportions rather than measurements.
