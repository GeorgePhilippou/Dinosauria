## Overview

Cetiosaurus was a large eusauropod dinosaur from the Middle Jurassic of England and possibly France, approximately 174–165 million years ago (Aalenian to Bathonian), making it one of the best-known Jurassic sauropods from Britain. Reaching an estimated 18 metres in length, it was a massive, quadrupedal herbivore with a long neck and tail. Its name, meaning 'whale lizard', was given because its bones were initially mistaken by early researchers for those of a giant marine animal.

Named by John Phillips in 1871, with earlier material described by Richard Owen in 1841, Cetiosaurus was one of the first sauropods to be scientifically studied and holds a place in the early history of palaeontology. It is placed within the Cetiosauridae, an early-diverging eusauropod family. With 30 PBDB occurrences across England, Morocco, Spain, and France, Cetiosaurus-referred material has a wide geographic spread, though some referrals may be erroneous or involve distinct taxa.

## Anatomy & life

Cetiosaurus was among the first sauropod dinosaurs to be described scientifically and played a key role in the gradual recognition of giant terrestrial dinosaurs. Early confusion over its nature—sea monster or land animal—reflects the early state of palaeontology in the 19th century. Its eventual recognition as a terrestrial herbivore contributed to the emerging concept of Dinosauria as a group including very large land animals.

## Fossil record

Material now attributed to Cetiosaurus was first collected from Jurassic outcrops in England during the 1830s and described by Richard Owen in 1841, who initially considered it a giant crocodilian or marine reptile. The name Cetiosaurus ('whale lizard') reflects this misidentification. John Phillips formally described the best-known species, C. oxoniensis, in 1871 from more complete Oxfordshire material, establishing it as a land-dwelling sauropod.

Several partial postcranial skeletons and very limited cranial material.

## Where it lived

During the Middle Jurassic, England lay at a subtropical latitude and was covered by shallow tropical seas interspersed with low islands. Cetiosaurus lived on these landmasses, browsing on vegetation. Its solid vertebrae and large body mass suggest it was less agile than the hollow-boned sauropods of the Late Jurassic, but it was one of the dominant large herbivores of its time and region.

Middle Jurassic limestone deposits yielding the most complete and well-documented Cetiosaurus material.

## Classification

Cetiosaurus had a moderately long neck and tail, solid (not highly pneumatised) vertebrae compared to later sauropods, and robust limbs. Its solidly built vertebrae distinguish it from the hollow-boned titanosaurs and diplodocids, placing it among more basal eusauropods. Multiple species have been assigned to Cetiosaurus historically, but most are now considered nomina dubia or reassigned to other genera.

## Open questions

- Historical referrals are taxonomically heterogeneous.
- Its exact position among basal eusauropods varies.
- No complete skull is known.
