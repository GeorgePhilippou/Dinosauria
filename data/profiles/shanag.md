## Overview

Shanag was a very small carnivorous theropod from the Early Cretaceous of Mongolia, living approximately 142–126 million years ago. Known only from fragmentary skull material, it is estimated at roughly 0.45 metres in length, making it among the smallest known non-avian dinosaurs. The NHM places it broadly within Saurischia, and its precise phylogenetic position remains uncertain due to the limited fossil material.

Named by Turner, Hwang, and Norell in 2007, Shanag ashile takes its genus name from the ritual masked dancers of the Mongolian Buddhist Tsam festival. The species name references the Ashile locality where the fossils were found. Though fragmentary, Shanag is significant as one of the few very small Early Cretaceous theropods known from the region, and its skull fragments display features suggestive of troodontid or dromaeosaurid affinity, though assignment has proven difficult.

## Anatomy & life

At an estimated 0.45 metres, Shanag would have been among the tiniest known non-avian theropods. Such diminutive size suggests it occupied a niche as a predator of insects, small vertebrates, or other invertebrates in the Early Cretaceous Mongolian ecosystems. Its small body would also have reduced water requirements in what was likely a seasonally dry environment.

The Early Cretaceous beds of central Mongolia are not as intensively sampled as the famous Late Cretaceous Gobi sites, making any find from these formations scientifically valuable. Shanag adds to growing evidence that small maniraptorans were diverse in Central Asia well before the Late Cretaceous radiation more familiar from better-known sites.

## Fossil record

Shanag ashile was described in 2007 by Turner, Hwang, and Norell from skull fragments collected at the Öösh locality in central Mongolia. The genus name honours the colourful Tsam festival dancers of Mongolian Buddhist tradition, while the species name refers to the Ashile (Öösh) provenance of the specimen. The holotype consists solely of partial skull bones, limiting detailed anatomical reconstruction.

Partial jaw, vertebra and limb fragments from one individual.

## Where it lived

Skull fragments of Shanag were recovered from Early Cretaceous fluvial deposits at the Öösh locality.

## Classification

The precise placement of Shanag within Maniraptora is debated. Turner and colleagues suggested possible dromaeosaurid or basal paravian affinity, but the fragmentary nature of the material prevents a definitive conclusion. Some analyses have placed it as a basal member of Paraves, near the common ancestor of dromaeosaurids, troodontids, and birds.
