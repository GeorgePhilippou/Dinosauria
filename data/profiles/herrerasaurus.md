## Overview

Herrerasaurus ischigualastensis was an early predatory dinosaur from the Ischigualasto Formation of Argentina. Multiple skeletons, including complete skulls and very substantial postcrania, document a lightly built biped; whether herrerasaurids lie inside Theropoda or just outside the sauropodomorph-theropod split remains debated.

Herrerasaurus was one of the largest predators of the Ischigualasto fauna, reaching six metres long around 231 million years ago. Its flexible intramandibular joint — a sliding articulation between the dentary and postdentary bones unique among early dinosaurs — allowed a powerful grip on struggling prey.

Its phylogenetic position remains debated: most analyses treat it as either a basal saurischian (Herrerasauridae) or an early theropod. Either way it documents the very rapid ecological diversification of early dinosaurs in the Late Triassic of South America.

## Anatomy & life

Herrerasaurus was a medium-sized biped, three to six metres long, that lived in the Ischigualasto valley of north-west Argentina about 230 million years ago, when dinosaurs were still a minor part of the fauna. It shared its habitat with large rhynchosaurs and cynodonts and with the crocodile-line predator Saurosuchus.

Its skull was long and narrow with a sliding joint in the lower jaw, and its teeth were recurved and serrated. The hands had three long clawed fingers plus two reduced ones and were clearly built for grasping. The hindlimbs were long and the pelvis carried features found in later theropods, though in an early combination.

Named in 1963 by Osvaldo Reig from a partial skeleton, Herrerasaurus was transformed in 1988 when Paul Sereno's team found a skeleton with a complete skull. That specimen made it, for a time, the best-known early dinosaur, and it remains central to debates about what the first dinosaurs looked like.

- A complete skull and several substantial skeletons make it one of the best-documented Triassic dinosaurs.
- Recurved, serrated teeth and grasping hands document a predatory lifestyle.
- Its mix of ancestral and theropod-like features keeps its place on the dinosaur tree unsettled.

**Feeding: Meat-eater.** Serrated, recurved teeth, a flexible lower jaw and grasping hands indicate carnivory. Prey is inferred from the fauna rather than from gut contents.

**Movement: Bipedal.** Long hindlimbs and short forelimbs in articulated skeletons show two-legged walking and running; speed is estimated from proportions only.

**Appearance: Skeleton well established.** Proportions come from articulated skeletons; skin, colour and any feather-like covering are not preserved.

## Fossil record

Herrerasaurus is known from the 1963 holotype postcranium, a 1988 skeleton with an essentially complete skull, and additional partial skeletons including material once named Ischisaurus and Frenguellisaurus. Together they cover the whole skeleton.

A complete skull with lower jaws and teeth, cervical, dorsal, sacral and caudal vertebrae, both girdles, forelimbs with complete hands, and hindlimbs with feet, spread across the holotype, PVSJ 407 and referred specimens.

No single specimen preserves everything, so composite reconstructions combine individuals of somewhat different sizes.

Skull, jaw and limb anatomy show a large early bipedal predator with debated exact placement near early saurischians.

- Ischisaurus cattoi and Frenguellisaurus ischigualastensis are generally regarded as synonyms of Herrerasaurus.
- Its mixture of primitive and derived features makes root-level classification unstable.

## Where it lived

Late Triassic ecosystem containing some of the earliest well-known dinosaurs.

The principal Ischigualasto occurrences are radiometrically constrained near 231-229 Ma.

## Classification

Herrerasaurus gives its name to Herrerasauridae, a group of early predatory dinosaurs that includes Staurikosaurus and Gnathovorax. Analyses disagree on whether herrerasaurids are the earliest branch of Theropoda or sit outside the theropod–sauropodomorph split as basal saurischians.

## Open questions

### Theropod or not?

Herrerasaurids combine ancestral hips with theropod-like hands and skulls. Whether they are early theropods or fall outside Theropoda altogether changes with each new analysis.

### How big did it get?

The complete skull belongs to a roughly four-metre animal. Larger partial specimens once named Frenguellisaurus suggest six metres, but no complete large individual exists.

### What did the earliest dinosaurs look like?

Herrerasaurus is often used as a model, but its specialised predatory features may not reflect the dinosaur ancestor.
