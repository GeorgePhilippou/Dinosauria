## Overview

Camarasaurus supremus was the most abundant sauropod of the Late Jurassic Morrison Formation, known from dozens of specimens including several juveniles and complete skulls. Its large, spoon-shaped teeth — far more robust than the peg-like teeth of diplodocids — allowed it to process tougher vegetation: cycads, ferns and woody conifers that diplodocids could not efficiently exploit.

The name "chambered lizard" refers to the large pneumatic chambers in its neck vertebrae that reduced mass. Trackways from the Morrison show that Camarasaurus moved in herds with adults positioned at the periphery protecting juveniles — one of the most direct pieces of fossil evidence for parental or social protective behaviour in sauropods.

## Anatomy & life

Camarasaurus was a heavily built sauropod with a comparatively short neck and tail for its group, a boxy skull with large nostril openings, and stout, spoon-shaped teeth set along the full length of the jaws. Adults of the smaller species reached about fifteen metres; the type species, Camarasaurus supremus, was larger and is among the biggest animals in the Morrison Formation.

Its teeth were thicker and more heavily worn than those of the diplodocids with which it shared the landscape, indicating that it processed tougher vegetation and probably fed at a different range of heights. Gut contents are not preserved, so the actual plants it ate are inferred from tooth wear and from the Morrison flora.

The name means chambered lizard, a reference to the hollowed vertebrae Cope described in 1877. Camarasaurus is by far the most commonly recovered sauropod in the Morrison Formation, and juvenile, subadult and adult skeletons together record how it grew.

- A short, deep skull with a blunt snout and large bony nostril openings set well forward of the eyes.
- Robust spoon-shaped teeth along the whole jaw margin, with heavy wear indicating a coarser diet than contemporary diplodocids.
- Hollowed vertebrae lightened the neck and back; the neck was shorter relative to the body than in Diplodocus or Brachiosaurus.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Broad, worn teeth and a strong jaw show bulk browsing on tougher plants. Feeding height and the specific plants eaten are inferred, not observed.

**Movement: Quadrupedal.** Articulated hands and feet show columnar, weight-bearing limbs. Any herd behaviour is inferred from bonebeds and trackways rather than demonstrated.

**Appearance: Skeletal outline well supported.** Skull, body and limb proportions are known from articulated skeletons; skin, colour and the position of the fleshy nostrils are interpretive.

## Fossil record

Camarasaurus is the most commonly found sauropod in the Morrison Formation. Articulated skeletons with skulls, including a nearly complete juvenile from the Carnegie Quarry, mean that its anatomy is better known than that of almost any other sauropod.

Complete skulls with teeth, entire vertebral columns, both limb girdles, articulated hands and feet, and skeletons ranging from juveniles to large adults, collected from Colorado, Utah, Wyoming and neighbouring states since 1877.

Abundance does not mean every mounted display is one animal: most mounts combine material, and some historic referrals to particular species rest on non-diagnostic bones.

Common skull and skeletal material preserves robust jaws, spoon-shaped teeth and growth information.

## Where it lived

Late Jurassic Morrison floodplains with sauropods, stegosaurs and large theropods.

## Classification

Camarasaurus is a macronarian sauropod and the namesake of Camarasauridae, a lineage of broad-toothed, relatively short-necked sauropods that sits outside the titanosauriform group containing Brachiosaurus. Four species are commonly recognised, but their boundaries are still under revision.

## Open questions

### How many species are valid?

Four species are usually listed, but specimen-level analysis shows that the differences between them are subtle and some historical referrals cannot be tested.

### How heavy was a large adult?

Mass estimates range widely because they depend on modelling methods and on which individuals are chosen as adults.

### How did it feed?

Tooth wear and skull shape support coarse browsing, but neck posture, feeding height and how it shared resources with diplodocids are inferred.
