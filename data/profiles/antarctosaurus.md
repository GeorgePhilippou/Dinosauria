## Overview

Antarctosaurus wichmannianus was a large titanosaurian sauropod from the Late Cretaceous of South America, described by Friedrich von Huene in 1929. Despite its name — which means 'non-northern lizard', not 'Antarctic lizard' — it lived in what is now Argentina, with additional referred material from Brazil, India, and Uruguay. Estimated at approximately 18 metres in length, it inhabited floodplain environments of the Neuquén Basin during the Coniacian to Campanian stages.

Von Huene also named a second species, Antarctosaurus giganteus, based on enormous femora that if correctly attributed would make it one of the largest land animals ever known. However, many subsequently named species of Antarctosaurus have been considered dubious or have been transferred to other genera. The taxonomy of the genus remains unsettled, and the type species alone is considered reliable.

## Anatomy & life

The known material of Antarctosaurus wichmannianus includes cranial elements and postcranial bones, making it one of the more substantially known South American titanosaurs. The skull material is slender and lightly built, with teeth described as pencil-like and suited to bulk herbivory. The vertebrae exhibit the typical hollow, pneumatised construction of titanosaurs.

## Fossil record

Antarctosaurus wichmannianus was described by German palaeontologist Friedrich von Huene in 1929 from material collected in Neuquén and Río Negro provinces of Argentina. The generic name means 'not northern lizard' (combining Greek antarktikos, opposite north, with sauros), emphasising its Gondwanan origin. The species name honours Ricardo Wichmann, who collected the original specimens. In the same publication, von Huene also named Antarctosaurus giganteus from separate enormous limb bones.

A disarticulated, fragmentary type assemblage with uncertain element association.

## Where it lived

Antarctosaurus inhabited the Neuquén Basin of Patagonia, which during the Late Cretaceous was a system of river floodplains and lakes in a seasonally dry climate. This region was one of the most dinosaur-rich environments on Gondwana, yielding numerous titanosaurs, abelisaurid theropods, rebbachisaurid sauropods, and other Cretaceous vertebrates.

Multiple Antarctosaurus specimens have been found here in the Neuquén Basin, one of the most productive regions for South American titanosaurs.

## Classification

The genus Antarctosaurus has a complicated taxonomic history. Von Huene originally assigned multiple species to it, but subsequent studies have considered all but the type species A. wichmannianus as either dubious or belonging to other genera. The potential identity of A. giganteus, based on enormous limb bones suggesting a body mass rivalling Patagotitan, has not been definitively resolved due to the fragmentary nature of the specimens.

## Open questions

- The holotype may combine more than one individual.
- The genus-level diagnosis and phylogenetic position require revision.
- Historical species referrals are heterogeneous.
