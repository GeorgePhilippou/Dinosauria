## Overview

Baryonyx walkeri was a baryonychine spinosaurid from the Barremian Weald Clay Formation of England. The holotype is a substantial partial skull and skeleton, and associated gut-region remains provide rare direct evidence of both fish and ornithopod consumption.

Baryonyx walkeri was discovered in 1983 by amateur fossil hunter William Walker in a clay pit in Surrey, England — one of the most significant British dinosaur finds of the 20th century. Its preserved stomach contents included both fish scales and the bones of a juvenile Iguanodon, proving it had a dual diet as a fish specialist and an opportunistic predator of terrestrial prey.

A spinosaurid closely related to Suchomimus, Baryonyx had a long narrow skull, 96 interlocking conical teeth and an enormous thumb claw over 31 cm long — the feature that gives it its name ("heavy claw"). The original specimen is now one of the centrepiece displays at the Natural History Museum, London.

## Anatomy & life

Baryonyx was a large theropod with a long, low snout lined with many conical teeth, a kinked upper jaw and a large hooked claw, about 31 centimetres along its curve, on the first finger of each hand. The name means 'heavy claw'. It was the first spinosaurid known from a substantial skeleton, and it reshaped how palaeontologists understood the group.

The holotype, found by amateur collector William Walker in a Surrey clay pit in 1983, is roughly two-thirds of a skeleton. Its bones were not fully fused, so the individual may not have been fully grown. Published lengths of around 7.5 to 10 metres and masses of about 1.2 to 1.7 tonnes rest on this one animal.

Fish scales and teeth of the fish Scheenstia, together with partly digested bones of a young iguanodontian, were found in the region of the stomach. This is rare direct evidence of diet in a non-avian dinosaur and shows that Baryonyx took both aquatic and terrestrial prey, whether by hunting or scavenging.

- A long, crocodile-like snout with about twice as many teeth as a typical large theropod.
- Gut-region remains preserve fish scales and iguanodontian bones, direct evidence of a mixed carnivorous diet.
- The enlarged thumb claw gives the genus its name and is preserved on the holotype.

**Feeding: Fish and dinosaur prey.** Fish scales and partly digested iguanodontian bones lay within the holotype's rib cage; whether prey was hunted or scavenged cannot be told.

**Movement: Bipedal.** Hindlimb and pelvic bones show a typical large theropod stance; the robust forelimbs did not bear weight in normal walking.

**Appearance: Skull and body outline supported.** The skeleton constrains proportions well; skin, colour and any soft crest or sail are not preserved.

## Fossil record

Baryonyx is anchored by a single partial skull and skeleton from a Surrey clay pit, the most complete spinosaurid skeleton found in Europe. Referred teeth and bones from elsewhere add little that is diagnostic.

The holotype includes the front of the snout, parts of the braincase and lower jaw, many teeth, vertebrae from neck to tail, ribs and gastralia, the shoulder girdle and forelimbs with the large claw, the pelvis and much of the hindlimb.

The skeleton was partly weathered and disturbed before collection, and much of the tail and the ends of some limbs are missing. Gut-region fish scales and iguanodontian bones are preserved with it.

Conical teeth, long jaws, large thumb claw and stomach contents support fish-eating and opportunistic predation.

- The holotype includes much of the skull, vertebral column and limbs, including the enlarged first manual claw.
- Fish scales and partly digested iguanodontian bones show a broad carnivorous diet, not exclusive fish-eating.

## Where it lived

Early Cretaceous wetland and river-influenced environments in southern England.

The type locality is Barremian in the Weald Clay Formation.

## Classification

Baryonyx is a spinosaurid theropod and the name-giving member of Baryonychinae, the branch with more numerous teeth and less specialised jaws than Spinosaurus. Its relationship to Suchomimus is close and sometimes debated, but the two are not treated as synonyms.

## Open questions

### Was the holotype fully grown?

Unfused bones suggest it may not have been, so the size of a mature Baryonyx remains open.

### Which other fossils belong to it?

Isolated teeth and bones from England, Spain and Portugal have been referred to Baryonyx, but many cannot be distinguished from other baryonychines.

### How did it hunt?

The gut contents show what it ate, not how. Wading and snatching fish is a reasonable inference from the jaws and claws, but it is not directly observed.
