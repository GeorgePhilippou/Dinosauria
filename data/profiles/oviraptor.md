## Overview

Oviraptor philoceratops was an oviraptorid from the Djadokhta Formation of Mongolia, known from one incomplete skeleton with a crushed skull found beside a nest. Many complete museum reconstructions actually represent Citipati.

Oviraptor philoceratops carries one of the most unfortunate names in palaeontology — "egg thief that loves ceratopsian eggs" — based on a misidentification. The original specimen was found next to a nest of eggs in Mongolia in 1923, assumed to be stealing from a Protoceratops nest. Decades later, embryos inside identical eggs were identified as Oviraptor itself — the animal was brooding its own clutch, not stealing Protoceratops eggs.

This correction transformed understanding of oviraptorosaur behaviour: Oviraptor and its relatives were not nest raiders but careful parents, brooding their eggs in a posture identical to modern birds. Oviraptor is a crested oviraptorosaur from the Djadokhta Formation of Mongolia (~75 Ma), about 1.5–2 metres long with a prominent cranial crest of unknown function.

## Anatomy & life

Oviraptor was a lightly built, bird-like theropod with a short, deep, toothless skull, long grasping hands and a crest of some kind above the snout. It lived in the dune fields of the Djadokhta Formation of Mongolia during the Campanian, alongside Protoceratops and Velociraptor. Estimates of around one and a half to two metres in length come from the single incomplete skeleton.

Its name, "egg thief", records a mistake. Roy Chapman Andrews's expedition found the skeleton in 1923 lying over a clutch of eggs assumed to belong to Protoceratops, and Osborn suggested it had been caught raiding the nest. Embryos found inside identical eggs in the 1990s showed that the eggs were oviraptorid, so the animal was almost certainly a parent, not a thief.

Oviraptor is much less well known than its fame suggests. The familiar tall-crested skeletons in museums and books are mostly the relative Citipati, described in 2001. The Oviraptor holotype has a badly crushed skull, so the exact shape of its crest and the finer details of its anatomy remain uncertain.

- A short, deep, toothless skull with a beak and a crest whose true outline is obscured by crushing.
- Long arms and three-fingered hands with strongly curved claws, suited to grasping.
- Found on a nest of oviraptorid eggs, making it a probable brooding parent rather than an egg raider.

**Feeding: Unresolved.** A toothless beak and deep jaws are compatible with plants, eggs, shellfish or small animals; no direct dietary evidence exists for Oviraptor itself.

**Movement: Bipedal.** Hindlimb and pelvic bones in the holotype and complete skeletons of related oviraptorids show a two-legged runner.

**Appearance: Crest shape uncertain.** The crushed skull leaves the crest profile open; feathering is known in other oviraptorosaurs but not preserved in this specimen.

## Fossil record

Oviraptor philoceratops rests on a single incomplete skeleton with a badly crushed skull, found lying over a clutch of eggs at the Flaming Cliffs in 1923. Most of the complete oviraptorid skeletons displayed as Oviraptor belong to Citipati.

A crushed and disarticulated skull with lower jaws, cervical and dorsal vertebrae, ribs, a furcula and shoulder girdle, both forelimbs with hands, the pelvis and partial hindlimbs, all from one animal, plus the nest of eggs it lay upon.

Because only one specimen is securely assigned, growth, variation and the true crest profile cannot be assessed. Anatomy described from referred Zamyn Khondt material is now attributed to Citipati.

Later discoveries showed oviraptorosaurs brooded eggs, changing the old “egg thief” interpretation.

- The eggs near the type are oviraptorosaur eggs, overturning the original idea that it was stealing Protoceratops eggs.
- The holotype is less complete and has a lower crest than the animal often illustrated as Oviraptor.
- Brooding is strongly evidenced across oviraptorids, but exact diet remains unresolved.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous arid to semi-arid Gobi Desert dune and interdune deposits.

The Djadokhta type horizon is Campanian, approximately 75-71 Ma.

## Classification

Oviraptor is the name-bearing genus of Oviraptoridae and Oviraptorosauria, a group of feathered, beaked maniraptoran theropods. Its exact position within Oviraptoridae is hard to fix because the holotype is crushed; several specimens once called Oviraptor have been moved to Citipati and other genera.

## Open questions

### What did the crest look like?

The skull is shattered and flattened. Restorations range from a low ridge to a tall Citipati-like casque, and none can be confirmed from the holotype.

### What did it eat?

Lizard bones were reported in the holotype's body cavity, and shellfish, eggs and plants have all been proposed. No study has settled the question.

### How much of what we know is really Oviraptor?

Only the holotype is securely assigned. Referred material from Zamyn Khondt and elsewhere belongs to Citipati or remains unnamed, so most published "Oviraptor" anatomy needs a second look.
