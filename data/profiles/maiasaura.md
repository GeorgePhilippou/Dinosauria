## Overview

Maiasaura peeblesorum ("good mother lizard") transformed understanding of dinosaur parental behaviour. From the Late Cretaceous Two Medicine Formation of Montana (~76 Ma), nesting colonies preserving eggs, hatchlings and juveniles at different stages were discovered by Jack Horner and Bob Makela in 1978. The hatchlings' underdeveloped limb bones showed they were born helpless and required parental feeding in the nest — the first evidence of extended parental care in non-avian dinosaurs.

The Maiasaura bonebeds in Montana are extraordinary: a single catastrophic event — possibly a volcanic ashfall — killed approximately 10,000 individuals in one horizon. This mass-death assemblage across a wide area provides bone histology data for the entire population, showing Maiasaura grew at rates comparable to modern large mammals rather than reptiles.

## Anatomy & life

Maiasaura was a medium to large hadrosaurid, with adults roughly seven to nine metres long. It had a broad, flat-headed skull with a small solid crest in front of the eyes, a wide beak and grinding dental batteries. Hatchlings were less than half a metre long, and bone histology shows that they grew rapidly, approaching adult size within about eight years.

It is famous for the nesting grounds discovered near Choteau, Montana, in 1978. Nests containing hatchlings with worn teeth but poorly ossified limb joints indicated that young stayed in the nest and were fed, evidence that gave the genus its name. The record supports extended nest occupancy and repeated use of the same nesting sites, though the sex of the caring parent and the details of provisioning are not known.

Elsewhere in the Two Medicine Formation, a bonebed containing the remains of thousands of individuals killed together preserves a cross-section of a population. Sampling of that bonebed has produced one of the most detailed growth and survivorship records for any dinosaur.

- Nest colonies with eggs, embryos and nest-bound hatchlings, the first strong evidence of extended parental care in a dinosaur.
- A growth series from embryo to adult showing rapid early growth and near-adult size in under a decade.
- A flat, broad skull with a small solid crest above the snout, unlike the hollow crests of lambeosaurine relatives.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Dental batteries and tooth wear show grinding of tough plants; worn teeth in hatchlings indicate that nestlings ate plant material too.

**Movement: Quadrupedal and bipedal.** Limb anatomy allows both gaits, and growth changes suggest juveniles were more bipedal than adults. Habitual speed is inferred.

**Appearance: Skeletal outline well supported.** Skull and body proportions are known at every growth stage, but skin, colour and any soft crest are not preserved for this genus.

## Fossil record

Maiasaura is known from nesting colonies, embryos, hatchlings, juveniles and adults, and from a bonebed containing the remains of thousands of individuals. Few dinosaurs are documented across so much of their life cycle.

Skulls and skeletons of adults and juveniles, embryos and hatchlings within nests, eggs and eggshell, and bonebed material representing all skeletal regions across the growth series.

The name-bearing specimen is a partial adult skull. Much of the vast bonebed material is disarticulated, so complete individual skeletons are rarer than the sheer number of bones suggests.

Nesting grounds and growth series provide direct evidence for reproduction and development.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous seasonal floodplain settings in Montana.

## Classification

Maiasaura is a saurolophine hadrosaurid within Brachylophosaurini, a group of flat-headed duck-billed dinosaurs with small solid crests that also includes Brachylophosaurus. Its position is consistent across recent analyses.

## Open questions

### How exactly were the young cared for?

Nest-bound hatchlings show that adults attended nests, but which sex did so, whether food was brought or regurgitated, and how long care lasted are inferences.

### What killed the bonebed population?

The mass-death assemblage may record a single catastrophic event or a combination of events, which affects how its population structure is read.

### How many species are there?

Only Maiasaura peeblesorum is recognised. Whether all Two Medicine material belongs to one species has not been fully tested at specimen level.
