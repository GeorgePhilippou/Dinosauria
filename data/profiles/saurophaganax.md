## Overview

Saurophaganax (meaning 'king of the lizard eaters') was one of the largest predatory dinosaurs of the Late Jurassic, reaching an estimated 12 metres in length. It lived approximately 152–143 million years ago (Kimmeridgian–Tithonian) in what is now Oklahoma, USA, and is known from fragmentary but distinctive material from the Morrison Formation. Named by Daniel Chure in 1995–1996, it is classified within Allosauridae alongside the better-known Allosaurus.

Saurophaganax is scientifically significant as a possible contender for the largest known allosaurid theropod and for documenting allosaurid diversity within the Morrison Formation ecosystem. Some researchers have debated whether it should be considered a very large species of Allosaurus (A. maximus) or a distinct genus; current consensus generally treats it as a separate genus, Saurophaganax maximus. Its size makes it a possible apex predator even within the predator-rich Morrison Formation.

## Anatomy & life

Saurophaganax is estimated at 10.5–12 metres in length, making it comparable to or slightly larger than most Allosaurus specimens. Some palaeontologists have retained it as Allosaurus maximus rather than a separate genus; the debate hinges on whether the distinguishing features (particularly neural spine morphology) are sufficient for generic separation or merely represent large-body-size variation within Allosaurus.

The Morrison Formation of the western United States preserves one of the most diverse large-theropod assemblages of any Jurassic ecosystem, including Allosaurus, Torvosaurus, Ceratosaurus, and Marshosaurus. Saurophaganax, if valid as a distinct taxon, would represent the largest of these predators, capable of taking very large sauropod prey.

The known Saurophaganax material comes from a small number of Oklahoma Morrison Formation localities, representing elements of the vertebral column, pelvis, and limb bones. The material, while fragmentary, shows proportional differences from typical Allosaurus that have supported its generic separation by most recent workers.

## Fossil record

Material later assigned to Saurophaganax was collected from Oklahoma in the 1930s–1940s, initially referred to Allosaurus. Daniel Chure formally named Saurophaganax maximus in 1995 (with full publication in 1996), recognising distinctive vertebral features that differentiated the Oklahoma material from typical Allosaurus fragilis specimens. The name reflects its probable status as the apex predator of its ecosystem.

An isolated name-bearing neural arch plus historically referred quarry material now divided among sauropod and theropod specimens.

- The holotype OMNH 1123 is an isolated neural arch, not a complete or associated giant theropod skeleton.
- Some bones formerly referred to Saurophaganax are more consistent with diplodocid sauropods.
- The securely theropod material from the Kenton quarry was separated as Allosaurus anax in 2024.
- Older size estimates for a giant Saurophaganax combine referred bones and should not be presented as measurements of the name-bearing specimen.

## Where it lived

Late Jurassic fluvial floodplain deposits of the Morrison Formation yielding the Saurophaganax material.

The record is restricted to the Kenton Member of the Morrison Formation in Oklahoma. A broad formation-wide occurrence range does not establish the range of the disputed name.

## Open questions

- The dinosaurian identity of the holotype remains uncertain.
- The new combination Allosaurus anax and the proposed treatment of the name Saurophaganax require testing in subsequent work.
