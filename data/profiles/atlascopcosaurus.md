## Overview

Atlascopcosaurus loadsi was a small herbivorous ornithopod dinosaur from the Early Cretaceous of Victoria, Australia, living approximately 126–110 million years ago during the Barremian to Albian stages. About 3 metres long, it is known from fragmentary dental and jaw material recovered from the Eumeralla Formation (also known from the Strzelecki Group exposures) at sites near Dinosaur Cove along the Victorian coast. The genus is named in honour of Atlas Copco, the mining equipment company that provided drilling machinery used during excavations.

Described by Rich and Rich (Vickers-Rich) in 1989, Atlascopcosaurus is placed within Elasmaria, a clade of basal ornithopods thought to have inhabited Gondwana. Its Australian provenance is significant, as it inhabited a polar or near-polar environment during the Early Cretaceous, when Victoria was within the Antarctic Circle. This makes it part of a unique dinosaur fauna adapted to the prolonged polar darkness that characterised these high-latitude habitats.

## Anatomy & life

Atlascopcosaurus is known from fragmentary jaw material and teeth, limiting detailed anatomical description. The teeth are characterised by features consistent with a basal ornithopod, including relatively simple crown morphology suited to herbivory. Its estimated body length of approximately 3 metres places it among the smaller ornithopods known from Australia. Classification within Elasmaria reflects its similarities to other Gondwanan ornithopods.

Atlascopcosaurus is part of a distinctive polar dinosaur assemblage from the Eumeralla Formation that provides crucial evidence for dinosaur life at high latitudes. Australian Early Cretaceous dinosaurs occupied an isolated landmass connected to Antarctica, meaning this fauna evolved largely in isolation from contemporaneous Asian and North American communities. The fragmentary nature of Atlascopcosaurus material highlights the challenges of recovering and interpreting polar fossil vertebrates.

## Fossil record

Atlascopcosaurus loadsi was described by Thomas Rich and Patricia Vickers-Rich in 1989 from jaw fragments found at Dinosaur Cove, Victoria. The generic name honours Atlas Copco, whose compressed-air drilling equipment was essential for excavating the hard rock in which the fossils were entombed. The species name recognises William Loads of Atlas Copco Australia. Excavation at Dinosaur Cove was an extraordinarily difficult undertaking, involving tunnelling into sea cliffs.

A partial maxilla plus isolated dentaries and teeth.

## Where it lived

During the Early Cretaceous, Victoria was located within the Antarctic Circle at approximately 75–80 degrees south latitude. This meant Atlascopcosaurus endured several months of complete polar darkness each year. Whether these dinosaurs were resident year-round or migrated is debated. The presence of large-eyed relatives like Leaellynasaura from the same formation has been used to argue for year-round residence with adaptations for low-light conditions.

Fragmentary jaw and dental material of Atlascopcosaurus was found in coastal cliffs at Dinosaur Cove, one of the most productive Early Cretaceous polar dinosaur sites in the world.

## Open questions

- The diagnosis relies on limited dental characters.
- Its exact position within Gondwanan ornithopods is unresolved.
