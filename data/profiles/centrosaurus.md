## Overview

Centrosaurus apertus was a centrosaurine ceratopsid from the Campanian of Alberta (~76–75 Ma), bearing a prominent nasal horn and a frill with distinctive paired hooks projecting forward from the top margin. Massive bone beds in the Dinosaur Park Formation preserve thousands of Centrosaurus individuals — among the largest dinosaur bone beds anywhere in the world — providing population-level data at a scale unmatched for any other ceratopsian.

Analysis of the Centrosaurus bone beds has been used to test herd structure, disease prevalence and growth biology across an entire population. One remarkable study identified cancer (an aggressive bone tumour) in a Centrosaurus specimen — the oldest cancer confirmed in any tetrapod — providing direct evidence of pathological conditions in Cretaceous dinosaurs.

## Anatomy & life

The Hilda Bone Bed — stretching over 2.3 square kilometres and containing thousands of Centrosaurus individuals — is the result of a single catastrophic event, likely a flood during a seasonal migration. Demographic analysis shows a typical herd structure: mainly sub-adults and adults, with juveniles underrepresented (likely because their smaller, less dense bones were destroyed more rapidly). The enormous scale implies herds of thousands of individuals.

A 2020 study by Ekaterina Todorova and colleagues described a large aggressive bone tumour (osteosarcoma) in a Centrosaurus fibula. The tumour had grown extensively and the animal was clearly in poor health at death, yet showed no evidence of predation. This — the oldest confirmed malignant bone cancer in any tetrapod — shows that cancer was a biological reality for dinosaurs as it is for modern vertebrates.

Centrosaurus shows considerable individual variation in nasal horn curvature and frill hook morphology, complicating species distinctions within the genus. The nasal horn could curve forward, upright or backward in different individuals; whether this reflects sexual dimorphism, age variation or individual variation is debated. The frill hooks served primarily for display and species recognition.

## Fossil record

Thousands of cranial and postcranial bones, including many skulls, skeletons and multi-individual bonebeds.

Vast monodominant bonebeds document mass-mortality events; the older "drowned during a river-crossing migration" idea has been challenged by newer work pointing to catastrophic seasonal flooding as the more likely cause.

- A large nasal horn and hooked frill ornament are documented across growth stages.
- Mass-death bonebeds support group aggregation at death but do not alone reveal permanent herd structure or migration.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous coastal plain of western Canada with diverse hadrosaur and theropod faunas.

C. apertus is concentrated in the lower-middle Dinosaur Park Formation, late Campanian.

## Open questions

- Bonebed processes and social interpretation are debated.
- Some named centrosaur species may reflect variation or different horizons.
