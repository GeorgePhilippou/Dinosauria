## Overview

Allosaurus fragilis was the dominant predator of the Late Jurassic Morrison Formation, reaching nearly ten metres and 2.7 tonnes. Its wide-opening jaws were used in a distinctive axe-strike attack — driving the upper jaw downward into prey — rather than the crushing bite of a tyrannosaur, allowing it to wound and incapacitate animals far larger than itself.

Dozens of specimens are known, making it one of the best-studied large theropods. The Cleveland-Lloyd Quarry in Utah has yielded over 70 individuals — probably a predator trap accumulation. Direct fossil evidence of predator-prey interaction with Stegosaurus survives as reciprocal bone injuries on both species.

## Anatomy & life

Allosaurus was a large, two-legged meat-eater that lived across the floodplains of western North America in the Late Jurassic. Typical adults were about eight to nine metres long and weighed roughly one and a half to two tonnes; a few individuals were larger, and the upper end of its size range is still debated because the biggest bones are fragmentary or of contested identity.

Its skull was lightweight for its size, with a pair of short horn-like bosses in front of the eyes and rows of serrated, blade-like teeth. The neck was strongly muscled, the arms were relatively long with three large hooked claws, and the tail was long and deep. Biomechanical work suggests the jaws opened very widely and that the skull was built to withstand vertical strikes rather than an exceptionally powerful bite.

Two species are widely recognised from the Morrison Formation: Allosaurus fragilis and the somewhat older A. jimmadseni, named in 2020. Because the genus is known from so many individuals of different sizes, it is a standard reference for theropod growth, injury and skull function. Bonebeds such as the Cleveland-Lloyd Dinosaur Quarry preserve dozens of animals together, but how they came to be there, and whether Allosaurus ever cooperated when feeding, remains unresolved.

- A light, deep skull with short paired brow bosses and serrated blade-like teeth, capable of a very wide gape.
- Long, powerful arms with three strongly curved claws, unlike the reduced forelimbs of later tyrannosaurs.
- Dozens of individuals from juveniles to large adults, including one of the most complete theropod skeletons known.

**Feeding: Large carnivore.** Blade-like teeth, tooth-marked bones of Morrison sauropods and stegosaurs, and a puncture in an Allosaurus tail vertebra matching a Stegosaurus tail spike record predation and combat. Cooperative hunting is not established.

**Movement: Bipedal walker.** Hindlimb anatomy and Morrison trackways support two-legged walking; running speed estimates rest on biomechanical models rather than direct observation.

**Appearance: Skeletal outline well supported.** Proportions are known from complete skeletons. No skin impressions, feathers, colour or the exact form of the brow horns' keratin covering are directly preserved.

## Fossil record

Allosaurus is one of the best-sampled large theropods. Near-complete articulated skeletons, complete skulls and a bonebed holding at least 46 individuals document every part of the body across a wide range of ages. The weak point is the name itself: the original holotype is a handful of fragments.

Complete skulls with dentition and braincases, full vertebral columns, pectoral and pelvic girdles, forelimbs with three-clawed hands, hindlimbs and feet, furculae and gastralia, from many individuals. Pathologies such as healed fractures and infections are common, and bone histology has been sampled across the growth series.

The Marsh holotype YPM 1930 is too fragmentary to diagnose the species, so the identity of Allosaurus fragilis rests in practice on well-preserved referred skeletons and a proposed neotype rather than on the name-bearing specimen.

Skulls, teeth, limb bones and healed injuries support interpretations of feeding, growth and predatory behaviour.

## Where it lived

Late Jurassic Morrison floodplains with sauropods, stegosaurs and other theropods.

## Classification

Allosaurus is the name-bearing genus of Allosauridae within Allosauroidea, a group of large tetanuran theropods. Its position is stable; the live questions concern how many species the Morrison material contains and whether the giant Saurophaganax bones belong to Allosaurus.

## Open questions

### How many species are there?

Allosaurus fragilis and A. jimmadseni are widely accepted. Older names such as A. europaeus, Antrodemus and Creosaurus, and the referral of individual specimens, still need specimen-by-specimen treatment.

### Did it hunt in groups?

Bonebeds with many individuals have been read as evidence of gregarious behaviour, but they can equally represent predator traps or repeated attraction to carcasses. No direct evidence of cooperative hunting exists.

### How big did it get?

Ordinary adults are well measured, but the largest bones assigned to Allosaurus or to Saurophaganax are isolated, so the maximum size and its taxonomic meaning remain open.
