## Overview

Styracosaurus albertensis ("spiked lizard") was a centrosaurine ceratopsid from the Campanian of Alberta (~75 Ma), bearing one of the most elaborate frill and horn arrays of any ceratopsian: a large nasal horn up to 60 cm long, four to six long curved spikes projecting from the frill margin, and smaller frill ornaments. Its elaborate display structures make it one of the most recognisable ceratopsids.

Bone beds in the Dinosaur Park Formation preserve dozens of Styracosaurus individuals together — one of the best-documented centrosaurine bone beds. These mass-death assemblages are interpreted as herd drownings during river crossings, analogous to modern wildebeest migrations, and provide population-level data on growth rates, health and demographics.

## Anatomy & life

Styracosaurus was a rhinoceros-sized horned dinosaur that lived on the coastal plains of what is now southern Alberta about 76 to 75 million years ago. It was around five metres long and walked on four sturdy legs, with a deep beaked skull carrying a single long nasal horn and a frill fringed with four to six long spikes.

The nasal horn could exceed half a metre in length, while the brow horns were reduced to low bumps. The frill spikes are the animal's signature, but they vary strikingly between individuals in number, length and curvature, and one skull even has a small extra spike growing from the middle of the frill. This variation is real, not reconstruction, and complicates attempts to name species from frill shape alone.

Its jaws held batteries of shearing teeth for processing tough vegetation, and it belonged to Centrosaurinae, the short-frilled branch of the horned dinosaurs. Bonebeds containing many individuals show that at least some centrosaurines lived or died in groups, although Styracosaurus itself is known from far fewer individuals than Centrosaurus.

- A single nasal horn more than 50 cm long, with the brow horns reduced to small bumps.
- A frill edged with four to six long spikes whose number and shape vary from one skull to the next.
- Batteries of tightly packed shearing teeth and a deep, parrot-like beak for cropping plants.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** The toothless beak and shearing tooth batteries match a diet of tough, low-growing plants; the specific plants eaten are not preserved.

**Movement: Quadrupedal.** Robust forelimbs and hindlimbs are known from the holotype skeleton and AMNH 5372; forelimb posture is still modelled rather than observed.

**Appearance: Horn cores and frill preserved.** The bony horn cores and spikes are real, but their keratin sheaths, skin and colour are inferred.

## Fossil record

Styracosaurus albertensis is anchored by the holotype skull collected in 1913, whose lower jaws and much of the skeleton were recovered from the same quarry in 1935. A second skull and skeleton at the American Museum, further skulls and a Dinosaur Park bonebed round out the record.

Complete and partial skulls with the nasal horn and spiked frill, lower jaws, the vertebral column, ribs, pectoral and pelvic girdles and limb bones of at least two associated individuals, and disarticulated bones from a multi-individual bonebed in Dinosaur Provincial Park.

Even with several skulls, the sample is small compared with Centrosaurus, so the full range of frill variation and the growth series are incompletely known.

Nasal horn, frill spikes and bonebeds document centrosaurine ceratopsid diversity.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous western Canadian coastal plain ecosystems.

## Classification

Styracosaurus is a centrosaurine ceratopsid, close to Centrosaurus and to the pachyrhinosaurs. Whether the Montana species Rubeosaurus ovatus should be returned to Styracosaurus, as some recent work proposes, remains disputed.

## Open questions

### How many species are there?

Styracosaurus parksi, named from AMNH 5372, is now treated as S. albertensis. Whether Rubeosaurus ovatus from Montana is a second Styracosaurus species remains debated.

### Why do the frills differ so much?

Spike number and shape vary between skulls and even between the two sides of one skull. Individual variation and growth explain much of it, which makes species defined on ornament alone hard to test.

### What were the spikes for?

Display and species recognition are the usual interpretations. There is no direct evidence of combat use, and the keratin sheaths that would have covered the spikes are not preserved.
