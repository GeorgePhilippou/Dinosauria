## Overview

Coelophysis bauri was a small early neotheropod from the Chinle Group of the south-western United States. The Ghost Ranch bonebeds contain hundreds of individuals and many articulated skeletons, making its anatomy and growth exceptionally well documented.

Coelophysis was a slender Late Triassic theropod, about two metres long, that inhabited the flood-plains of what is now the American Southwest around 215 million years ago. Its hollow limb bones kept it lightweight and fast; its narrow jaws bore small serrated teeth suited to catching lizards and small vertebrates.

The Ghost Ranch quarry in New Mexico preserves hundreds of Coelophysis individuals of all age classes in a single catastrophic deposit — one of the most productive Triassic sites in the world. These bonebeds allowed the first detailed study of growth and possible social behaviour in an early theropod.

## Anatomy & life

Coelophysis was a lightly built, long-necked theropod of the Late Triassic, roughly three metres long but weighing only about the same as a large dog. It had a narrow snout with small, blade-like serrated teeth, long legs, a long stiffening tail and grasping three-fingered hands, and lived on the river floodplains recorded by the Chinle Group of the American south-west.

It was named in 1889 by Edward Drinker Cope from scrappy bones collected near Abiquiu, New Mexico. Its fame came in 1947 when an American Museum team led by Edwin Colbert opened a quarry at Ghost Ranch that yielded hundreds of skeletons, many complete and articulated, ranging from juveniles to adults. A skeleton from that quarry, AMNH 7224, was made the neotype in 1996 to fix the name.

The bonebed has fed speculation about herds and cannibalism. The mass burial is now read as animals killed and concentrated by floods rather than proof of social living, and the supposed juvenile Coelophysis inside two adults' ribcages proved to be small crocodile relatives. What is secure is the anatomy: Coelophysis is the reference point for early theropod skeletons.

- A slender skull with serrated teeth and a kinked upper jaw typical of coelophysoids.
- Hollow, thin-walled bones gave the genus its name, "hollow form".
- Hundreds of individuals from one quarry document growth from juvenile to adult and two adult body forms.

**Feeding: Carnivore.** Serrated teeth and gut contents including small reptile bones show a diet of small animals; the earlier cannibalism claim was withdrawn.

**Movement: Bipedal runner.** Long hindlimbs and a lightweight build in articulated skeletons indicate a fast two-legged runner; actual speeds are modelled, not measured.

**Appearance: Skeleton fully known.** Proportions are secure; no skin, feathers or colour are preserved, so the external covering is reconstructed.

## Fossil record

Coelophysis bauri is among the best-represented early dinosaurs, with hundreds of individuals, many complete and articulated, from the Ghost Ranch quarry in New Mexico. Its anatomy and growth are known in a detail few Triassic animals approach.

Complete skulls with teeth, full vertebral columns and tails, furculae, shoulder and hip girdles, forelimbs with hands and hindlimbs with feet, in many articulated skeletons, plus gut contents in a few individuals.

Nearly all of this material comes from one quarry, so the sample records one population and one moment; earlier referred material from elsewhere is now largely reassigned.

Large bonebeds preserve growth series and slender theropod anatomy.

- The neotype AMNH 7224 stabilises C. bauri, and Rioarribasaurus colberti is an objective junior synonym.
- A famous cannibalism claim was overturned when the supposed juvenile remains inside one ribcage were identified as a crocodylomorph.

## Where it lived

Late Triassic floodplain settings near the early diversification of dinosaurs.

Diagnostic C. bauri material is late Norian to Rhaetian in the Chinle Group, especially the Rock Point Formation.

## Classification

Coelophysis is the name-bearing genus of Coelophysidae and Coelophysoidea, a group of slender early neotheropods. Because its skeleton is so well known it anchors most analyses of early theropod relationships. Several species and specimens once assigned to it, including material from Africa and Arizona, are now placed in other genera.

## Open questions

### Did it live in groups?

Hundreds of animals buried together suggest a gathering, but flood transport and a drought-stricken water hole can produce the same result. The bonebed is not proof of pack living.

### What are the two adult body forms?

Adults fall into robust and gracile morphs. Sexual dimorphism is the usual explanation, but individual variation has not been ruled out.

### How much of the old record belongs here?

Coelophysis-like bones from Arizona, Zimbabwe and elsewhere were once referred to the genus; most are now assigned to other taxa, leaving Ghost Ranch as the core sample.
