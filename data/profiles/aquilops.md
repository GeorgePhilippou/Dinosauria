## Overview

Aquilops americanus was a tiny neoceratopsian dinosaur from the Early Cretaceous of North America, living approximately 109–104 million years ago. It was one of the smallest ceratopsians known, estimated at about 60 centimetres in length and approximately 1 kilogram in mass, and is known from a single partial skull collected from the Cloverly Formation of Montana. Its eagle-like, strongly hooked beak gave it the name meaning 'eagle face'.

Named by Farke and colleagues in 2014, Aquilops is the oldest known neoceratopsian from North America and provides important evidence for an early dispersal of this group from Asia to North America. Its Asian affinities suggest that small neoceratopsians crossed into North America via a Bering land bridge tens of millions of years before the large-horned ceratopsids diversified there.

## Anatomy & life

Aquilops is known from a single partial skull measuring only a few centimetres across, indicating the animal was extremely small even by ceratopsian standards. The skull displays a strongly hooked beak, a small frill at the back, and the slender build typical of basal neoceratopsians. It lacked the elaborate horns and large frills of later ceratopsids, being much more similar anatomically to Asian forms such as Protoceratops.

Aquilops is remarkable for being approximately 20 million years older than the next-oldest neoceratopsians known from North America. Its close resemblance to Asian neoceratopsians such as Liaoceratops and Auroraceratops supports the hypothesis that early ceratopsians migrated from Asia to North America during the Early Cretaceous, long before the larger-bodied ceratopsids evolved their diversified horns and frills.

At approximately 1 kilogram, Aquilops was about the size of a domestic rabbit. It was a ground-dwelling herbivore that likely fed on low-growing vegetation, using its sharp beak to clip plant material. The Cloverly Formation environment represented a seasonally dry or forested lowland setting, also home to the theropod Deinonychus and sauropods such as Sauroposeidon.

## Fossil record

Aquilops americanus was described in 2014 by Andrew Farke and colleagues from a partial skull collected from the Cloverly Formation of Montana. The generic name derives from Latin aquila (eagle) and Greek ops (face), referring to the strongly hooked rostral bone of the skull. The species name americanus reflects its North American provenance and its importance as the first neoceratopsian demonstrated from Early Cretaceous North America.

- The hooked rostral bone and associated skull elements diagnose the genus.
- Body size and posture are inferred from relatives because no postcranial bones are known.

## Where it lived

The only known Aquilops specimen — a partial skull — was recovered from this Early Cretaceous formation, making it the oldest neoceratopsian recorded in North America.

The relevant Cloverly beds are late Aptian to early Albian.

## Open questions

- Only one partial skull is known.
- The individual was not fully mature.
- Its exact position among early neoceratopsians is weakly resolved.
