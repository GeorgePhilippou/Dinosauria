## Overview

Nothronychus mckinleyi was the first therizinosaur described from North America, discovered in the Moreno Hill Formation of New Mexico (~91 Ma) and described in 2001. Its discovery filled a major gap: before Nothronychus, therizinosaurs were known only from Asia, and this North American representative confirmed that the group had dispersed across the Bering land connection during the Cretaceous.

Like all therizinosaurs, Nothronychus was a herbivorous theropod with a broad pelvis, leaf-shaped teeth, long arms with large claws and an overall body plan suited to browsing high vegetation. A second species, N. graffami, was described in 2009 from slightly different Utah strata.

## Anatomy & life

Nothronychus and the later-described Falcarius (from the Cedar Mountain Formation of Utah, ~125 Ma) document that therizinosaurs entered North America during the Cretaceous, probably from Asia via Beringia. Falcarius, earlier in time, shows more carnivorous-grade features than Nothronychus, suggesting the group was still transitioning toward herbivory when it arrived.

Nothronychus shows the full therizinosaur suite of herbivory adaptations: broad pelvis, opisthopubic configuration (pubis pointing backward, convergent with ornithischians), leaf-shaped dentition and expanded gut capacity. The arms were long with large curved claws, consistent with pulling down branches. Its forelimb proportions suggest browsing at different heights from contemporaneous sauropods in the same region.

## Fossil record

James Kirkland and Douglas Wolfe described Nothronychus mckinleyi from material recovered during road construction in New Mexico. The species name honours William McKinley; the genus name means "sloth claw." A second species from Utah was found in marine Cretaceous deposits — an unusual context suggesting the animal was washed offshore after death.

Two partial skeletons representing two species, with extensive axial and appendicular material but limited skull remains.

The large manual claws and herbivorous theropod proportions confirm its therizinosaur identity as the first North American member of the group.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous of the southwestern United States, in a coastal plain setting near the Western Interior Seaway.

## Open questions

- Species-level differences and exact diet remain open.
- Cranial anatomy and integument are incomplete.
