## Overview

Puertasaurus was a gigantic titanosaurian sauropod from the Late Cretaceous of Patagonia, Argentina, living approximately 83–72 million years ago (Campanian). Named by Novas, Salgado, Calvo, and Agnolin in 2005, it is known from only four bones—one cervical vertebra, one dorsal vertebra, and two caudal vertebrae—recovered from the Cerro Fortaleza Formation of Santa Cruz Province in 2001. Despite this scant material, the enormous size of the bones suggests it was among the largest dinosaurs ever to have lived, with length estimates often placed at 26–40 metres.

Puertasaurus belongs to Titanosauria within the Colossosauria, a clade encompassing the largest known land animals. Its single cervical vertebra is exceptionally wide (about 1.18 m across), suggesting an extremely broad neck. The species name honours fossil hunters Pablo Puerta and Santiago Reuil who discovered and excavated the material. With only one known individual, precise size and ecological reconstructions remain highly speculative.

## Anatomy & life

Puertasaurus is frequently listed among the largest dinosaurs ever, but with only four bones, size estimates carry enormous uncertainty. The dorsal vertebra is about 1.06 m tall and 1.68 m wide, and the cervical vertebra spans approximately 1.18 m—dimensions comparable to or exceeding those of Argentinosaurus. Estimates of total length ranging from about 26 to 40 metres have been proposed, but none can be considered definitive.

Patagonia has produced an extraordinary diversity of giant titanosaur sauropods, including Argentinosaurus, Patagotitan, Dreadnoughtus, and Futalognkosaurus. Puertasaurus adds to this roster and underscores southern South America as a major centre of giant sauropod diversity in the Late Cretaceous.

## Fossil record

The Puertasaurus remains were discovered in 2001 by Pablo Puerta and Santiago Reuil in the Cerro Fortaleza Formation of southern Patagonia. The genus was formally described in 2005 by Fernando Novas and colleagues. The type species, Puertasaurus reuili, honours both discoverers.

- The enormous dorsal vertebra demonstrates a very broad trunk region.
- No skull or limb bones are known from the holotype.
- Exact length and mass estimates are highly speculative because scaling four vertebrae requires uncertain proportions.

## Where it lived

Fossil tree trunks found in the same locality suggest Puertasaurus may have inhabited a forested environment in Late Cretaceous Patagonia—in contrast to the more open habitats often envisaged for giant titanosaurs. Its enormous size would have allowed it to reach high foliage unavailable to smaller herbivores.

## Open questions

- Formation nomenclature and age remain debated.
- Nearly the entire skeleton and all precise size metrics are unknown.
