## Overview

Tenontosaurus ('sinew lizard') was a large ornithopod dinosaur from the Early Cretaceous of North America, living approximately 120–110 million years ago. It reached around 7 metres in length and weighed up to 900 kilograms, making it one of the more substantial ornithopods of its time. Fossils are known from both Canada and the United States, particularly from the Cloverly Formation and Antlers Formation. It walked primarily on all four legs but was likely capable of rearing on its hind limbs.

Named by John Ostrom in 1970, Tenontosaurus is taxonomically placed within Iguanodontia and represents an important link in ornithopod evolution. It is famously associated with the dromaeosaurid Deinonychus, whose teeth and shed claws are frequently found near Tenontosaurus remains, suggesting predator–prey interactions or scavenging. The evidence that Tenontosaurus may have lived in herds — inferred from multi-individual bonebeds — provides insights into social behaviour as an anti-predator strategy.

## Anatomy & life

Tenontosaurus is strongly associated with the predatory dromaeosaurid Deinonychus antirrhopus, whose teeth and claws appear repeatedly in bonebeds with Tenontosaurus carcasses. This predator–prey relationship, popularised after Ostrom's research, informed later depictions of pack-hunting behaviour in dromaeosaurids, though whether Deinonychus actively cooperated in hunts remains debated. The evidence that Tenontosaurus may have travelled in herds is consistent with the hypothesis that large group size provided a defence against such predators.

Tenontosaurus is characterised by a relatively long tail stiffened by ossified tendons, a broad skull with a horny beak, and robust forelimbs suggesting semi-quadrupedal gait. Its body plan — intermediate between bipedal basal ornithopods and the fully quadrupedal Iguanodon — makes it a useful taxon for understanding transitions in ornithopod locomotion.

## Fossil record

Tenontosaurus was named by John Ostrom in 1970 on the basis of material from the Cloverly Formation of Montana and Wyoming. The generic name refers to the distinctive long, stiffened tendons along the tail and back. Two species are currently recognised: T. tillettorum (Ostrom, 1970) and T. dossi (Winkler, Murry and Jacobs, 1997) from Texas.

Many partial skeletons and a near-complete individual, plus multi-individual bonebeds.

- Tenontosaurus tilletti is represented by numerous partial skeletons and at least one near-complete skeleton.
- The tail was exceptionally long and reinforced by ossified tendons.
- Several bonebeds contain Deinonychus teeth and remains, demonstrating repeated ecological association but not a unique hunting strategy.
- Multi-individual accumulations may reflect sociality, drought or transport; the wiki no longer states herd behaviour as established fact.

## Classification

Placed within Iguanodontia by Ostrom and subsequent authors, Tenontosaurus is more basal than typical iguanodonts and lacks their characteristic thumb spike. Phylogenetic analyses consistently recover it as an early-diverging iguanodontian, providing an informative outgroup for understanding the evolution of the more derived hadrosauroid lineage.

## Open questions

- The causes of multi-individual accumulations and predator-prey associations remain debated.
- The validity and limits of the second named species T. dossi require further revision.
