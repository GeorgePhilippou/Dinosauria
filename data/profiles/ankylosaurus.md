## Overview

Ankylosaurus magniventris was the largest known ankylosaurid, reaching about six to eight metres, from the latest Cretaceous Hell Creek Formation (~68–66 Ma). Its massive bony tail club — formed by two large oval knobs fused to a rigid tail handle — could swing laterally to deliver bone-breaking blows to attacking Tyrannosaurus. Ankylosaurus was one of the last surviving non-avian dinosaurs before the K-Pg mass extinction.

Despite being the most famous ankylosaur, Ankylosaurus is known from surprisingly little material: three incomplete skulls and fragmentary postcranial remains. No complete skeleton exists. Most popular reconstructions are based heavily on the better-known Euoplocephalus, which is known from dozens of specimens and is a close but smaller relative.

## Anatomy & life

Ankylosaurus was a broad, low-slung, four-legged plant-eater that lived in western North America during the final two million years of the Cretaceous. It is the largest known ankylosaurid: recent estimates put the biggest skull at nearly 65 centimetres wide and the whole animal at perhaps six to eight metres long and several tonnes, though no complete skeleton exists to measure directly.

Its skull was wide and triangular, with horn-like bosses at the back corners, a beak at the front, small leaf-shaped teeth and a complicated looping nasal passage. The body carried bony plates set in the skin, and the tail ended in a heavy club formed from fused vertebrae and osteoderms, capable of powerful sideways swings.

Because the three main specimens preserve different parts of the body, restorations of the full animal stitch together non-overlapping material and borrow armour arrangements from better-known relatives such as Euoplocephalus. Ankylosaurus is a rare animal in its formations, and where it fits ecologically among the more common horned and duck-billed dinosaurs is still debated.

- A wide, low skull with horn-like bosses at each rear corner and a looping, convoluted nasal passage.
- A heavy tail club of fused vertebrae and osteoderms, known from referred material rather than the holotype.
- The largest known ankylosaurid, yet documented by only a handful of incomplete individuals.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Small leaf-shaped teeth and a broad beak indicate low browsing on soft vegetation. No gut contents are known for Ankylosaurus itself.

**Movement: Quadrupedal.** Limb bones from referred specimens and the ankylosaurid body plan support four-legged walking. Speed and gait are modelled rather than observed.

**Appearance: Partly reconstructed.** Skull, tail club and many osteoderms are preserved, but no specimen shows the armour in place across the body, so its exact layout follows Euoplocephalus and other ankylosaurids.

## Fossil record

Ankylosaurus is known from a holotype with partial skull and forequarters, a second specimen with a complete skull, tail club and limbs, and a third large skull, plus scattered osteoderms and teeth. Together they cover most of the body, but no individual preserves the armour in place or the skeleton entire.

Two complete and one partial skull, isolated teeth, cervical to caudal vertebrae including the tail club, ribs, both shoulder blades, partial limb bones and many osteoderms, spread across specimens from Montana, Wyoming and Alberta.

Because the specimens preserve different regions and the osteoderms were found displaced, the shape of the whole animal and the layout of its armour are reconstructed by combining specimens and comparing with relatives.

Armour plates, skull bones and tail-club elements define the heavily armoured body plan.

## Where it lived

Latest Cretaceous western North American floodplains.

## Classification

Ankylosaurus is the name-bearing genus of Ankylosauridae and Ankylosaurinae, the club-tailed armoured dinosaurs. Its membership of that group is secure; its position among the latest Cretaceous North American ankylosaurines is analysis-dependent, and its unusual skull and armour set it apart from close relatives.

## Open questions

### How was the armour arranged?

Osteoderms are known from all three main specimens, but none preserves them in place across the body. Published reconstructions differ, and the 2017 revision proposed a more Euoplocephalus-like arrangement than earlier models.

### How big was it really?

Estimates range from about six to eight metres and from under five to about eight tonnes, depending on which specimen is scaled and how. There is no complete skeleton to settle the question.

### Why is it so rare?

Ankylosaurus is scarce compared with Triceratops and Edmontosaurus in the same rocks. It may have lived in upland or inland habitats less often preserved, but this is a hypothesis rather than an observation.
