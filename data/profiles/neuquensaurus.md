## Overview

Neuquensaurus (meaning 'Neuquén River lizard') was a saltasaurid titanosaur sauropod from the Late Cretaceous (approximately 86–66 million years ago) of Argentina and possibly Uruguay. At around 15 metres in length it was a relatively compact member of its family. Fossils come primarily from the Neuquén Group deposits of Neuquén and Río Negro Provinces, as well as the Lecho Formation in Salta. It was a quadrupedal herbivore that moved in gregarious groups.

Originally described by Lydekker in 1893, Neuquensaurus is notable for possessing osteoderms — bony dermal scutes — that provided protective armour across its back, an unusual trait for sauropods. This links it closely to Saltasaurus and other derived titanosaurs in which osteoderms are known. Its long stratigraphic range in Argentina, spanning the Santonian through Maastrichtian, makes it one of the better-attested Patagonian titanosaurs.

## Anatomy & life

Neuquensaurus is one of the sauropods for which bony osteoderms — embedded scutes that form a mosaic of armour over the back — are directly documented. This feature, also seen in the closely related Saltasaurus, appears to be a derived characteristic of the saltasaurid lineage within titanosaurs and may have provided protection from large theropods.

PBDB ecospace data indicates that Neuquensaurus was gregarious, consistent with bone-bed and track-site evidence for titanosaur herding behaviour. Its herbivorous diet would have focused on the varied Late Cretaceous flora of Patagonia. The relatively modest body size compared to some titanosaurs may reflect ecological or island-effect constraints in its Patagonian range.

## Fossil record

Neuquensaurus australis was first described by Richard Lydekker in 1893 from material collected in Patagonia, Argentina. It was subsequently reassigned among several genera before being recognised as a distinct saltasaurid. The genus name reflects the Neuquén region of Patagonia, and the species name australis means 'southern'.

Material referred to Neuquensaurus has had a complex history, having been placed in Titanosaurus, Laplatasaurus, and other genera at various times. PBDB credits Powell (1992) with the attribution currently in use. The species N. robustus was also named and its relationship to N. australis continues to be discussed in the context of individual variation and sexual dimorphism.

Abundant disarticulated cranial-limited and postcranial remains from multiple individuals, plus osteoderms.

- The available material documents much of the postcranial skeleton and includes osteoderms.
- Historical species and referrals overlap with the complicated genus Saltasaurus and require specimen-level care.
- Its relatively small adult dimensions are supported by mature bone histology, although exact mass estimates vary.

## Where it lived

Campanian-age red-beds of the Neuquén Group that have produced the bulk of Neuquensaurus skeletal material.

Secure material spans Upper Cretaceous units of the Neuquen Basin; a Maastrichtian-only range is too narrow.

## Open questions

- Association of older collections to species and individuals is imperfect.
- Exact species boundaries within the historical material remain debated.
