## Overview

Pachyrhinosaurus was a centrosaurine ceratopsid represented by three named North American species and large bonebeds. The type species P. canadensis was named from the St. Mary River Formation of Alberta, with referred material from the Horseshoe Canyon Formation, and bears a thick nasal boss rather than a conventional nasal horn.

Pachyrhinosaurus ("thick-nosed lizard") was a large centrosaurine ceratopsid from the late Campanian and early Maastrichtian of Alberta and Alaska (~73–69 Ma), unusual for lacking a prominent nasal horn in adults — instead bearing a thick bony boss on the nose and a prominent frontal boss between the eyes. Three species are known, including P. perotorum from Alaska, which lived north of the Arctic Circle.

The Alaskan Pachyrhinosaurus population provides some of the best evidence that large dinosaurs could inhabit polar regions: the North Slope of Alaska was above the Arctic Circle at ~70 Ma, and Pachyrhinosaurus experienced months of polar darkness and cool temperatures, demonstrating that at least some dinosaurs could thrive in conditions far from the subtropical environments typically imagined.

## Anatomy & life

Pachyrhinosaurus was a large, four-legged horned dinosaur of the centrosaurine group that lived in western North America during the last part of the Cretaceous. Adults were about six to eight metres long, with a deep body, a short tail and a heavy skull carrying a parrot-like beak and batteries of shearing teeth.

Instead of the nose horn seen in most ceratopsids, adult Pachyrhinosaurus grew a massive, flattened, rugose boss over the snout, with smaller bosses over the eyes. The frill carried a pair of long horns that curved upwards, plus a variable set of spikes and hooks along its rim; the three named species differ chiefly in the arrangement of these frill ornaments.

Because it is known from bonebeds containing many individuals of different sizes, the genus provides an unusually clear record of how the boss and frill developed as animals grew. Juveniles had small, horn-like nasal projections that were only later replaced by the thick boss. Whether the animals lived in large herds, and how the boss was used, are interpretations rather than direct observations.

- A thick, flattened nasal boss replaced the nose horn in adults, giving the genus its name, meaning "thick-nosed lizard".
- Three named species, P. canadensis, P. lakustai and P. perotorum, differ mainly in the shape and arrangement of frill ornament.
- Multi-individual bonebeds in Alberta and Alaska record growth from juvenile to adult in a single ceratopsid.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** The rostral beak and dental batteries with shearing wear facets are typical of ceratopsids that processed tough vegetation. No gut contents are known.

**Movement: Quadrupedal.** Limb and girdle bones from bonebeds show the robust, four-legged build shared by all ceratopsids. Walking speed and gait are modelled rather than observed.

**Appearance: Skull ornament well documented.** The shape of the boss and frill is directly known from many skulls, but the keratin covering of the boss, skin texture and colour are inferred.

## Fossil record

Pachyrhinosaurus is one of the better-sampled ceratopsids. Skulls of all growth stages and abundant disarticulated postcranial bones are known from bonebeds in Alberta and Alaska, although no single complete articulated skeleton has been described.

Complete and partial skulls of juveniles, subadults and adults, isolated frill and boss elements, and disarticulated vertebrae, ribs, girdle and limb bones from bonebeds at Scabby Butte and Pipestone Creek in Alberta and the Kikak-Tegoseak Quarry in Alaska.

Because bonebed material is disarticulated, most postcranial bones cannot be tied to particular skulls, and body proportions are reconstructed from mixed individuals.

Bosses instead of long nose horns and rich bonebeds document centrosaurine diversity.

- Bonebeds contain many disarticulated individuals and growth stages, not one herd frozen in behaviour.
- Boss and frill ornament changed markedly during growth.
- Migration and horn-covering shape are plausible but not directly demonstrated.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous northern floodplain environments, including high-latitude ecosystems.

The genus-level range spans late Campanian P. lakustai through Maastrichtian P. canadensis and P. perotorum; species ages must not be merged.

## Classification

Pachyrhinosaurus is a centrosaurine ceratopsid and the name-bearer of the tribe Pachyrhinosaurini, a group of boss-bearing centrosaurines that also includes Achelousaurus. Its three species are consistently recovered as each other's closest relatives.

## Open questions

### What covered the boss in life?

The rugose bone surface implies a keratinous covering, but its thickness and shape, whether a flat pad or a horn-like structure, cannot be read directly from the fossils.

### Did it migrate?

The presence of the genus in both Alberta and Arctic Alaska has prompted suggestions of seasonal migration, but bonebeds record where animals died, not where they travelled.

### How many species are there?

Three species are named, but ornament changes with growth and varies between individuals, so some referrals of isolated skulls and bonebed material remain under study.
