## Overview

Podokesaurus was a small, slender predatory dinosaur from the Early Jurassic of Massachusetts. It is one of very few dinosaur skeletons from the Connecticut River valley, a region far better known for its thousands of fossil footprints.

It was described in 1911 by Mignon Talbot, a geology professor at Mount Holyoke College, making her the first woman to name a non-avian dinosaur. Talbot believed the rocks were Triassic; they are now known to be Early Jurassic.

## Anatomy & life

The skeleton showed a lightly built, two-legged animal with hollow bones, a long neck and tail and long, slender hind limbs, the body plan of the coelophysoids, a group of early carnivorous theropods. Its size is usually given as around a metre long, but because the original bones are lost this cannot be checked.

Like its relatives it probably hunted small animals such as lizards, early mammal relatives and insects. The small three-toed footprints called Grallator, which are abundant in the same rocks, were made by animals of similar shape, though they cannot be matched to Podokesaurus itself.

## Fossil record

Talbot found the skeleton in a sandstone boulder near the college in 1910. It preserved much of the body, including parts of the vertebral column, limbs and hips, but no skull. In 1917 a fire destroyed the college building where it was kept, and the specimen was lost.

Casts and photographs made before the fire still exist, and later researchers have studied these. They show a coelophysoid, but they are not detailed enough to confirm features that would clearly separate Podokesaurus from Coelophysis or other close relatives.

## Where it lived

The Portland Formation fills the Hartford Basin, one of a chain of rift valleys that opened as North America began to separate from Africa. It records rivers, seasonal lakes and floodplains under a warm climate. Its footprints show that small and medium-sized theropods were common, alongside early sauropodomorphs and ornithischians.

## Classification

Podokesaurus is placed in Coelophysoidea, a group of early theropods that includes Coelophysis and Segisaurus. Some researchers have suggested it is the same as Coelophysis, while others treat it as an indeterminate coelophysoid whose name cannot be tied to a unique set of features. Without the original fossil, neither view can be fully tested.

## Open questions

### Is it a separate genus?

Casts suggest a coelophysoid but may not preserve features unique to Podokesaurus, so it may be an indeterminate specimen or a synonym of Coelophysis.

### How big was it?

The commonly quoted size of about a metre cannot be re-measured from the lost original.
