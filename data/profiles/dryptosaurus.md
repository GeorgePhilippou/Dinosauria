## Overview

Dryptosaurus (meaning 'tearing lizard') was a large carnivorous theropod from the Late Cretaceous of eastern North America, living approximately 84–66 million years ago. Known primarily from a partial skeleton found in New Jersey, it is estimated at about 6.4 metres in length. Dryptosaurus is historically significant as one of the earliest large theropods discovered in North America, originally unearthed in 1866, and it may represent an eastern branch of tyrannosauroid evolution largely separate from the better-known western taxa.

Dryptosaurus aquilunguis was originally named Laelaps by Edward Drinker Cope in 1866, but this name was already occupied by a mite genus. Othniel Charles Marsh renamed it Dryptosaurus in 1877, making it part of the famous Cope–Marsh 'Bone Wars'. PBDB data places it within Tyrannosauroidea, and its long forelimbs and large hand claws distinguish it from the short-armed tyrannosaurs of the western interior, hinting at the ecological diversity of eastern Laramidia or Appalachia.

## Anatomy & life

Dryptosaurus inhabited Appalachia, the eastern landmass of Late Cretaceous North America isolated from the western interior seaway. Unlike the contemporaneous western tyrannosaurids, which had reduced forelimbs, Dryptosaurus appears to have retained longer arms with large curved claws — a feature that may reflect its distinct evolutionary lineage rather than a basal tyrannosauroid grade.

Dryptosaurus became culturally iconic through a famous 1897 painting by Charles R. Knight, depicting two Dryptosaurus individuals leaping and grappling in combat — one of the earliest attempts to portray dinosaurs as dynamic, active animals. This image helped shape twentieth-century popular perceptions of theropod behaviour.

The known fossil material of Dryptosaurus consists primarily of partial postcranial bones and fragmentary skull elements, making a full anatomical reconstruction difficult. Referred specimens from Wyoming and Texas are treated with caution, and the overall rarity of large theropod fossils from eastern North America leaves many aspects of Dryptosaurus's biology poorly constrained.

## Fossil record

The holotype of Dryptosaurus was discovered in 1866 in the Barnsboro Marl of New Jersey and described by Edward Drinker Cope as Laelaps aquilunguis. When Othniel Charles Marsh realised the name Laelaps was preoccupied by a mite, he formally renamed the dinosaur Dryptosaurus in 1877. The resulting dispute over naming priority was part of the broader Cope–Marsh rivalry that transformed North American palaeontology.

One fragmentary but diagnostic postcranial skeleton.

- The holotype includes limb, girdle, vertebral and hand elements but no diagnostic complete skull.
- A large manual claw is real, but the full proportions and use of the forelimb are uncertain.
- Isolated eastern North American theropod remains should not automatically be referred to Dryptosaurus.

## Where it lived

Principal locality; the holotype partial skeleton was recovered from Late Cretaceous marine and coastal deposits of what is now New Jersey.

The secure type occurrence is Maastrichtian; a broader 84-66 Ma range conflates uncertain referrals.

## Open questions

- No skull is securely known.
- Precise tyrannosauroid relationships and adult size are unresolved.
