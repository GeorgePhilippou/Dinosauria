## Overview

Plateosaurus engelhardti was one of the most successful dinosaurs of the Late Triassic — a large prosauropod reaching up to seven metres, known from over 100 individuals across central Europe (Germany, France, Switzerland). It was a committed biped that could not walk on four legs, despite early reconstructions showing a quadrupedal posture, and its body plan presages the giant sauropods that would evolve from its lineage.

Mass-death sites in Germany and Switzerland — where dozens of individuals of all sizes are found together — suggest many individuals became mired in soft ground, possibly during seasonal migrations toward water in a semi-arid Triassic landscape. Bone texture studies show that Plateosaurus grew more like a reptile (variable growth rate) than like a bird or mammal (rapid determinate growth).

## Anatomy & life

Plateosaurus was a long-necked, small-headed plant-eater from the Late Triassic of Central Europe, one of the first large dinosaurs in the fossil record. Mature animals ranged from roughly five to ten metres long, a spread that bone histology attributes to individuals growing to very different final sizes rather than to several species.

Digital modelling of complete skeletons shows that its forelimbs could not be turned palm-down to bear weight, so adults walked on their hind legs; the large thumb claw and grasping hand were free for other uses. Leaf-shaped, coarsely serrated teeth indicate a plant diet, probably taken from shrubs and low trees.

The genus was named in 1837 from fragmentary bones near Nuremberg, but its fame rests on the mass graves at Trossingen and Halberstadt in Germany and Frick in Switzerland, where dozens of animals apparently became mired in mud. The species names have a tangled history, settled in 2019 when P. trossingensis and its complete Stuttgart skeleton were fixed as the reference for the genus.

- Dozens of skeletons from bonebeds document every part of the body, from skull to tail tip.
- Adults of the same age reached very different sizes, a rare direct record of developmental plasticity in a dinosaur.
- Forelimb anatomy rules out weight-bearing on the hands, supporting habitual bipedality.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Serrated leaf-shaped teeth and a downturned jaw joint fit cropping vegetation; gastroliths once claimed are not confirmed.

**Movement: Bipedal.** The hands could not pronate to take weight, so adults moved on two legs; trackways are not securely attributed.

**Appearance: Skeleton fully known.** Proportions are secure from complete skeletons; skin, colour and any soft-tissue outline are not preserved.

## Fossil record

Plateosaurus is known from more than a hundred individuals, many of them articulated and nearly complete, recovered from bonebeds at Trossingen, Halberstadt and Frick. It is the most completely documented dinosaur of the Triassic.

Complete skulls with teeth, full vertebral series, ribs and gastralia, shoulder and hip girdles, forelimbs with the enlarged thumb claw, hindlimbs and feet, from many articulated skeletons of different sizes.

The abundance is concentrated in a few bonebeds, so the genus is exceptionally well known anatomically while the original Heroldsberg type material remains too fragmentary to compare closely.

Growth series and skeletons document a large bipedal sauropodomorph before true giant sauropods.

## Where it lived

Late Triassic European terrestrial basins with mass-death assemblages.

## Classification

Plateosaurus is the name-bearing genus of Plateosauridae, a group of Late Triassic sauropodomorphs outside Sauropoda. It is the reference taxon for early sauropodomorph anatomy. Since 2019 P. trossingensis has been the type species, with P. engelhardti and P. gracilis also recognised by many workers.

## Open questions

### How many species are there?

P. trossingensis, P. engelhardti and P. gracilis are the main names in use, but the fragmentary Heroldsberg type material makes some assignments hard to test.

### Why do adults differ so much in size?

Histology shows animals of similar age stopping growth at very different sizes. Whether environment, sex or something else drove this is unknown.

### How did the bonebeds form?

Animals trapped in mud, seasonal drought and flood transport have all been proposed for Trossingen and Frick; the mud-trap model best explains the upright, partly articulated skeletons.
