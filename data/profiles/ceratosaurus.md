## Overview

Ceratosaurus nasicornis was a horned ceratosaurian theropod from the Morrison Formation of western North America. The holotype preserves a skull and much of the skeleton; additional specimens improve coverage, while several proposed species and referrals remain disputed.

Ceratosaurus nasicornis was a medium-large Late Jurassic theropod of the Morrison Formation, about six metres long, recognised by a prominent nasal horn, smaller paired brow horns, and a unique row of small osteoderms along the dorsal midline. It coexisted with the larger and more common Allosaurus.

Ceratosaurus belongs to Ceratosauria — a lineage that diverged from the main tetanuran theropods early in the Jurassic. Its comparatively longer teeth and possible association with fish-bearing deposits have led to suggestions of a partially aquatic hunting strategy, though this remains debated.

## Anatomy & life

Ceratosaurus was a medium-sized meat-eating dinosaur that lived alongside Allosaurus and Torvosaurus on the floodplains of the Late Jurassic Morrison Formation. The name-bearing skeleton from Garden Park, Colorado, belonged to an animal about five to six metres long; isolated bones from Utah and Colorado suggest that some individuals grew larger, although no complete large adult is known.

It is instantly recognisable from a blade-like horn core on the nasal bones and a pair of low ridges above the eyes, all of which are directly preserved. Its teeth were unusually long for the size of the skull, its arms were short with a four-fingered hand, and its tail was deep and flexible. A row of small bony plates ran along the back, a feature otherwise almost unknown among theropods.

Ceratosaurus belongs to Ceratosauria, a lineage of predators that split early from the tetanuran line leading to birds. It was less common than Allosaurus in the same rocks, and its long teeth and deep tail have prompted suggestions, still untested, that it hunted different prey or in different settings.

- A midline nasal horn core and paired brow ridges are preserved on the holotype skull.
- Very long, blade-like teeth in a comparatively short, deep skull.
- A row of small osteoderms along the back, associated with the holotype skeleton.

**Feeding: Carnivore.** Recurved, serrated teeth and a deep skull indicate a predatory diet. No bite-marked prey or gut contents are directly attributed to Ceratosaurus.

**Movement: Bipedal.** Long hindlimbs, short forelimbs and a fused pelvis in the holotype leave no doubt about two-legged walking; speed is not measured.

**Appearance: Horn and osteoderms preserved.** The bony horn core and dorsal osteoderms are real; the keratin covering, skin texture and colour are inferred.

## Fossil record

Ceratosaurus rests on the Garden Park holotype, an associated skeleton with a nearly complete skull collected in 1883–1884. Later specimens from Colorado, Utah and possibly Portugal add larger bones and partial skulls, but none is as complete as the first.

The holotype USNM 4735 preserves a crushed but nearly complete skull with lower jaws, most of the vertebral column, the pelvis, hindlimbs and parts of the forelimbs, together with a row of small osteoderms. Referred specimens add partial skulls, jaws, vertebrae and limb bones.

The holotype skull was distorted during burial, so some proportions in mounted reconstructions are corrected estimates. Larger referred specimens are incomplete, which is why the maximum size of the genus is uncertain.

Nasal horn, blade-like teeth and distinctive skull anatomy identify this ceratosaurian theropod.

- The midline nasal horn core and paired orbital rugosities are directly preserved.
- A midline row of small osteoderms is associated with the holotype, but their complete arrangement is unknown.

## Where it lived

Late Jurassic ecosystems shared with larger allosauroids and sauropod prey.

Secure C. nasicornis material is Kimmeridgian-Tithonian in the Morrison Formation.

## Classification

Ceratosaurus is the name-giving member of Ceratosauria, an early-branching group of theropods distinct from the allosaurs and tyrannosaurs. Within Ceratosauria it is usually placed close to Genyodectes and outside the abelisauroid radiation.

## Open questions

### How many species were there?

Ceratosaurus magnicornis and C. dentisulcatus were named from Fruita and the Cleveland-Lloyd Quarry, but several studies treat their differences as growth or individual variation within C. nasicornis.

### Did it live outside North America?

A partial skeleton from the Lourinhã Formation of Portugal has been referred to Ceratosaurus, but the referral rests on limited overlapping material and is not universally accepted.

### What was the horn for?

The nasal horn is too thin to have been an effective weapon. Display is the usual interpretation, but there is no direct evidence of how it was used or whether it differed between sexes.
