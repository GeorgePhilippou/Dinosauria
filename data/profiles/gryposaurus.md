## Overview

Gryposaurus notabilis ("hook-nosed lizard") was a large flat-headed hadrosaur from the Campanian of Alberta (~76 Ma), distinguished by a prominent arch or bump on the nasal bones — the "hook" referred to in its name. At about eight metres it was one of the most common hadrosaurs in the Belly River Group fauna alongside Corythosaurus and Parasaurolophus.

Multiple species of Gryposaurus are known from the Campanian of North America, spanning several million years of the late Campanian epoch. Its geographic range and temporal persistence suggest it was an ecologically flexible herbivore capable of adapting to changing Late Cretaceous environments.

## Anatomy & life

Gryposaurus had the standard hadrosaur dental battery of hundreds of teeth for grinding tough vegetation. The nasal arch is the only significant external distinction from other saurolophine hadrosaurs. The tail was deep and muscular — used for swimming, as in modern crocodilians — and the hands bore a hoof-like weight-bearing pad for quadrupedal locomotion.

The Dinosaur Park Formation hosted multiple hadrosaur species simultaneously, including both flat-headed saurolophines (Gryposaurus) and elaborately crested lambeosaurines (Corythosaurus, Lambeosaurus, Parasaurolophus). Niche partitioning among these diverse herbivores likely involved habitat preferences, dietary differences and behavioural segregation rather than direct competition for identical resources.

## Fossil record

Named by Lambe in 1914 from Dinosaur Park Formation material; multiple species have been described across the Campanian of North America (G. notabilis, G. incurvimanus, G. monumentensis). The arched nasal region is its diagnostic feature — smaller in juveniles, more pronounced in adults — and may have served in intraspecific head-butting or species recognition.

Numerous skulls and skeletons across several growth stages.

The distinctive arched nasal region and saurolophine dentition are diagnostic; bonebeds document gregarious behaviour.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous coastal plain environments of western North America.

## Open questions

- Species boundaries and synonymy, including G. incurvimanus, remain debated.
- Soft-tissue form over the nasal arch is unknown.
