## Overview

Tarbosaurus bataar was a large tyrannosaurine from the Nemegt Formation of Mongolia, known from numerous skulls and skeletons across growth stages.

Tarbosaurus bataar was the apex predator of the Late Cretaceous Nemegt Formation of Mongolia (~70 Ma) — the Asian ecological equivalent of Tyrannosaurus, reaching similar size and filling an identical predatory role in a contemporaneous but geographically distinct ecosystem. Its skull is proportionally even narrower than T. rex and its forelimbs particularly reduced.

Whether Tarbosaurus should be synonymised with Tyrannosaurus (as T. bataar) has been debated since the 1990s. Most current analyses treat them as separate genera based on consistent skull proportions, biogeography and the principle that combining them would require unexplained intercontinental dispersal. The Beringian land bridge may have allowed some exchange, but differences between the two are well supported.

## Anatomy & life

Tarbosaurus was a very large two-legged predator and the biggest carnivore of the Nemegt ecosystem. The largest individuals are estimated at ten to twelve metres long and several tonnes in mass, a little smaller than the largest Tyrannosaurus. The skull is deep but narrower than that of its North American cousin, with a locking joint between the upper jaw bones that stiffened the snout.

Its arms were the shortest relative to body size of any tyrannosaurid, ending in two-fingered hands. The braincase preserves natural casts showing large olfactory regions, consistent with a strong sense of smell, while the eyes faced more sideways than in Tyrannosaurus.

Juveniles were slender, long-legged animals with low, narrow skulls and blade-like teeth, and adults became far more robust. That transformation, documented by a two- to three-year-old skeleton, means Tarbosaurus probably occupied different feeding roles at different stages of life. Bite marks on hadrosaur and sauropod bones from the Nemegt Formation are usually attributed to it, but which animal made them cannot be proven.

- A deep, narrow skull with a rigid snout and a distinctive locking mechanism between the maxilla and the jugal bones.
- The proportionally smallest forelimbs of any tyrannosaurid, ending in two functional fingers.
- One of the most complete tyrannosaurid growth series known, from a juvenile a few years old to full-sized adults.

**Feeding: Meat-eater.** Thick serrated teeth and a heavily built skull indicate a predator or scavenger of large animals. Tooth-marked Saurolophus and sauropod bones from the same rocks are consistent with, but do not prove, Tarbosaurus feeding.

**Movement: Bipedal.** Complete hindlimbs are preserved in several individuals. Adult speed is not measurable from bone and biomechanical estimates vary.

**Appearance: Skeletal outline supported.** Proportions come from articulated skeletons. Scaly skin is inferred from impressions in related tyrannosaurids rather than from Tarbosaurus itself; colour is unknown.

## Fossil record

Tarbosaurus bataar is one of the best-sampled large theropods. Soviet, Polish, Mongolian and Japanese expeditions have recovered more than thirty specimens from the Nemegt Formation, including near-complete adults and a small juvenile skeleton.

Complete skulls with dentition and braincases, full vertebral columns, ribs and gastralia, pectoral and pelvic girdles, both forelimbs with two-fingered hands, complete hindlimbs and feet, from small juveniles to the largest adults.

A large proportion of the specimens were collected before modern excavation standards and some are held far from their type locality, so provenance details for older material can be imprecise.

A growth series documents ontogenetic changes similar to Tyrannosaurus; detailed skull comparison supports its separation from T. rex.

- Most current analyses retain Tarbosaurus as distinct from Tyrannosaurus, though the combination Tyrannosaurus bataar has historical use.
- Juveniles had proportionally longer, lower skulls and changed feeding mechanics through growth.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous Nemegt floodplain ecosystem of Mongolia with hadrosaurs, sauropods and ornithomimosaurs.

Nemegt Formation specimens are Maastrichtian, about 70-66 Ma.

## Classification

Tarbosaurus is a tyrannosaurine tyrannosaurid, the Asian sister taxon of Tyrannosaurus in most analyses. Some workers treat it as a species of Tyrannosaurus (T. bataar); most keep the genera separate on cranial differences, and the question remains open.

## Open questions

### Is Tarbosaurus really a separate genus?

Tarbosaurus and Tyrannosaurus differ in skull architecture and forelimb proportions, but they are very close relatives. Whether that difference merits a separate genus is a judgement rather than a fact, and Tyrannosaurus bataar has been used by some authors.

### What did the juveniles eat?

The two- to three-year-old skeleton MPC-D 107/7 has a slender skull and blade-like teeth unlike those of adults, so juveniles probably took smaller, different prey. The exact diet is not preserved.

### How many species are in the sample?

Several names were once applied to Mongolian tyrannosaurids of different sizes. Most are now read as growth stages of one species, but the small number of well-studied juveniles leaves some room for doubt.
