## Overview

Parasaurolophus walkeri bore the most distinctive cranial crest of any hadrosaur — a long, hollow tube curving backwards from the skull, in large adults reaching over one metre. The crest contained a looping nasal passage connected to the respiratory tract; when air was blown through it, the crest would have produced a deep, foghorn-like resonance specific to each species and sex, used for communication across the forested Late Cretaceous floodplains.

Three species are recognised from the Late Campanian of North America (~76–73 Ma), differing in crest length and shape. Computer modelling of the nasal passage geometry has allowed researchers to synthesise the probable call of Parasaurolophus — the most direct acoustic reconstruction of any prehistoric animal — producing a low-frequency sound in the 30–80 Hz range.

## Anatomy & life

Parasaurolophus was a large lambeosaurine hadrosaurid, a duck-billed plant-eater with a broad toothless beak and dental batteries of hundreds of tightly packed grinding teeth. Estimates of roughly 9–10 metres in length rest on the incomplete Alberta type skeleton scaled against better-known relatives, so the figure is an approximation rather than a measurement.

Its defining feature is the crest: a long, backward-curving tube of bone rising from the snout, hollow inside and threaded by the nasal passages. In the type species the tube reached well beyond the back of the skull. Growth-series specimens, including a very young animal from Utah, show that the crest began as a low dome and lengthened through life.

Acoustic modelling of the internal passages supports the idea that the crest acted as a resonating chamber that lowered and amplified the animal's calls, and its size and shape also make visual display likely. These remain functional hypotheses supported by anatomy; no call can be reconstructed with certainty, and colour, skin patterning and any soft-tissue extension of the crest are unknown.

- A hollow, tubular crest housed elongated nasal passages and changed markedly in shape during growth.
- Three species are separated by crest curvature and by their stratigraphic position in Alberta, New Mexico and Utah.
- Skin impressions on the type specimen show a covering of small, non-overlapping scales on parts of the body.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** The toothless beak and grinding dental batteries are typical of hadrosaurids and indicate processing of tough vegetation; the actual plants eaten are not preserved.

**Movement: Two- and four-legged.** Hadrosaurids could walk on all fours and rise onto the hind legs; Parasaurolophus limb material is incomplete, so this is inferred from close relatives.

**Appearance: Crest and body outline known.** The bony crest and skin-scale impressions are directly preserved; colour, any soft-tissue crest extension and pattern are not.

## Fossil record

Parasaurolophus is known from an Alberta type skeleton with skin impressions, several skulls from New Mexico and Utah, and a nearly complete juvenile. Good cranial material supports the crest studies, but no adult skeleton is complete.

The holotype ROM 768 is a skull with the front half of the skeleton, including the neck, back, shoulder girdle, forelimbs and pelvis, with patches of scaly skin. Other species rest chiefly on skulls, notably a near-complete skull of P. tubicen and the P. cyrtocristatus type skull, and a young individual from Utah preserves most of its skeleton.

Because most specimens are skulls, statements about body proportions lean heavily on the single Alberta skeleton and on other hadrosaurids.

Crest anatomy is central to sound, display and species-recognition interpretations.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous North American coastal plain and floodplain environments.

## Classification

Parasaurolophus is a lambeosaurine hadrosaurid and the namesake of Parasaurolophini, the tube-crested group that also includes Charonosaurus from China and Tlatolophus from Mexico. Its placement within Lambeosaurinae is stable; relationships among the three species vary between analyses.

## Open questions

### What did the crest sound like?

Resonance modelling gives plausible frequency ranges for the nasal passages, but the larynx and soft tissues are unknown, so actual calls cannot be reconstructed.

### Was there a soft-tissue frill behind the crest?

A notch in the back vertebrae of the type specimen has been read as an anchor for a skin flap or a resting point for the crest, but neither idea is confirmed.

### Do the three species belong to one lineage?

Crest shape and rock age separate the species, but their relationships to one another and to the Asian Charonosaurus differ between studies.
