## Overview

Ornithomimus edmontonicus was an ostrich-sized ornithomimosaur from the Late Cretaceous Horseshoe Canyon Formation of Alberta, about 68 million years ago. Exceptional specimens from the Dinosaur Park Formation preserve integument showing that adults were feathered on the arms (wing-like feathers) while their bodies bore scales, a pattern strikingly similar to modern ratite birds.

Ornithomimus is one of the best-documented North American ornithomimosaurs, known from multiple complete specimens spanning different growth stages. The arm-feather pattern in adults may have been used for brooding or display, paralleling the functional role of wing feathers in modern ostriches and emus.

## Anatomy & life

Like all ornithomimosaurs, Ornithomimus had a small head, large eyes, a toothless beak, long arms with three subequal fingers, and long gracile hindlimbs. The metatarsal proportions are among the most bird-like of any non-avian dinosaur. There is evidence of a keratinous beak sheath.

The toothless beak and arm musculature suggest omnivory or herbivory rather than active predation. Ornithomimus coexisted with Albertosaurus and Troodon in a coastal lowland environment of rivers, swamps and open ground. Its rapid locomotion would have been its primary defence against predators.

## Fossil record

Named by Marsh in 1890, Ornithomimus became better known through Lambe's Early 20th-century work on Alberta material. The feathered specimens described by van der Reest and colleagues in 2016 were the first direct evidence of integument in ornithomimosaurs and revealed a striking age-related pattern: juveniles lacked arm feathers; adults bore them. This mirrors the ontogenetic changes in modern ratites.

Multiple articulated skeletons for O. edmontonicus, including feathered specimens; fragmentary O. velox type.

Long limbs, toothless jaws and feather traces support bird-like body covering in ornithomimosaurs.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous western Canadian floodplains.
