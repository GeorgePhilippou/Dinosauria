## Overview

Apatosaurus was a robust diplodocid sauropod from the Morrison Formation of western North America. Multiple partial skeletons document most of the body, but skulls are rare and species-level referrals have been repeatedly revised.

Apatosaurus ajax was one of the most heavily built sauropods of the Late Jurassic Morrison Formation, reaching 21–22 metres and 22 tonnes. Long misidentified as "Brontosaurus" — a name used for over a century before being synonymised in 1903 and partially rehabilitated in 2015 for a distinct species — Apatosaurus was a diplodocid that carried its neck horizontally and fed on low vegetation, unlike the high-browsing Brachiosaurus sharing the same ecosystem.

Its whip-like tail could potentially crack at supersonic speeds, generating a sonic boom. The neck vertebrae were massively built yet heavily pneumatised — honeycomb-like air chambers reduced the neck's mass by roughly 50% despite its enormous bulk. Apatosaurus and Diplodocus occupied different feeding zones in the same ecosystem, reducing competition despite their similar overall body plan.

## Anatomy & life

Apatosaurus was one of the heaviest animals of the Morrison Formation, a four-legged plant-eater some twenty-one to twenty-three metres long with a deep body, a long neck of unusually broad, robust vertebrae and a whip-like tail. Mass estimates from the best skeleton cluster around sixteen to twenty-two tonnes, although any figure for a sauropod depends on the method used.

Its head was small and horse-like, with peg-shaped teeth confined to the front of the jaws, a skull type known with confidence only from a few specimens found near articulated skeletons. The neck bones carry huge downward-projecting cervical ribs, which has prompted suggestions about neck-based display or combat, but these remain hypotheses.

Two species are widely accepted, Apatosaurus ajax and A. louisae. For most of the twentieth century Brontosaurus was treated as a junior synonym, but a specimen-level analysis in 2015 argued that it is distinct; that conclusion is influential and still being tested, so the boundary between the two names is a live research question rather than settled fact.

- Exceptionally broad, heavily built neck vertebrae with large, downward-projecting cervical ribs.
- A small, low skull with peg-like teeth restricted to the front of the mouth, known from only a few associated specimens.
- A long, tapering tail ending in slender whip-like vertebrae, shared with other diplodocids.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Peg-like teeth with wear facets indicate cropping vegetation without chewing. Feeding height and preferred plants are inferred from neck posture models, not directly preserved.

**Movement: Quadrupedal.** Complete limbs and girdles from several skeletons, together with Morrison sauropod trackways, establish four-legged walking. Speed is modelled, not measured.

**Appearance: Skeletal outline well supported.** Body proportions are secure. No skin, colour or keratinous structures are known for Apatosaurus itself; small dorsal spines shown in some restorations come from an unidentified diplodocid.

## Fossil record

Apatosaurus is known from multiple partial to nearly complete skeletons across Colorado, Wyoming, Utah and Oklahoma. The A. louisae holotype CM 3018 is one of the best sauropod skeletons ever found. Skull material is scarce, and which specimens belong to which species has been revised repeatedly.

Complete vertebral columns from neck to tail tip, pectoral and pelvic girdles, complete fore- and hindlimbs and feet, chevrons and ribs from several skeletons, plus a small number of skulls found associated with or close to skeletons.

Because so few skulls are known and early mounts used the wrong skull, the head of Apatosaurus is reconstructed from a handful of specimens. Species assignment of many partial skeletons depends on which phylogenetic analysis is followed.

Robust limb bones, vertebrae and tail anatomy support its heavy diplodocid body plan.

- A. ajax is the type species; A. louisae is also widely accepted.
- Brontosaurus was synonymised with Apatosaurus for much of the twentieth century, but a 2015 specimen-level analysis revived it as a separate genus; that result is influential but still tested in later work.

## Where it lived

Late Jurassic Morrison floodplains shared with Camarasaurus, Diplodocus, Stegosaurus and Allosaurus.

Diagnostic material is Kimmeridgian-Tithonian within the Morrison Formation.

## Classification

Apatosaurus is the name-bearing genus of Apatosaurinae within Diplodocidae. Its place among diplodocids is secure; what remains contested is how many species and genera the apatosaurine material represents, including whether Brontosaurus is separate.

## Open questions

### Is Brontosaurus a separate genus?

The 2015 specimen-level analysis by Tschopp and colleagues found enough differences to separate the two. Later studies have accepted, questioned or refined this, so the answer depends on the analysis followed.

### Which skull goes with which skeleton?

Only a few skulls have been found close to Apatosaurus skeletons. Historic mounts carried Camarasaurus-like skulls for decades, and some skull-to-skeleton associations remain uncertain.

### What were the huge cervical ribs for?

The broad, heavily built neck is unlike other diplodocids. Suggestions include neck-to-neck combat or display, but no direct evidence tests these ideas.
