## Overview

Iguanodon bernissartensis was the second dinosaur formally described — named by Gideon Mantell in 1825 from teeth found in Sussex — and one of the three genera that Richard Owen used to define Dinosauria in 1842. In 1878 coal miners in Bernissart, Belgium discovered 38 nearly complete skeletons, providing the first opportunity for scientists to study a dinosaur in full and transforming palaeontology.

For over a century Iguanodon was reconstructed walking upright like a kangaroo, with the famous thumb spike placed on the nose as a horn. Modern analysis shows it walked on four legs with the body held horizontally, and the thumb spike was a true defensive weapon — or possibly a tool for foraging. At 10 metres and 4 tonnes it was one of the largest Early Cretaceous herbivores.

## Anatomy & life

Iguanodon bernissartensis was a bulky ornithopod with a long, deep skull ending in a toothless beak, batteries of leaf-shaped cheek teeth and a heavy tail stiffened by ossified tendons. Its most famous feature is a conical thumb spike, once mistakenly placed on the nose, whose function remains debated.

The forelimbs are long and stout, with a wrist bound by fused bones and three central fingers that bore hoof-like claws. This anatomy supports habitual walking on all fours, while the powerful hindlimbs indicate that the animal could also rear or move bipedally when needed.

Because more than thirty individuals were recovered from the Sainte-Barbe Clays at Bernissart, palaeontologists can study variation between animals of the same species. The length and mass figures quoted for Iguanodon are still estimates: they are scaled from mounted skeletons rather than measured from living animals.

- A conical thumb spike on each hand is the signature feature; its use in defence, feeding or display is interpretation rather than observation.
- Cheek teeth with ridged crowns and a mobile upper jaw allowed the animal to grind plant material, an unusually sophisticated chewing system for a reptile.
- Robust forelimbs and hoof-like middle fingers show that Iguanodon habitually walked on four legs, with bipedal movement available.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Dense batteries of worn cheek teeth and a horny beak are preserved on many skulls. Which plants were eaten is not recorded by gut contents.

**Movement: Mostly quadrupedal.** Hand and wrist anatomy support weight-bearing on the forelimbs; how often the animal walked on two legs is inferred, not observed.

**Appearance: Skeleton well established.** Proportions are among the best known for any dinosaur. Skin texture, colour and any soft-tissue crests are not preserved for this species.

## Fossil record

Iguanodon bernissartensis is known from more than thirty individuals recovered at Bernissart between 1878 and 1881, many of them articulated and near complete, together with skulls, hands, feet and ossified tail tendons.

Articulated skulls with tooth batteries, complete vertebral columns with ossified tendons, shoulder and hip girdles, and full forelimbs and hindlimbs including the spiked hands and three-toed feet, all from many individuals.

The Belgian material is extraordinarily complete, but pyrite decay has damaged some bones since excavation, and specimens from England and Spain referred to the genus are far less complete.

Thumb spikes, beaked jaws and abundant skeletons transformed early dinosaur reconstruction.

## Where it lived

Early Cretaceous European floodplain and wetland settings.

## Classification

Iguanodon is a styracosternan iguanodontian, part of the lineage leading towards hadrosaurs. Modern revisions restrict the genus to I. bernissartensis and a few close relatives; many species once called Iguanodon are now placed in other genera such as Mantellisaurus.

## Open questions

### What was the thumb spike for?

Defence, breaking into plant food and intraspecific combat have all been proposed. None is directly testable from the fossils.

### Did Iguanodon live in herds?

The Bernissart animals were buried together, but the deposit may have accumulated over time. It does not by itself prove permanent group living.

### Which species belong in the genus?

Only I. bernissartensis is uncontroversial. Some historical European specimens and I. galvensis from Spain remain difficult to assign with confidence.
