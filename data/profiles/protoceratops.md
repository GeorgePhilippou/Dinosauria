## Overview

Protoceratops andrewsi was a small sheep-sized ceratopsian (~1.8 m) from the Campanian of Mongolia (~75 Ma), known from hundreds of specimens including eggs, embryos and every growth stage — the most complete growth series of any non-avian dinosaur outside Psittacosaurus. Roy Chapman Andrews' 1923 American Museum expeditions found the first dinosaur eggs ever confirmed as such alongside Protoceratops nests.

A famous specimen preserves Protoceratops and Velociraptor locked together in combat — the "Fighting Dinosaurs" — killed and buried simultaneously during a sandstorm or collapsing dune. Protoceratops had a neck frill but no horns — the skull shows the developmental trajectory from small frill in juveniles to large frill in adults, with males likely having larger frills than females.

## Anatomy & life

Protoceratops was a stocky, four-legged plant-eater about the size of a sheep, with a parrot-like beak, a broad bony frill at the back of the skull and no true horns. It lived in the sand-dune and oasis landscapes recorded by the Djadokhta Formation of Mongolia and equivalent rocks in Inner Mongolia during the Campanian.

Adults reached roughly two metres in length, and hundreds of specimens from hatchlings to old individuals let palaeontologists trace how the frill and nasal boss enlarged with age. Whether the differences seen among adult frills reflect males and females has been tested statistically and not confirmed.

Protoceratops is also famous for what happened around it. The Fighting Dinosaurs specimen preserves one locked in combat with a Velociraptor, and a nest of fifteen juveniles shows that young animals stayed together after hatching. The eggs once attributed to it, however, turned out to belong to the oviraptorosaur Oviraptor.

- A short, deep skull with a hooked beak, cheek teeth in shearing batteries and a frill that grew larger with age.
- Hundreds of individuals across all growth stages, one of the best growth series for any dinosaur.
- Direct associations with a predator and with a clutch of juveniles are preserved in the fossil record.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** A strong beak and shearing tooth batteries fit tough, low-growing vegetation; the particular plants are not preserved.

**Movement: Quadrupedal.** Complete articulated skeletons and limb proportions show a four-legged walker; a hand print has also been referred to the genus.

**Appearance: Skeleton fully known.** Bone proportions are secure; possible skin impressions have been reported, but colour and any tail bristles remain speculative.

## Fossil record

Protoceratops andrewsi is one of the most abundantly preserved dinosaurs anywhere. Articulated skeletons, complete skulls, juveniles in nests and even a specimen locked with a predator have come from the Djadokhta Formation since 1922.

Complete skulls with beaks and tooth batteries, full vertebral columns, girdles, all four limbs with hands and feet, articulated adults, a nest of fifteen juveniles, and a pair of skeletons preserved in combat.

The abundance supports detailed anatomy and growth work; it does not by itself settle questions about sexual dimorphism, social behaviour or how the second species relates to the first.

Growth series, skulls and the Fighting Dinosaurs fossil document ceratopsian anatomy and ecology.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous Gobi Desert dune and interdune settings.

## Classification

Protoceratops is the name-bearing genus of Protoceratopsidae, a group of small hornless neoceratopsians from Late Cretaceous Asia. It lies outside Ceratopsidae, the horned dinosaurs of North America, but shares with them the frill and cheek-tooth batteries. Two species are recognised: P. andrewsi from Mongolia and P. hellenikorhinus from China.

## Open questions

### Were the frill differences male and female?

A 2015 shape analysis of dozens of skulls found continuous variation rather than two clusters, so sexual dimorphism in the frill is not demonstrated.

### What was the frill for?

Display, muscle attachment and species recognition have all been proposed. Its late growth is consistent with a signalling role but does not prove it.

### How did the Fighting Dinosaurs die?

Sudden burial by a collapsing dune or sand flow is the usual explanation for the pair being preserved mid-struggle, but the exact event is inferred, not recorded.
