## Overview

Erketu (named after Tengri, a Mongolian creator-god) was a long-necked sauropod dinosaur from the Cretaceous of Mongolia and possibly China, estimated at around 15 metres in length. Known from articulated cervical vertebrae (neck bones), a sternal plate (breastbone), and an articulated lower leg, Erketu is placed within Euhelopodidae, a family of titanosauriform sauropods from Asia. Its most distinctive feature appears to be an extremely elongated neck, with individual neck vertebrae proportionally longer than those of most other sauropods.

Erketu ellisoni was described by Ksepka and Norell in 2006, based on material from the Baynshire Formation of Dornogov Province, Mongolia. A second occurrence is recorded from the Wangshi Formation of Shandong, China. The unusual neck proportions of Erketu, if correctly attributed, would make it one of the most extremely long-necked sauropods known, exceeding even Mamenchisaurus in relative neck length — though interpretations depend on specimen completeness.

## Anatomy & life

The cervical vertebrae of Erketu are proportionally very long relative to their width, suggesting the animal possessed a neck that was extreme even by sauropod standards. If the neck proportions scale as estimated, Erketu may have had one of the longest necks relative to body size of any known dinosaur. Such a neck could have allowed feeding at heights or angles inaccessible to other herbivores, potentially reducing competition.

Euhelopodidae is a family of Asian titanosauriforms that includes Euhelopus from China and possibly Erketu from Mongolia. These sauropods are characterised by long necks and robust bodies, and they represent an important part of the Cretaceous Asian sauropod fauna. The family's relationships within Titanosauriformes remain debated.

The known material of Erketu is limited, which restricts confident reconstruction of the whole animal. Estimates of total body length (~15 m, per NHM) rely partly on comparison with better-known relatives. Future discoveries of more complete Erketu material would significantly improve understanding of this unusual long-necked sauropod.

## Fossil record

Erketu ellisoni was formally described by Ksepka and Norell in 2006 from specimens collected by American Museum of Natural History expeditions to Mongolia. The genus name honours Tengri (Erketu), a supreme deity in Mongolian shamanism, and the species name recognises Mark Ellison of the AMNH. The holotype material consists primarily of articulated cervical vertebrae.

One incomplete postcranial skeleton dominated by partial neck and associated girdle and limb material.

## Where it lived

## Open questions

- The skull and most of the tail are unknown.
- Formation age and the genus position within Somphospondyli remain under study.
