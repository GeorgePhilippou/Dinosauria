## Overview

Euhelopus (meaning 'good marsh foot') was a long-necked sauropod dinosaur from China, living during the Late Jurassic to Early Cretaceous, approximately 154–100 million years ago. Estimated at around 10 metres in length, it is known from a skull and partial postcranial skeletons from Shandong Province, representing one of the few Asian sauropods known from cranial material. Its placement within Euhelopodidae groups it with other distinctively long-necked Asian sauropods, and its anatomy contributes to understanding of early titanosauriform evolution in Asia.

Euhelopus zdanskyi was originally named Helopus by Friedrich von Huene in 1929, before being renamed Euhelopus by Alfred Sherwood Romer in 1956 because Helopus was preoccupied. The specimen was collected from the Mengyin Formation of Shandong by Otto Zdansky, a Swedish palaeontologist working in China in the 1920s. Euhelopus holds a special place in sauropod studies as it was one of the first Asian sauropods to be scientifically described based on reasonably complete material.

## Anatomy & life

Euhelopus is notable for having associated skull material, a rarity among sauropods. The skull has a relatively elongated snout, spatulate teeth, and large narial openings. These features have contributed to phylogenetic analyses placing Euhelopus within Asian titanosauriforms, though its exact position has been debated — some analyses have historically placed euhelopodids in varying positions within Eusauropoda or Titanosauriformes.

Euhelopus is part of a distinct lineage of Asian sauropods that includes long-necked forms such as Mamenchisaurus and Erketu. During the Cretaceous, Asia was largely isolated from other landmasses, promoting the evolution of endemic dinosaur faunas. The euhelopodid lineage appears to have been a persistent element of Asian sauropod diversity from the Jurassic through much of the Cretaceous.

As a moderately large sauropod with an elongated neck, Euhelopus was likely a high-browser or mid-level browser in forested or woodland environments. The relatively gracile build compared to titanosaurian sauropods suggests it was not the heaviest plant-eater in its ecosystem. Its distribution across multiple formations and provinces in China suggests a wide geographic range during its existence.

## Fossil record

The type specimen of Euhelopus was collected from Shandong Province, China, by Otto Zdansky during the Sino-Swedish Expedition in the 1920s and described by Friedrich von Huene in 1929 as Helopus zdanskyi. Romer renamed it Euhelopus in 1956 after discovering that Helopus was already in use for a bird. The name means 'good marsh foot', though the inference of a marsh-dwelling habitat is outdated.

Two associated partial skeletons including substantial skull and neck material.

## Open questions

- The age of the Mengyin Formation is not tightly constrained.
- The association and completeness of the two individuals limit size estimates.
- Position within Somphospondyli varies among analyses.
