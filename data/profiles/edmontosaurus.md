## Overview

Edmontosaurus regalis was a large, broad-beaked saurolophine hadrosaurid from the Horseshoe Canyon and equivalent formations of Canada. Numerous skulls and skeletons across growth stages document its anatomy; later Maastrichtian E. annectens is a separate species and should not be blended into one undifferentiated profile.

Edmontosaurus regalis and E. annectens were among the largest hadrosaurs, reaching 12–13 metres and up to 4 tonnes, from the latest Cretaceous of North America (~73–66 Ma). A remarkable "Edmontosaurus mummy" — with extensive skin and soft-tissue preservation including a fleshy crest on the head — provided unprecedented detail about hadrosaur integument and revealed a cock's-comb-like structure not visible in the skeleton alone.

Edmontosaurus was the most abundant large herbivore at the very end of the Cretaceous, sharing its world with Tyrannosaurus and Triceratops. Bite marks on Edmontosaurus tail vertebrae from a large tyrannosaur — and a healed tail that shows the animal survived the attack — provide direct evidence of sublethal predator-prey interaction between T. rex and its most common prey.

## Anatomy & life

Edmontosaurus regalis was one of the largest hadrosaurids, with adults around nine to twelve metres long. Its skull was long and low, ending in a broad, toothless beak, and behind the beak the jaws carried dental batteries of hundreds of closely packed teeth that formed a continuous grinding surface. Unlike its crested relatives it had no hollow bony crest.

The animal is known from many individuals of different sizes, so its growth is unusually well documented. Skull shape changed considerably as it grew, and some historic species names were founded on juveniles or on individuals of the closely related, later species Edmontosaurus annectens. This profile is scoped to E. regalis.

A specimen from the Wapiti Formation preserves the impression of a fleshy comb on top of the head, a structure that bones alone would never have revealed. Other specimens preserve patches of small, non-overlapping scales. Neither shows colour, and the comb's function is unknown.

- A broad, flattened beak backed by dental batteries with hundreds of teeth arranged in a grinding surface.
- No bony crest, but one specimen preserves a soft, fleshy comb on top of the head.
- A juvenile-to-adult growth series shows how the skull lengthened and deepened with age.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Tooth wear and jaw mechanics show grinding of tough plant material. The particular plants eaten by E. regalis are not directly preserved.

**Movement: Quadrupedal and bipedal.** Hoof-like hands and strong hindlimbs allow both gaits; how often it used each, and its speed, are inferred.

**Appearance: Skin and comb partly known.** Scale impressions and a fleshy head comb are preserved, but colour, pattern and the extent of soft structures over the rest of the body are not.

## Fossil record

Edmontosaurus regalis is known from numerous skulls and substantial skeletons from Alberta, including the two type specimens described by Lawrence Lambe in 1917 and a specimen preserving skin and a fleshy comb. It is among the best-documented hadrosaurids.

Complete and partial skulls with dental batteries, full vertebral series, both girdles, complete limbs with hands and feet, juveniles through large adults, and impressions of scaly skin and a soft cranial comb.

Some historic specimens were assigned to other species or genera and still require reassessment; the broader genus, including E. annectens, adds famous mummified specimens from the United States that are not E. regalis.

Hadrosaur skulls, dental batteries, skeletons and skin impressions document anatomy and growth in detail.

- Multiple specimens preserve skin impressions, and one Edmontosaurus specimen preserves a fleshy head comb.
- Dental batteries and wear surfaces document sophisticated plant processing.
- It could move both quadrupedally and bipedally; habitual speed and herd structure are not directly known.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous North American floodplains and coastal plains.

The profile is scoped to E. regalis, primarily about 73-69 Ma; E. annectens extends the genus into the latest Maastrichtian but is taxonomically distinct.

## Classification

Edmontosaurus is a saurolophine hadrosaurid, one of the flat-headed duck-billed dinosaurs that lacked the hollow crests of lambeosaurines. E. regalis is the type species; E. annectens from the latest Maastrichtian is a separate, later species.

## Open questions

### Which specimens belong to which species?

Several historic names and specimens need reassessment. Juvenile skulls in particular have been confused with separate species.

### Did all individuals have a comb?

The fleshy comb is known from one specimen. Whether it was present in both sexes or at all ages is unknown.

### Did it live in herds?

Bonebeds elsewhere show that Edmontosaurus could occur in large groups, but herd structure and social behaviour are inferred rather than observed.
