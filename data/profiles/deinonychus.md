## Overview

Deinonychus antirrhopus was a medium-sized predatory dromaeosaurid from the Cloverly Formation and equivalent units of North America. Multiple partial skeletons, skull elements, teeth and growth stages document the animal, although many early reconstructions combined several individuals.

Deinonychus antirrhopus sparked the Dinosaur Renaissance. John Ostrom's 1969 description overturned a century of thinking about dinosaurs as slow, cold-blooded, tail-dragging reptiles by demonstrating that this Early Cretaceous predator was active, agile and held its tail rigid for balance. The "Velociraptors" in Jurassic Park are modelled directly on Deinonychus — roughly twice the size of an actual Velociraptor.

Multiple Deinonychus specimens found near a Tenontosaurus carcass in Montana generated the still-debated pack-hunting hypothesis. Whether it hunted cooperatively or whether multiple individuals simply fed opportunistically at a carcass is unresolved; the fossil association alone cannot distinguish between the two interpretations.

## Anatomy & life

Deinonychus was a mid-sized predatory theropod, roughly three to three and a half metres long and perhaps 60–100 kilograms in mass, with a long stiffened tail, grasping three-fingered hands and a large, strongly curved claw on the second toe of each foot. Its name, 'terrible claw', refers to that weapon, which was held clear of the ground during walking.

The skull was relatively deep with a wide gape and blade-like serrated teeth. Early reconstructions gave it a shorter, more boxy head because they combined bones from more than one individual; later specimens show a longer, lower profile more like other dromaeosaurids. Growth-stage differences in the skull and teeth have also been documented.

No feathers are preserved on any Deinonychus fossil, but feathered relatives on either side of it in the family tree make an extensive feather covering the most likely condition. The claw was probably used for gripping and pinning prey, and several individuals have been found with the plant-eater Tenontosaurus, although this does not demonstrate coordinated pack hunting.

- A large retractable second-toe claw is preserved directly and was the inspiration for the genus name.
- Its description by John Ostrom in 1969 was central to the idea of active, bird-like dinosaurs.
- Feathers are expected on phylogenetic grounds but have never been found on the animal itself.

**Feeding: Meat-eater.** Serrated teeth, grasping hands and the sickle claw indicate predation; shed teeth and bite marks link it to Tenontosaurus carcasses, though whether it hunted or scavenged them is debated.

**Movement: Bipedal.** The hindlimbs and stiffened tail indicate a two-legged animal; suggestions of exceptional speed rest on proportions rather than trackways.

**Appearance: Skeleton well understood.** Body outline is well constrained by the bones; feathering is predicted from relatives and colour is unknown.

## Fossil record

Deinonychus is known from a handful of partial skeletons collected in Montana and Wyoming since the 1930s, plus skull elements, isolated bones and abundant shed teeth. Together they cover most of the skeleton, though the famous early reconstructions were composites.

Partial skulls and jaws, vertebrae and the rod-stiffened tail, shoulder and pelvic girdles, forelimbs with complete hands, and hindlimbs with the diagnostic feet, spread across the Yale, Harvard and American Museum collections. Shed teeth are common in the Cloverly Formation and in the Antlers Formation of Oklahoma.

Because the material comes from several individuals of different sizes, any single mounted skeleton combines bones from more than one animal.

Sickle claws, stiffened tail and grasping forelimbs were central to the Dinosaur Renaissance interpretation of active theropods.

- The enlarged second-toe claw is directly preserved and was held clear of the ground in walking.
- Feathering is strongly supported by close phylogenetic bracketing, even though no direct body-feather impressions are known for Deinonychus.
- Co-occurrence of several individuals and Tenontosaurus does not establish coordinated pack hunting.

## Where it lived

Early Cretaceous North American terrestrial ecosystems.

Secure Cloverly occurrences are late Aptian-Albian, broadly 115-108 Ma.

## Classification

Deinonychus is a dromaeosaurid within Eudromaeosauria, the group of larger, robust 'raptors' that also includes Velociraptor, Dromaeosaurus and Utahraptor. Its close relationship to birds within Maniraptora is well supported.

## Open questions

### Did it hunt in packs?

Several Deinonychus with one Tenontosaurus is suggestive, but such associations can form without cooperative hunting, and tooth chemistry suggests juveniles and adults ate differently.

### How was the claw used?

Slashing, stabbing, climbing and pinning prey have all been proposed; mechanical tests favour gripping and restraint, but the question remains open.

### How extensive was its plumage?

Feathers are inferred from relatives such as Velociraptor and Microraptor. Their distribution, length and colour on Deinonychus are unknown.
