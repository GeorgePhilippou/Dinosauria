## Overview

Brachylophosaurus canadensis ("short-crested lizard") was a large saurolophine hadrosaur from the Campanian of Montana and Alberta (~77 Ma), known for exceptional specimen preservation — including several partial mummies with preserved soft tissue. The most famous, "Leonardo" (MOR 1125), preserves skin, muscle, beak and gut contents, making it one of the best-preserved non-avian dinosaur specimens ever found.

Leonardo's gut contents provide direct evidence of the diet of a saurolophine hadrosaur: predominantly conifers, ferns and flowering plants — a diverse mix consistent with opportunistic browsing. The soft tissue preservation also reveals muscle attachment scars, webbing between fingers and the texture of the beak in unprecedented detail.

## Anatomy & life

Found in 2000 by rancher Nate Murphy in the Judith River Formation of Montana, Leonardo (MOR 1125) is a sub-adult Brachylophosaurus preserving 90% of the body surface in mummified form. The specimen preserves skin texture on the face, neck, body, hands and feet; a preserved beak; the stomach region with identifiable plant fragments; and even the eyelid and mouth lining. It entered the Guinness Book of Records as the best-preserved dinosaur ever found.

Plant fragments in Leonardo's stomach region have been identified as conifer needles, flowering plant material, ferns and aquatic vegetation. This broad plant selection is consistent with an opportunistic browser rather than a dietary specialist — a pattern also consistent with the diverse plant material available in Late Cretaceous coastal lowlands.

The mummification reveals details invisible in skeletal material: webbing between the fingers (suggesting some aquatic capability), a keratinous beak wider than the underlying bone, and a body outline indicating fat deposits in the neck and back region. Muscle volume revealed by the mummy is larger than bone scars alone would predict, providing more accurate mass estimates.

## Fossil record

Numerous skulls and skeletons, several nearly complete and preserving skin impressions.

Stomach contents preserve direct dietary evidence; skin impressions and internal organs are known from mummified specimens.

- Several so-called mummies preserve scales and body outlines, not an intact living appearance.
- Plant fragments in one body cavity are potentially gut contents, but contamination and taphonomic mixing limit fine dietary conclusions.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous floodplains of the northern interior of North America.

Diagnostic occurrences are late Campanian, principally about 78-76 million years old.

## Open questions

- Species-level referral of some material requires stratigraphic context.
- Gut-content and molecular preservation claims need cautious interpretation.
