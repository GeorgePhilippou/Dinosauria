## Overview

Cetiosauriscus was a medium-to-large diplodocoid sauropod from the Middle Jurassic of England, approximately 166–162 million years ago (Callovian), found in the Oxford Clay Formation of England. Estimated at about 15 metres in length and between 4 and 10 tonnes in weight, it was a long-bodied, long-tailed herbivore with forelimbs as long as its hindlimbs—an unusual proportion among diplodocoids. Its name means 'like a whale lizard', reflecting a superficial resemblance to the unrelated Cetiosaurus.

Named by Friedrich von Huene in 1927, Cetiosauriscus stewarti was long considered poorly understood, but renewed analysis has confirmed its placement within Diplodocoidea, probably as a flagellicaudate. It is one of the few Middle Jurassic sauropods known from relatively complete postcranial material from England, making it important for understanding sauropod diversification in the European Jurassic island chain.

## Anatomy & life

A distinctive feature of Cetiosauriscus is the equal length of its forelimbs and hindlimbs, which is uncommon among diplodocoids, where the hindlimbs are typically longer. This gives the back a more horizontal or slightly foresloping profile. The tail was moderately long by sauropod standards. The skull is unknown, as with most diplodocoids for which postcranial material is better represented than cranial material.

During the Callovian, much of what is now Europe was an archipelago of low-lying islands surrounded by shallow epicontinental seas. Cetiosauriscus lived on or near these island landmasses and was likely capable of crossing shallow marine barriers. The Oxford Clay sea was rich in fish, marine reptiles, and molluscs, while adjacent land areas supported diverse terrestrial faunas.

## Fossil record

Cetiosauriscus stewarti was named by Friedrich von Huene in 1927 from material collected in England. The type specimen and referred material come from the Oxford Clay Formation, which has been a rich source of Jurassic vertebrate material since the 19th century. The species name honours the collector or a contributing researcher.

One incomplete postcranial skeleton comprising posterior vertebral column, girdles and limbs.

- The name C. stewarti has been conserved for specimen NHMUK PV R3078.
- Swiss material once called Cetiosauriscus greppini belongs to the separate genus Amanzia.

## Where it lived

Marine and nearshore Callovian clay deposits across central England that preserve the main Cetiosauriscus specimen.

The type skeleton is Callovian in the Oxford Clay Formation.

## Classification

Modern phylogenetic analyses place Cetiosauriscus within Diplodocoidea, likely within Flagellicaudata (the group containing diplodocids and dicraeosaurids). This assignment has been revised from earlier placements that associated it more closely with Cetiosaurus, an unrelated eusauropod. The Callovian age of Cetiosauriscus makes it one of the earlier flagellicaudates, relevant for understanding when diplodocoids first diversified.

## Open questions

- Phylogenetic analyses disagree between non-neosauropod eusauropod and diplodocoid affinities.
