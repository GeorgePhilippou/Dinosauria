## Overview

Albertosaurus sarcophagus was a large albertosaurine tyrannosaur from the latest Campanian of Alberta (~70 Ma), slightly younger than Gorgosaurus and preceding the arrival of Tyrannosaurus. A single remarkable bonebed in the Horseshoe Canyon Formation preserves at least 26 Albertosaurus individuals of varying ages — the strongest direct fossil evidence for gregarious behaviour in any tyrannosaur.

The Horseshoe Canyon bonebed, discovered by Barnum Brown in 1910 and re-excavated by Philip Currie from 1997, shows a bimodal size distribution: a few large adults and many sub-adults and juveniles. This pattern is consistent with group hunting or at least seasonal aggregation, though the exact nature of the grouping remains debated.

## Anatomy & life

Albertosaurus was a large two-legged predator of the tyrannosaur family, smaller and more slender than Tyrannosaurus, with a long, low skull, a deep lower jaw and small bony hornlets above the eyes. Adults are estimated at roughly eight to nine metres long, with body mass estimates of around two tonnes depending on the method used.

The skull carried a single row of thick, serrated teeth in each jaw and was built to withstand heavy loading, which supports a bite-driven feeding style. The two-fingered arms were very short. Bone histology shows that individuals grew fastest in their teenage years and reached full size at around twenty, with the oldest known animal about twenty-eight years old.

Its long shins and feet relative to its thigh are anatomically real, and are usually read as signs of a relatively agile animal for its size. What Albertosaurus actually ate, how fast it moved and whether the animals in the Dry Island bonebed lived together are interpretations rather than direct observations.

- A long, low skull with prominent hornlets above the eyes distinguishes albertosaurines from the deeper-skulled tyrannosaurines.
- Proportionally long hindlimbs and a lighter build than Tyrannosaurus are directly visible in the skeletons.
- A growth series from juveniles to old adults is one of the best for any large theropod.

**Feeding: Meat-eater.** Robust serrated teeth and a strongly built skull indicate a predatory or scavenging carnivore. No gut contents are known, so specific prey is inferred from the fauna it lived alongside.

**Movement: Bipedal.** The hindlimb proportions are longer than in Tyrannosaurus of the same body size, but absolute running speed cannot be measured from bones alone.

**Appearance: Skeletal outline supported.** Overall proportions come from articulated skeletons. No skin impressions are confirmed for Albertosaurus itself, so scales, any feathering and colour are inferred from relatives.

## Fossil record

Albertosaurus sarcophagus is known from many skulls and partial to near-complete skeletons in the Horseshoe Canyon Formation of Alberta, including a single bonebed that preserves juveniles, subadults and adults together. The name rests on a partial skull collected in 1884.

Complete and partial skulls with dentition, cervical, dorsal, sacral and caudal vertebrae, ribs and gastralia, pectoral and pelvic girdles, forelimbs, hindlimbs and feet, from individuals ranging from small juveniles to old adults.

Some historical specimens were once referred to other tyrannosaurids, and the type series itself was re-examined in 2010, so referral of older material should be checked against the modern revisions.

The Dry Island bonebed provides the best evidence for social or group behaviour in any large theropod.

## Where it lived

Late Cretaceous coastal plain environments of western Canada.

## Classification

Albertosaurus is a tyrannosaurid and the name-bearing genus of Albertosaurinae, the more slender branch of the family that also contains Gorgosaurus. The two genera are so similar that some workers have united them, but most current analyses keep them separate.

## Open questions

### Did Albertosaurus hunt in groups?

The Dry Island bonebed holds at least a dozen individuals of different ages. Whether they died together as a social group or were gathered by a flood or other event is still debated; the bonebed alone does not prove pack behaviour.

### Is Gorgosaurus the same genus?

Gorgosaurus libratus has at times been placed in Albertosaurus. Detailed cranial comparisons support keeping them apart, but the question turns on how much difference a genus should contain.

### How fast could it run?

Its long legs suggest a relatively quick animal for a large theropod, but speed estimates rely on biomechanical models and vary widely.
