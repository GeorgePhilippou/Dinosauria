## Overview

Supersaurus vivianae is among the longest dinosaurs ever discovered — estimates based on the most complete specimen (BYU 9044, "Jimbo") range from 33–34 metres — placing it alongside Diplodocus but with a larger overall body. A diplodocid from the Morrison Formation of Wyoming (~153 Ma), it was named by James Jensen in 1985 from Colorado material.

The discovery of Supersaurus demonstrated that diplodocids could reach even larger sizes than Diplodocus, complicating the picture of sauropod size evolution. Whether Supersaurus and Ultrasauros (another giant Jensen named from the same quarry) are the same animal has been debated; most material formerly referred to Ultrasauros is now considered Supersaurus.

## Anatomy & life

Scaling from the complete "Jimbo" specimen suggests a length of 33–34 m and a mass of 35–40 tonnes. This places Supersaurus among the longest terrestrial animals ever, though the more massive titanosaurs (Patagotitan, Argentinosaurus) may have been heavier. The elongation is primarily in the tail and neck rather than the torso.

As a diplodocid, Supersaurus would have been a horizontal low-browser like Diplodocus, sweeping through ground-level vegetation with its peg-like front teeth. At 33+ metres, its tail alone would have been a formidable defensive weapon against theropods. Its enormous size would have given it resistance to attack by all but the largest Morrison predators.

## Fossil record

James Jensen discovered the Dry Mesa Quarry in Delta County, Colorado in 1972 and excavated some of the largest dinosaur bones ever found, including the scapulocoracoid of what he named Supersaurus (2.4 m long). Jensen also named Ultrasauros and Dystylosaurus from the same quarry; subsequent analysis showed much of this material belongs to Supersaurus, with Ultrasauros considered a chimera of Supersaurus and Brachiosaurus material.

Holotype scapulocoracoid plus multiple referred partial skeletons from separate localities.

The enormous individual bones — particularly the 2.4 m scapulocoracoid — directly document extreme body size; diplodocid proportions confirm its classification.

## Where it lived

Late Jurassic Morrison floodplain ecosystem.

## Open questions

- Referral of widely separated specimens and maximum length remain debated.
