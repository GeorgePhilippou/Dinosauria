## Overview

Elaphrosaurus (meaning 'fleet lizard') was a lightly built ceratosaurian theropod that lived approximately 155–145 million years ago during the Late Jurassic in what is now Tanzania. Estimated at around 6.2 metres in length, it was a medium-sized but exceptionally gracile dinosaur with a long body, short hindlimbs relative to its trunk, and a shallow chest — a combination unusual for a theropod of its size. Its fossils were recovered from the world-famous Tendaguru Formation.

Described by Werner Janensch in 1920, Elaphrosaurus has been subject to considerable debate about its phylogenetic placement — early researchers tentatively placed it with coelophysoids, but more recent analyses consistently support ceratosaurian affinities. It is now considered closely related to Limusaurus, a bizarre toothless ceratosaur from China, and the two together form the family Noasauridae or Elaphrosaurinae. Close phylogenetic proximity to Limusaurus — which may have been herbivorous or omnivorous — raises intriguing questions about Elaphrosaurus's own diet, since no skull material is known.

## Anatomy & life

The known skeleton of Elaphrosaurus displays a notably long trunk and relatively short hindlimbs compared with other theropods of comparable size, with an unusually shallow ribcage. These features distinguish it from other ceratosaurs and initially led some researchers to propose coelophysoid affinities. The proportions suggest a specialised locomotor or feeding adaptation that remains difficult to interpret without cranial material.

Because no skull or teeth are known for Elaphrosaurus, its diet is entirely unknown. Its close relative Limusaurus is remarkable for losing its teeth ontogenetically and may have been herbivorous or omnivorous as an adult. Whether Elaphrosaurus shared similar dietary shifts is speculative, but the phylogenetic link prompts researchers to question the assumption that it was a straightforward carnivore.

## Fossil record

Elaphrosaurus bambergi was described by Werner Janensch in 1920 from a nearly complete but headless skeleton excavated during the German Tendaguru Expedition (1909–1913) in German East Africa, now Tanzania. The species name honours Paul Bamberg, a German businessman who helped fund the expedition. The absence of skull material has significantly hampered understanding of this animal.

Single partial postcranial skeleton lacking skull and hands.

## Where it lived

Elaphrosaurus inhabited the Tendaguru landscape alongside Brachiosaurus, Giraffatitan, Dicraeosaurus, and stegosaurs, forming part of a diverse Late Jurassic Gondwanan fauna. As a medium-sized theropod it would have occupied a distinct ecological niche, potentially pursuing smaller prey such as juvenile sauropods, lizards, or small ornithischians.

The only confirmed locality; a nearly complete but headless skeleton was recovered during the German Tendaguru expeditions.

## Open questions

- The skull, dentition and hands are unknown.
- Diet and maximum adult size cannot be directly established.
