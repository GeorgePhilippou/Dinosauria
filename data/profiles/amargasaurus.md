## Overview

Amargasaurus cazaui was a distinctive Early Cretaceous diplodocoid sauropod from Argentina (~130 Ma), with two parallel rows of tall neural spines running along its neck and back — the most dramatic spinal ornamentation of any sauropod. The spines could have supported a sail-like skin web, individual keratinous spines like a porcupine, or simply been covered in muscle — no soft-tissue evidence directly confirms which.

At around nine metres Amargasaurus was modest-sized for a sauropod, making it more manoeuvrable than the giant Morrison Formation species. Its discovery in 1991 provided important information about the Early Cretaceous sauropod fauna of South America, a continent increasingly isolated from other landmasses as Pangaea continued to break apart.

## Anatomy & life

Amargasaurus was a small sauropod by the standards of its group, probably around nine to ten metres long. It belonged to the dicraeosaurids, a family of diplodocoid sauropods with unusually short necks that appear to have browsed at low to moderate heights rather than reaching high into the canopy.

Its defining feature is the neck. Each cervical vertebra carried a pair of extremely elongate neural spines that projected upwards and backwards, forming two rows along the neck that continued, at lower height, over the back. Bone microstructure suggests the spines were covered in a hard sheath, but whether they supported a skin sail, a keratin cover or something else is still argued, and their function is unknown.

The skull is only partly preserved, but the braincase shows that the head was habitually held with the snout angled downwards, consistent with low browsing. Its peg-like teeth, inferred from close relatives, would have stripped rather than chewed vegetation.

- Paired neural spines on the neck vertebrae are the tallest known for any sauropod relative to body size.
- A short neck for a sauropod, typical of dicraeosaurids, suggests feeding close to the ground.
- Braincase anatomy indicates the head was carried with the snout angled downwards.

**Feeding: Plant-eater.** Herbivory is not in doubt for a sauropod, and the downward-angled head supports low browsing. Preserved teeth are few, so tooth form leans on dicraeosaurid relatives.

**Movement: Quadrupedal.** Fore- and hindlimb elements are preserved and show the columnar four-legged stance of all sauropods.

**Appearance: Spines supported soft tissue.** Histology shows the spines bore a hard covering, but a sail, keratin sheath or fleshy ridge remain competing reconstructions.

## Fossil record

Amargasaurus rests almost entirely on the holotype skeleton collected in 1984 from the La Amarga Formation. It preserves part of the skull, most of the neck and back, the sacrum, some tail vertebrae and elements of the girdles and limbs.

The holotype includes the back of the skull with the braincase, the cervical, dorsal and sacral vertebrae, the first few caudal vertebrae, ribs, the pelvis and elements of the shoulder girdle, forelimb and hindlimb.

The snout, most of the tail and the hands and feet are missing or poorly known, so those regions in mounts and diagrams are reconstructed from relatives.

Elongate neural spines provide the key evidence for its unusual neck and back profile.

## Where it lived

Early Cretaceous South American terrestrial deposits.

## Classification

Amargasaurus is a dicraeosaurid, a family of short-necked diplodocoid sauropods known otherwise from Africa, North America and other South American finds. Its close relationship with Dicraeosaurus and the newer Bajadasaurus is well supported, though the exact branching order within the family shifts between analyses.

## Open questions

### What covered the neck spines?

Bone histology shows a hard outer covering, but a skin sail, horn-like sheaths and a fleshy crest have all been proposed. No soft tissue is preserved to settle the question.

### What were the spines for?

Display, defence, thermoregulation and species recognition have all been suggested. None can be tested directly from a single skeleton.

### How variable was the species?

Almost everything known rests on one individual, so differences between ages, sexes or individuals cannot be assessed.
