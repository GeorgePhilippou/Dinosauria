## Overview

Muttaburrasaurus langdoni was a large ornithopod from the Early to Late Cretaceous of Queensland, Australia (~100 Ma), reaching about seven metres. It is the most completely known dinosaur from Australia, and its inflated nasal region — forming a distinctive hollow crest or bulge on the snout — may have amplified calls or enhanced the sense of smell.

Australia was an isolated continent during the Cretaceous, separated from Antarctica and South America, and its dinosaur fauna is consequently distinctive. Muttaburrasaurus is an iguanodontian without close relatives in the Northern Hemisphere, illustrating the independent evolution of large ornithopods on the southern Gondwanan landmasses.

## Anatomy & life

The inflated nasal region may have been covered by soft tissue forming a resonating chamber for vocalisation — analogous (though not homologous) to the hollow crests of hadrosaurs like Parasaurolophus. Alternatively it may have housed enlarged nasal passages for enhanced olfaction. No soft tissue is preserved, so the function remains hypothetical.

Cretaceous Australia was separated from other continents and straddled the Antarctic Circle, experiencing months of polar darkness each year. Muttaburrasaurus and other Australian Cretaceous dinosaurs lived in cool, possibly dark winters, suggesting dinosaurs were physiologically capable of tolerating harsh seasonal conditions far better than a cold-blooded reptile could.

## Fossil record

Doug Langdon found the first Muttaburrasaurus skeleton near the Flinders River in Queensland in 1963. Ralph Molnar and Alan Bartholomai described it formally in 1981. A second specimen from White Mountains, Queensland has provided additional skull material, and isolated teeth and bones are known from South Australia and possibly New South Wales.

Two partial skeletons with skulls plus isolated referred remains.

The inflated nasal region and unusual shearing teeth distinguish it from other ornithopods and suggest a specialised diet.

## Where it lived

Early Cretaceous of eastern Australia, when a shallow inland sea covered much of the continent.

## Open questions

- The two principal skulls need renewed comparative study.
- Fine phylogenetic position and nasal function remain unsettled.
