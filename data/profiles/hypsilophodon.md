## Overview

Hypsilophodon foxii was a small, fleet-footed ornithopod from the Early Cretaceous of southern England (~130–120 Ma), about two metres long. For over a century it was depicted as a tree-climber — based on a misinterpretation of its foot anatomy — before modern analysis confirmed it was a fast ground-runner. Dozens of individuals from a single site on the Isle of Wight suggest it lived or gathered in groups.

Hypsilophodon's self-sharpening, high-ridged cheek teeth gave it its name and allowed it to process a wide range of tough plant material. It coexisted with Baryonyx and Iguanodon in the Early Cretaceous Wessex ecosystem and represents the basal ornithopod body plan — bipedal, fast, medium-sized — that persisted from the Jurassic into the Cretaceous.

## Anatomy & life

Thomas Henry Huxley and John Hulke described Hypsilophodon in 1869. A 1974 paper by Galton proposed it could climb trees based on the reversed hallux (first toe) and grasping foot — citing parallels with modern tree-climbing birds. This interpretation was widely accepted until Biomechanical analysis in the 1970s–80s showed the foot anatomy was inconsistent with arboreal grasping. Modern analyses confirm Hypsilophodon was a fully terrestrial runner.

Long hindlimbs relative to the body, a stiff tail and long lower leg segments indicate a fast runner. The self-sharpening ridged cheek teeth — which give the animal its name — processed vegetation by precise occlusion rather than the grinding batteries of hadrosaurs. The small size and agility would have been its primary defence against predators.

A single layer in the Wessex Formation on the Isle of Wight has yielded over 20 Hypsilophodon individuals of all ages. The cause of this accumulation — mass drowning, drought at a waterhole, or predator aggregation — is unknown, but the concentration confirms that Hypsilophodon occurred in social groups.

## Fossil record

Numerous skulls and partial-to-substantial skeletons from multiple individuals.

Light limbs, small skulls and herbivorous dentition support a small agile ornithischian lifestyle.

## Where it lived

Early Cretaceous floodplain environments of the Wealden record.

## Open questions

- Some historic referred material and exact neornithischian position remain under review.
- Specific vegetation and social organisation are unknown.
