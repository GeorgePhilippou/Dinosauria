## Overview

Acrocanthosaurus atokensis was a very large carcharodontosaurian theropod from Aptian-Albian rocks of the south-central United States. Several partial skeletons, including one with much of the skull and postcranium, make its tall neural spines and general anatomy well documented.

Acrocanthosaurus atokensis was the dominant large predator of Early Cretaceous North America (~115–108 Ma), an allosauroid reaching 11–12 metres long with tall neural spines forming a low muscle-supported ridge along its back — distinct from the full sails of Spinosaurus or Ouranosaurus. Trackways from Texas preserve the only direct evidence of a large theropod pursuing a sauropod.

As a carcharodontosaurid — more closely related to Giganotosaurus and Carcharodontosaurus than to Allosaurus despite superficial similarities — Acrocanthosaurus represents the North American branch of a Gondwanan predator lineage that briefly dominated the northern continents before tyrannosaurs replaced them.

## Anatomy & life

Acrocanthosaurus was one of the largest land predators of its time in North America, estimated at around eleven metres long and somewhere between four and six tonnes. Its name, meaning high-spined lizard, refers to the tall neural spines that run along the neck, back, hips and tail and that in life supported a thick ridge of muscle and connective tissue.

The skull is long and narrow with a low bony rim above each eye, and the jaws carry blade-like serrated teeth suited to slicing flesh rather than crushing bone. The forelimbs were short but strongly muscled, with three large clawed fingers; studies of the arm joints suggest they could not reach far forward and were probably used on prey already held by the jaws.

The animal lived on floodplains and coastal lowlands of what is now Oklahoma and Texas. Large three-toed theropod tracks in the Glen Rose Formation of Texas are widely attributed to Acrocanthosaurus because no other predator of its size is known from those rocks, but trackmaker identity cannot be confirmed from footprints alone.

- Tall neural spines, more than twice the height of the vertebral bodies, run from the neck to the tail and are preserved in the type material.
- A long, low skull with blade-like teeth places it among the carcharodontosaurian allosauroids rather than the tyrannosaurs.
- Short, powerful three-fingered arms with a limited range of motion have been studied directly from the North Carolina skeleton.

**Feeding: Meat-eater.** Serrated, laterally compressed teeth and a lightly built skull indicate a slicing rather than bone-crushing bite. No gut contents are known.

**Movement: Bipedal.** The hindlimb is well represented in the holotype and NCSM 14345. Speed and the identity of the Glen Rose trackmaker remain inferences.

**Appearance: High-spined back supported.** The neural spines are real, but whether they carried a low muscular ridge or a taller sail, and what colour the animal was, cannot be read from the bones.

## Fossil record

Acrocanthosaurus atokensis was named in 1950 on two partial skeletons from Oklahoma. A far more complete individual, NCSM 14345, found in 1983 and described in 2000, provides most of the skull and postcranium and anchors the modern picture of the animal.

A nearly complete skull and lower jaws, cervical, dorsal, sacral and caudal vertebrae with their tall neural spines, ribs, the shoulder and pelvic girdles, forelimbs with hands, and hindlimbs and feet, combined across the Oklahoma and Texas skeletons.

The holotype and paratype were partly damaged by weathering before collection, and some teeth and bones from other states referred to the genus cannot be confirmed.

Trackways in Texas preserve direct evidence of pursuit behaviour; the neural spines and skull confirm allosauroid identity.

- No specimen is literally complete, but NCSM 14345 preserves much of the skull and skeleton.
- The elongated neural spines supported a raised ridge of soft tissue; its display or physiological role is unknown.

## Where it lived

Early Cretaceous North American interior with large sauropods, ornithopods and the first flowering plants.

Secure records come chiefly from the Antlers and Twin Mountains formations, around the Aptian-Albian boundary.

## Classification

Acrocanthosaurus is a carcharodontosaurian allosauroid. Most analyses place it within Carcharodontosauridae, close to the base of the family, although a few have recovered it nearer to Allosaurus; the tall spines are a feature of the genus, not of the family as a whole.

## Open questions

### What did the tall spines support?

The neural spines are twice the height of the vertebral bodies but much lower than in Spinosaurus. Most workers reconstruct a thick muscular ridge; a thin sail, fat storage and display have all been proposed and none can be tested directly.

### Did it make the Texas trackways?

Large theropod footprints in the Glen Rose Formation are usually credited to Acrocanthosaurus as the only predator of matching size in the region, but no footprint can be tied to a skeleton with certainty.

### How widely did it range?

Isolated teeth and bones from Maryland and Arizona have been referred to the genus. Secure skeletons come only from Oklahoma and Texas, and the eastern records remain uncertain.
