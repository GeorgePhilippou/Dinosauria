## Overview

Archaeopteryx lithographica is the iconic transitional form between non-avian dinosaurs and modern birds, from the Late Jurassic Solnhofen Limestone of Bavaria (~150 Ma). Its exquisite feather impressions — preserved in the fine-grained limestone — show pennaceous flight feathers identical in structure to those of modern birds on a skeleton that retains numerous non-avian features: teeth, a long bony tail and free clawed fingers.

Twelve specimens are currently recognised, the first found just two years after Darwin published On the Origin of Species in 1859. The timing was extraordinary: Archaeopteryx became the central exhibit in debates about evolution, gradualism and the relationship between reptiles and birds. It remains one of the most studied and argued-over fossils in history.

## Anatomy & life

Archaeopteryx was a small feathered theropod about half a metre long from snout to tail-tip, roughly the size of a raven, with a mass estimated at under a kilogram. It combined bird-like features, including a wishbone, a partly reversed first toe and true flight feathers, with ancestral traits such as toothed jaws, three clawed fingers and a long bony tail.

The animals lived on islands in a shallow tropical sea, and their carcasses sank into fine lime mud that preserved feather impressions in exquisite detail. The wing feathers are asymmetrical, as in flying birds, and the bone walls of the wing are proportioned like those of birds that flap in short bursts, supporting some capacity for powered flight even though the shoulder lacks a modern bird's arrangement.

Its teeth and claws suggest a diet of insects and small vertebrates, but no gut contents are known. Feathers preserved on the body, legs and tail are directly observed; the colour of the animal rests on a single isolated feather whose melanosomes suggest black, and that feather's assignment to Archaeopteryx is itself uncertain.

- Asymmetrical flight feathers and a long feathered tail are preserved directly on several skeletons.
- Toothed jaws, clawed hands and a long bony tail mark it as a theropod dinosaur as well as an early avialan.
- Around a dozen skeletons from one region make it one of the best-documented small Jurassic theropods.

**Feeding: Small-prey carnivore.** Small pointed teeth and grasping hands point to insects and small animals; no stomach contents have been found.

**Movement: Bipedal, flight-capable.** Feather shape and wing-bone geometry support some powered flight, but the shoulder and breastbone differ from modern birds so its flight style is contested.

**Appearance: Plumage preserved.** Wing, tail and leg feathers are preserved as impressions; overall colouring is unknown apart from evidence for black in one feather.

## Fossil record

Archaeopteryx is known from roughly a dozen skeletal specimens and an isolated feather, all from the Late Jurassic lithographic limestones of Bavaria. The London and Berlin specimens alone preserve almost the whole skeleton with wing and tail plumage.

Articulated skeletons with skulls, vertebral columns, long bony tails, wishbones, wings with three clawed fingers, pelves and hindlimbs, plus impressions of wing, tail and leg feathers on several specimens. An isolated feather found in 1861 is of uncertain attribution.

The fossils are preserved as flattened impressions in fine limestone, so three-dimensional skull anatomy and braincase detail rely on a few specimens and CT scanning.

Feather impressions on arms and tail alongside teeth, clawed fingers and bony tail confirm transitional status between dinosaurs and birds.

## Where it lived

Late Jurassic shallow tropical lagoon environment in what is now Bavaria.

## Classification

Archaeopteryx is an early member of Avialae, the branch of maniraptoran theropods that includes birds. Its exact position relative to Anchiornis-like paravians and later birds has shifted between analyses, and the number of valid species within the genus remains debated.

## Open questions

### How many species are there?

A. lithographica and A. siemensii are widely accepted, but some specimens have been placed in separate genera and the boundaries between species are contested.

### Could it really fly?

Feather asymmetry and wing-bone geometry support flapping flight over short distances, yet the flight stroke of a modern bird was probably not possible.

### What colour was it?

Melanosome evidence suggests black in one isolated feather, but that feather may not belong to Archaeopteryx and the rest of the plumage is unknown.
