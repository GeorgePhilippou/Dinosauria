## Overview

Stenonychosaurus (meaning 'narrow claw lizard') was a small, intelligent-seeming omnivorous troodontid theropod from the Late Cretaceous of Canada, living approximately 77–74 million years ago. It reached about 2.5 metres in length and around 35 kilograms in mass, with very long hindlimbs, forward-facing large eyes, and a notably large brain relative to its body size. For decades its fossils were attributed to Troodon, but recent research has established that most material previously called Troodon from the Dinosaur Park Formation actually belongs to Stenonychosaurus.

Named by Gilmore in 1932 from isolated material, Stenonychosaurus inequalis attracted lasting scientific interest when Dale Russell proposed in 1982 that, had dinosaurs not gone extinct, this lineage might have evolved a humanoid body plan — a speculative thought experiment known as the 'dinosauroid'. While this idea is now considered fanciful, it reflects how unusual Stenonychosaurus's large-brained, large-eyed anatomy appeared relative to other dinosaurs. Its teeth are distinctive in possessing numerous small serrations along the mesial (front) edge, unusual for a theropod.

## Anatomy & life

The endocranial volume of Stenonychosaurus, estimated from skull material, is large relative to its body size — an encephalisation quotient sometimes cited as among the highest of any non-avian dinosaur. This does not directly translate to human-like intelligence, but likely reflects enhanced sensory processing and coordination. Its forward-facing eyes suggest binocular vision useful for depth perception when hunting.

Stenonychosaurus possessed teeth with numerous small serrations on the front edge, an unusual configuration among theropods and possibly related to processing small prey or plant material. Combined with large eyes, a large brain, and hooked toe claws, the overall picture is of a versatile predator and possibly opportunistic omnivore, hunting small vertebrates, insects, and perhaps consuming plant matter.

Troodontid nesting behaviour is known from the closely related Troodon formosus material (now considered uncertain at genus level). Troodontid nests from Montana and Alberta preserve eggs arranged in pairs, consistent with two eggs being laid at a time, and evidence suggests at least some parental brooding. If Stenonychosaurus behaved similarly, it would represent advanced parental care for a non-avian dinosaur.

## Fossil record

Stenonychosaurus inequalis was described by Charles W. Gilmore in 1932 from isolated hindlimb bones found in Alberta. For much of the 20th century the genus was overshadowed by the name Troodon, applied to similar troodontid material across North America. A 2017 reassessment by van der Reest and Currie formally established that the abundant Dinosaur Park Formation material belongs to Stenonychosaurus rather than Troodon, restoring the former name to prominence.

Fragmentary holotype hind limb plus more extensive referred cranial and postcranial material from the Dinosaur Park Formation.

- The Stenonychosaurus holotype CMN 8539 comprises hind-limb elements and is itself incomplete.
- A 2017 revision separated Dinosaur Park troodontid skeletons from the tooth-based name Troodon.
- A 2025 analysis found the Two Medicine and Dinosaur Park material closely comparable and preferred the older name Troodon.
- Claims about exceptional intelligence are based mainly on relative braincase size and should not be translated into human-like cognition.

## Where it lived

The majority of Stenonychosaurus material — teeth, partial skeletal elements, and isolated bones — comes from this rich Campanian formation.

The profile is restricted to Campanian Dinosaur Park Formation material; it does not inherit every historical North American occurrence assigned to Troodon.

## Open questions

- The 2025 Troodon neotype proposal has not by itself replaced the existing holotype.
- The limits of Stenonychosaurus relative to Troodon and other Campanian troodontids remain contested.
