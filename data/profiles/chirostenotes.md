## Overview

Chirostenotes was a medium-sized oviraptorosaurian theropod from the Late Cretaceous of North America, approximately 84–66 million years ago (Campanian to Maastrichtian), with fossils known from Alberta, Canada, and from Montana, Texas, and South Dakota, USA. Estimated at 1.7 to 2 metres long, it was an omnivore with distinctive narrow hands bearing long, slender fingers—reflected in its name meaning 'narrow-handed'. It belonged to the family Caenagnathidae, a group of toothless oviraptorosaurs.

Described by Charles Gilmore in 1924, Chirostenotes has a complex taxonomic history because its hands, feet, and skull were initially described as separate genera (Chirostenotes for the hands, Macrophalangia for the feet, and Caenagnathus for the skull). With 28 PBDB occurrences across multiple North American formations, it is one of the better-represented caenagnathids, though its large number of referred specimens reflects historical over-lumping of material.

## Anatomy & life

Chirostenotes had elongate, narrow fingers with large, curved claws—the feature that inspired its name. Like other caenagnathids and oviraptorids, it lacked teeth and had a beak-like mouth, which in caenagnathids was strengthened for crushing hard food items. Its body was lightly built and probably feathered. The skull, when eventually associated, showed a modest cranial crest.

As an omnivore, Chirostenotes likely consumed a varied diet including seeds, eggs, invertebrates, and small vertebrates. The toothless beak and powerful jaw muscles of caenagnathids suggest an ability to crack hard materials. Its ecological role was probably that of a generalist opportunist in the diverse Late Cretaceous ecosystems of North America.

## Fossil record

Chirostenotes pergracilis was named by Charles Gilmore in 1924 from hand material found in the Dinosaur Park Formation of Alberta. Separately described as Macrophalangia (feet) and later linked to Caenagnathus (toothless jaws), the multiple names given to separate body parts of the same or similar animals led to decades of taxonomic confusion. The synonymy of these elements was progressively established through the second half of the 20th century.

Chirostenotes has accumulated numerous referred specimens because isolated caenagnathid elements from across North America were broadly assigned to it. Some of this material likely belongs to distinct taxa, and ongoing revisionary work continues to refine the genus's definition. The 28 PBDB occurrences include material from multiple formations and time intervals, reinforcing the need for careful specimen-by-specimen reassessment.

Type hands and several incomplete referred cranial and postcranial specimens with varying association confidence.

- Modern studies generally distinguish Chirostenotes from the jaw-based genus Caenagnathus.
- A toothless beak and bipedal stance are supported at clade level; exact diet is unresolved.

## Where it lived

Late Campanian formation in southern Alberta, yielding numerous Chirostenotes specimens including type material.

Secure C. pergracilis material is late Campanian in the Dinosaur Park Formation, not Maastrichtian.

## Open questions

- Some referred skeletons may represent other caenagnathids.
- The relationship to Caenagnathus and Macrophalangia has a complex nomenclatural history.
