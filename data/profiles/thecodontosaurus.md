## Overview

Thecodontosaurus was a small, lightly built sauropodomorph, an early member of the lineage that later produced giant sauropods such as Diplodocus. It lived around what is now Bristol at the very end of the Triassic, when the area was a group of limestone islands in a warm, shallow sea.

It was named in 1836 by Henry Riley and Samuel Stutchbury, making it the fourth dinosaur ever named and the first from the Triassic. The name refers to its teeth, which sit in individual sockets in the jaw, a feature that seemed remarkable at the time.

## Anatomy & life

Thecodontosaurus walked on its hind legs and had a moderately long neck, a small head and grasping hands with a large thumb claw. Its teeth are leaf-shaped with coarse serrations, suited to slicing plants, though the front teeth are more pointed and it may also have eaten small animals.

A detailed study of its braincase suggests it had good balance and agility, and that it may have been more active and quick-moving than later, larger sauropodomorphs. Studies of its tooth shape and jaw mechanics support a diet that was mainly or wholly plants, but some mixed feeding cannot be ruled out.

## Fossil record

The first bones were found in 1834 in quarries on Durdham Down in Clifton, Bristol. Most of the original specimens, including the first-named jaw, were kept in the Bristol Museum and destroyed during a bombing raid in 1940. A jaw that had been sent to Yale was later designated as the replacement name-bearing specimen.

Since the 1970s, fissure fills at Tytherington quarry north of Bristol have produced hundreds of further bones, now at the University of Bristol. These include skull pieces and braincases, and they have allowed the whole skeleton to be redescribed in detail.

## Where it lived

At the end of the Triassic the Bristol area was an archipelago of limestone islands. Rain and streams washed bones and sediment into caves and cracks in the rock, where they were preserved. Thecodontosaurus lived alongside small reptiles such as the sphenodontian Clevosaurus and early crocodile relatives. It has been suggested that its small size reflects life on islands, though this is debated.

## Classification

Thecodontosaurus is one of the earliest-diverging sauropodomorphs, outside the group that includes Plateosaurus and the later sauropods. Material from fissures in South Wales, once named Thecodontosaurus caducus, has been moved to its own genus, Pantydraco.

## Open questions

### Plants only, or a mixed diet?

Most evidence points to a plant-dominated diet, but the shape of its front teeth leaves room for occasional meat-eating.

### Was it an island dwarf?

Its small size might reflect limited resources on the islands, or it may simply be the normal size for such an early sauropodomorph.
